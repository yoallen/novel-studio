# Where was I: starting each writing day

**Where was I** is the home page of every novel. It is the first tab on the right edge of the window, and it is the page you land on each time you open a book. Its job is to answer one question before you have finished your coffee: *what do I do next?*

It does that by gathering, in one place, everything you would otherwise have to remember: the next scene to write, how your writing days are going, the last lines you wrote, the notes you left in your pages, the questions still open, the decisions you made lately, the thoughts you saved and have not sorted, the times you stepped away from the page, and what you did this week.

**Why it helps your book.** Most writing days are lost in the first ten minutes, while you try to remember where you were. Where was I hands you your place back, so the first ten minutes go to the page.

Nothing on this page is homework. Every list is there to read and click when it helps, and to ignore when it doesn't.

## Where was I at a glance

From top to bottom, the page shows these parts. Some only appear when they have something to say.

| Part | What it is | When you see it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Next** and **Write** | The one next step, in large type, and the orange button that takes you there | Always |
| The stage row | **Stage**, **Draft** and a **Drafts** link: where the book is in its life | Always |
| **Plant the seed** | Six optional questions to start a blank novel | Only on a novel with no characters yet |
| Drafting progress | One column per day, and your word figures | Always (it is plain until you set a goal) |
| A "Set up this novel" note | An invitation to tell Story Canon what kind of book this is | Until you finish Novel setup |
| "Unsaved typing was kept" | A notice that typing from a closed window is waiting for you | Only after the program closed unexpectedly |
| **Where you stopped** | The last lines of the scene you were in | Once you have opened a scene |
| **Left for later** | Notes you left in your pages, [[like this]] | Always (it says "Nothing waiting" when empty) |
| **Questions still open** | Your newest open questions | Always |
| **Latest decisions** | The last things you decided | Always |
| **Thoughts waiting to be sorted** | Thoughts and ideas you saved but have not filed | Always |
| **Detours still open** | Times you left the page and have not come back yet | Only when there are some |
| **This week** | What you did in the last seven days | Always |

The rest of this chapter takes each part in turn, then shows how to use the page to start a writing day.

## Next and Write: the one step that stands out

**What it is.** At the top of the page, under the small word **Next**, is a single sentence in large type, such as *Keep writing "Fish-guts chain reaction"*. Below it is the orange **Write** button. Beside the button, in small grey type, is a detail line: the scene's status and word count (for example "Drafting, 63 words"), or where a planned scene sits in your plan (for example "Your next planned scene, at Fun and Games").

**Why it helps.** A writing day needs one clear first move, not a menu. Next is that move. It is the one thing on the page meant to stand out.

### How Story Canon chooses Next

Unless you have written your own next step (see below), Story Canon works it out from where you left off, in this order:

1. **You were in a scene.** Next says *Keep writing "the scene's name"*, and the detail line shows its status and words.
2. **You have planned scenes with no page yet**, and either the book has no words at all or you have no scene open. Next says *Write "the card's name"*, the first planned scene in story order, and the detail line names the beat it sits on.
3. **A scene is still at a not-started status** (Idea, Planned or Ready to draft, in the usual steps). Next says *Draft "that scene" in its chapter*.
4. **The book has no chapters yet.** Next says *Write the first line of your first scene*.
5. **Every scene has been started.** Next says *Start the next scene after "your last scene"*.

There is one exception to the first rule. If the scene you were in is still empty and was never planned, and a planned scene is waiting, Next points you at the planned scene instead. An empty, unplanned page is rarely the best place to start.

### What the Write button does

**Write** always lands you on a page you can type on, never on a form:

- If Next names a scene that has a page, **Write** opens it in **The Writing Room**, with the cursor where you left it.
- If Next names a planned scene that has no page yet, **Write** makes its page, puts it in the right place among your chapters, and opens it.
- If there is nothing to open, **Write** makes a new blank scene at the end of your last chapter (and a first chapter, if you have none) and opens it.

Planning is never required first. You can always click **Write** and start.

### Writing your own next step

Sometimes you know better than the program what comes next: "Draft the opening scene in the driveway", or "Fix the timeline in chapter 6 before anything else". You can say so.

1. Click **Settings** on the right edge of the window.
2. Find **Your next writing action**, near the top.
3. Type your next step in your own words.
4. Click **Save settings** at the bottom of the window.

Your words now appear under **Next**. **Write** opens the scene you were last in, or makes a new scene if you were in none. To go back to the program's suggestion, empty the box and save again.

**The common mistake** is writing a whole to-do list in that box. Next works because it is one step. Write the one you will do first, and change it when it is done.

**Example.** A baker's mystery has three half-finished scenes. The author types *Finish the scene where Ruth finds the second key* and saves. Every morning that week, Where was I opens on that sentence, and **Write** opens the scene she was in. On Friday the scene is done, so she empties the box, and Next goes back to suggesting the next scene on its own.

## The stage row: Stage, Draft and Drafts

Just under Next is a row with three things in it, and a grey line of explanation under them.

- **Stage** is a dropdown. It says what you are doing with the book now: Plotting and planning, First draft, Revising, Querying, Resting and so on. Choose a new one whenever the book's life changes.
- **Draft:** followed by a name in bold, such as **First draft**, says which full pass of the manuscript you are on. It is a label, not a button.
- **Drafts** is a link to the Drafts page, where you can keep a snapshot of the whole book.

The two can look like the same thing, and they are not: a book can be at the stage *Revising* while you work on its *Second draft*. The chapter on starting a novel explains both fully, in [Stages and drafts: two different questions](#stages-and-drafts-two-different-questions).

## A new novel: Plant the seed and Set up this novel

Two things appear on Where was I only while a book is new.

**Plant the seed** shows on a novel that has no characters yet. It asks six short questions (who the story is about, what they want, what stands in the way, and so on). Every answer is optional, and **Not now** puts it away. See [Plant the seed: six questions for a blank novel](#plant-the-seed-six-questions-for-a-blank-novel).

**Set up this novel** is a one-line note with a link. It invites you to tell Story Canon what kind of story this is and which methods you use, so it can offer the right tools. It goes away once you have finished Novel setup.

## Drafting progress on Where was I

Below the stage row is your drafting progress: a heading (the month's name, or your writing push's name, such as **November**), a sentence about today, a row of thin columns, one for each day, and a few figures underneath.

**What it answers.** *How are my writing days going, and am I on track?*

Until you set a goal, it simply shows how many words you wrote each day this month. Once you set a daily goal, or a writing push such as November and 50,000 words, it measures each day against that goal and tells you what each remaining day needs.

The short version of how to read the columns:

| What you see | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| A solid black column | You met that day's goal (or wrote anything at all, when there is no goal) |
| A grey striped column | You wrote that day, but less than its goal |
| A flat line and no column | No words that day |
| A dotted line | A day still ahead |
| A thick pink line under a day, with a pink number | Today |

Point at any column to see that day's exact count. Everything about goals, pushes, catching up, lighter days and rest days is in its own chapter: [Goals, November and catching up](#goals-november-and-catching-up).

**Looks wrong, but isn't.** Words you type today can take up to about twenty seconds to appear in **Today**, because a day's words are counted from saved versions of your scenes, and a version is saved after a short pause or when you leave the scene. See [When a stretch of typing becomes a saved version](#when-a-stretch-of-typing-becomes-a-saved-version).

## Unsaved typing was kept: recovering after a crash

**What it is.** If Story Canon closed while you were typing (a power cut, a crash, a laptop lid shut at the wrong moment), your typing is not lost. The next time you open the book, Where was I shows a notice: *Unsaved typing was kept for "the scene's name". Open the scene to choose which version to keep.* Under it is a button, **Open "the scene's name"**, for each scene that has kept typing.

**What to do.**

1. Click **Open "the scene's name"**. The scene opens in the Writing Room with a box over it titled *Typing from (the date and time) was kept*.
2. Read the **Kept typing** shown in the box. It is the end of what you typed before the program closed.
3. Click **Use the kept typing** to make it the scene's text, or **Keep the saved version** to keep what was saved before.

Either way, nothing is thrown away: the version you did not choose stays in the scene's **Earlier versions**. Until you choose, the page will not take typing, so nothing can overwrite the kept words by accident. The full detail is in [Kept typing after the program closed](#kept-typing-after-the-program-closed).

**The common mistake** is ignoring the notice and opening the scene some other way, then wondering why the page won't let you type. The page is waiting for your choice. Make it, and the page opens.

## Where you stopped: the last lines you wrote

**What it is.** Under the heading **Where you stopped** is a small grey line naming the chapter and scene you were last in (for example "Chapter 1, Fish-guts chain reaction"), and below it the last few lines of that scene, set off by a line down the left side. If the text is long, it begins with "…" because only the end is shown. If the scene has no words yet, it says *This scene is still blank.*

**Why it helps.** Rereading your last paragraph is the oldest trick for getting back into a scene. Ernest Hemingway made a habit of stopping each day while he still knew what would happen next, so the next morning he could pick up the thread at once. Where you stopped shows you the thread before you even open the page.

**How to use it.** Read the lines. If they bring the scene back, click **Write** at the top of the page (or **The Writing Room** tab), and you land at your cursor, in that scene.

## Left for later: notes waiting in your pages

**What it is.** A **note for later** is a reminder you type inside your manuscript between double square brackets, like `[[what day of the week is the block party?]]`, so you can keep writing instead of stopping to look something up. The **Left for later** section gathers every such note in the book, in the order of the book. The heading shows how many there are, for example **Left for later (1)**.

Each note shows:

- **The note itself**, as a link in slanted type.
- Under it, in small grey type, **where it is**: the chapter number, the scene's name, and the few words just before the note, in quotation marks, so you remember what you were writing when you left it.

The list shows the first eight notes. If there are more, a link **Show all** (with the number) opens the rest, and **Show fewer** folds them again.

**How to use it.**

1. Click a note. Its scene opens in the Writing Room with the note selected on the page.
2. Type the answer. Typing replaces the selected note, brackets and all.
3. The note leaves the list as soon as its brackets leave the page.

When the list is empty, it says so, and reminds you how to leave a note: type `[[` and `]]` around it, or press **Ctrl+Shift+L** while you write. Notes are never counted as story words. The full teaching is in [Notes for later: keep writing, fix it later](#notes-for-later-keep-writing-fix-it-later).

**Example.** A lighthouse keeper's story is moving fast, and the author can't remember how many steps the tower has. She types `[[how many steps?]]` and keeps going. A week later, on a slow morning, Left for later shows that note with "Chapter 4, The storm" and the words just before it. She clicks it, types *one hundred and twelve*, and the note is gone from the list.

## The marks beside questions and decisions

Each item in **Questions still open** and **Latest decisions** has a small mark at its left edge. These are the same marks Story Canon uses everywhere for how settled something is:

| Mark | Name | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A short line, like a blank to fill in | Still open (a blank) | Not decided yet: a question waiting for an answer |
| A small solid black square | Decided (inked) | You have decided this. It is in ink |

You will meet the other marks (pencil for developing, a slip for a suggestion, a flower mark for an example) on record pages in the Story Brain and in Narrative Zoom.

## Questions still open

**What it is.** The eight newest questions in your book that are still open. A question is a record in the Story Brain's **Questions I have**: something you need to find out or decide, such as *When did Anthony's mother die: before the novel, or during it?* Long questions are cut short with "…".

**Where they come from.** Every question you save with **Capture** (choosing **A question I have**), every question you add in the Story Brain, and questions that arrived when you brought in your planning.

**How to use it.**

1. Point at a question to see more of it, if it was cut short.
2. Click it. The Story Brain opens on that question's own page.
3. Answer it there, or mark it answered, and it leaves this list.

**Why it helps.** An open question is a decision you are carrying around in your head. Seeing the newest few each morning lets you notice which ones are now blocking the scene you are about to write, and settle those first.

**The common mistake** is treating the list as a chore to clear before writing. Most open questions can stay open for weeks. Answer the one that stands between you and today's scene, and leave the rest.

## Latest decisions

**What it is.** The five things you most recently decided (put in ink), newest first. Each entry has three lines:

1. **The record's name**, as a link, for example **Anthony Vernon** or **Ledger contents**.
2. **The field**, in small grey type, for example "Other facts about them" or "Where it pays off".
3. **What you decided**, in ordinary type.

So an entry that reads **Ledger contents**, then "Where it pays off", then "Convincing evidence at the reveal" means: on the record *Ledger contents*, in the field *Where it pays off*, you decided *Convincing evidence at the reveal*.

**How to use it.** Click the record's name to open its page in the Story Brain, where you can see the decision in place, change it, or see where it came from.

**Why it helps.** Decisions are easy to forget between sessions, and a forgotten decision gets made twice, differently. A glance at this list before you write keeps today's pages in line with what you settled yesterday.

**Example.** Rival florists are feuding over a wedding. Yesterday the author decided, on the record *The wedding*, field *Date*, that it is the last Saturday in June. This morning Latest decisions shows it at the top, so the scene she writes today doesn't put the wedding in May.

## Thoughts waiting to be sorted

**What it is.** The eight newest thoughts and ideas you saved that have not found a home yet. A **thought** is anything you jot down with **Capture** as *Just a thought*: unsorted, waiting for you to decide what it is. An **idea** is something you might use, saved as *An idea I am thinking about*, that you have not adopted or set aside yet. Both wait here until you sort them.

When the list is empty, it says *Nothing waiting. Every thought you saved has a home.* Under the list, **All questions and ideas** opens the Story Brain, where every question, idea and thought lives.

**How to use it.** Click a thought to open it in the Story Brain. There you can turn it into a question or an idea, file it, or connect it to a character or scene.

**Why it helps.** Capture works because it asks nothing of you in the moment. The price is a pile of unsorted notes. This list keeps the pile small and visible, so a good idea from Tuesday doesn't vanish under Wednesday's.

**The common mistake** is sorting thoughts instead of writing. Sort two or three on a slow day, not every day before you start.

## Detours still open

**What it is.** A **detour** is a time you left the page in the middle of writing: you were in a scene in the Writing Room and clicked over to the Story Brain to check a name, to Narrative Zoom to look at a beat, or back to Where was I. Story Canon notes each one, with where you were and where you went, so stepping away never costs you your place. **Detours still open** lists up to five detours you have not come back from yet, each named for where you went, such as *Went to Story Brain*.

Under the list is a link, **Writing Trail**, to the full record of every detour. The section only appears when there is at least one open detour.

**How a detour closes.** A detour closes when you return to writing with the pink **Return to Writing** ribbon at the top left of the window, or with **Ctrl+Shift+W**. Both put you back in the scene you left, with your cursor where it was, and close every open detour at once.

**Looks wrong, but isn't (mostly).** Two things surprise people here:

- **Clicking The Writing Room tab, or a scene, does not close a detour.** Only Return to Writing does. If you went back to the page another way, the detour stays listed until your next Return to Writing. Nothing is wrong with your work; the list is only a record.
- **Marking a detour resolved in the Writing Trail does not take it off this list**, even though the grey line under the heading says it will. Resolved and returned are two separate things in the Writing Trail (see [The Writing Trail page](#the-writing-trail-page)). Use Return to Writing to clear this list.

**Why it helps.** "Where was I?" is hardest to answer after a detour that turned into an hour. The list shows you that you left, and the ribbon takes you straight back.

## This week: what you did in the last seven days

**What it is.** A short list of what you did in the **last seven days** (counted back from now, not from Monday):

| Line | What it counts |
| --- | --- |
| **new manuscript words** | Words you added to your scenes while writing, minus words you cut |
| **scenes worked on** | Scenes you typed in |
| **decisions made** | Things you put in ink yourself |
| **thoughts, questions or ideas saved, sorted or not** | Everything you captured or added as a thought, question or idea, whether you have sorted it yet or not |
| **connections made between records** | Links between two records, such as a character connected to a scene they appear in. Point at the line for a reminder |
| **questions answered** | Questions you marked answered (this line only appears when there is at least one) |

**Why it helps.** Writing a book is more than typing. A week with few new words may still hold ten decisions and twenty connections, and those are the work that makes next week's words come easily. This list counts the thinking as well as the typing, in plain numbers, with no judgment.

**Looks wrong, but isn't.** Right after you bring in a planned novel, This week may say something like "51 thoughts, questions or ideas saved" while **Thoughts waiting to be sorted** says *Nothing waiting*. Both are true. The first counts everything created this week, including what arrived with your planning, sorted or not. The second shows only thoughts that still need sorting.

## Using Where was I to start each writing day

Here is a routine that takes about two minutes. Do it in this order.

1. **Open the book.** Where was I is the first page you see.
2. **Read Next.** If it is the right step, keep it in mind. If it isn't, you can still choose any scene in the Writing Room.
3. **Read Where you stopped.** Let the last lines bring the scene back.
4. **Glance at your progress.** Read **Today** and, during a push, **To finish on time**. That is today's number. Don't study the columns.
5. **Scan Latest decisions and Questions still open.** Look only for anything that touches today's scene. If one question blocks you, answer it now; leave the rest.
6. **Check Detours still open.** If yesterday ended in a detour, the ribbon will take you back to exactly where you were.
7. **Click Write.**

On a slow day, or at the end of a week, add two more steps: clear a few notes from **Left for later**, and sort two or three **Thoughts waiting to be sorted**.

**Why this order.** Next and Where you stopped get you into the scene. Progress gives you a number to aim for. Decisions and questions protect the scene from contradicting itself. Everything else can wait for a day when the words won't come.

**The common mistake** is reading the whole page every morning, opening every question and sorting every thought, and running out of time to write. Where was I is a doorway, not a room. Walk through it.

**Example.** Teresa is writing about two rival florists. She opens the book on a Tuesday. Next says *Keep writing "The arch"*. Where you stopped shows Delia climbing the ladder with the last of the peonies. Today says *0 words of your 1,667*. Latest decisions reminds her that, yesterday, she put in ink that Marcus is allergic to lilies, and that matters in this scene. She ignores the eight open questions, none of which touch the arch, and clicks **Write**. Forty minutes later she has 1,700 words and a lily in Marcus's buttonhole.

## When Where was I looks wrong but is working

| What you see | Why |
| --- | --- |
| **Today** still says 0 words just after you typed | A day's words are counted from saved versions. A version is saved after a short pause in typing, or when you leave the scene. Wait a moment, or come back from the Writing Room, and the count catches up |
| Next ignores your planned scenes | You have a scene open with words in it, so Next keeps you there. Planned scenes come next once nothing is under way, and they are always listed in the Writing Room under **Planned, not written yet** |
| A question you never typed is in Questions still open | It arrived with planning you brought in, or from a conversation. Click it to see its page and where it came from |
| Detours still open keeps listing a detour you came back from | You came back with the tab or a scene link, not with **Return to Writing**. Click the ribbon, or press Ctrl+Shift+W, once |
| The page can't be typed on after you open a scene from the "Unsaved typing was kept" notice | The scene is waiting for you to choose **Use the kept typing** or **Keep the saved version** |
| The columns don't measure anything | You have not set a goal. See [Everyday goals: a number for ordinary days](#everyday-goals-a-number-for-ordinary-days) |
