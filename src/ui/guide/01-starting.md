# Starting a novel

This chapter covers everything from the moment you open Story Canon to the moment your new novel is ready to write: the Library where your novels live, the three ways to start one, telling the program about you, telling it what kind of book this is, and the six questions that plant a blank novel.

## The Library: your shelf of novels

The **Library** is the first page you see when Story Canon has no novel open. It is a bookshelf holding every novel you are working on, with a place to start a new one.

**How to get there:** click **Library** at the left of the header, the line across the top of the page. You can go there from any screen.

From top to bottom, the Library holds:

1. The program's title page: *Story Canon*, its tagline, and the motto *Story Canon remembers. The author decides.*
2. The first time only, a card that asks about you. See [About you: telling Story Canon who you are](#about-you-telling-story-canon-who-you-are).
3. A card for any novel that is ready to bring in, if there is one.
4. **Your novels**: the shelf.
5. **On your other computers**, if you share novels between two computers and one is waiting.
6. **Start a novel**.
7. **Bring in an archive**.
8. **Removed novels**, if you have taken any off the shelf.

### A book on the shelf: what the cover shows

Each novel stands on the shelf as a small cloth-covered book. Everything on the cover:

| On the cover | What it is |
| --- | --- |
| The title, on a white label | Click it to open the novel. It is a button even though it looks like a printed label. The novel opens on **Where was I**. |
| The stage menu (such as *Plotting and planning*) | Where the book is in its life. Choose a different stage from it at any time. A new novel says *Choose a stage* until you pick one. See [Stages and drafts: two different questions](#stages-and-drafts-two-different-questions). |
| A draft name in gray (such as *First draft*) | Which full pass of the manuscript you are on. It shows only when it differs from the stage, so a book whose stage is *Second draft* doesn't say it twice. |
| A word count (such as *9 words*, or *9 words of 90,000*) | Words in your manuscript. The second number appears once you set a target length in **Settings**. |
| A genre (such as *Comedy*) | Your primary genre, if you have chosen one in **Novel setup**. |
| **Next:** and a few words | The next thing to do, such as *Keep writing "Fish-guts chain reaction"*. It is the same suggestion **Where was I** shows. |
| **Remove from shelf** (small, underlined, at the bottom) | Takes the book off the shelf without deleting anything. See below. |

**The order of the books.** The novel you worked on most recently stands first, at the left. So the order changes as you work. Read the title before you click anything on a cover.

### Removing a novel from the shelf

**What it is:** a way to tidy your shelf. Nothing in the novel is deleted. It simply moves to a list at the bottom of the Library.

**When to use it:** for a test novel, a book you have set down for good, or anything you don't want to see every day.

1. In the Library, find the novel's cover. Check the title on the label; the books move around.
2. Click **Remove from shelf** at the bottom of that cover.
3. A small box from Windows asks: *Take "(title)" off your shelf? Nothing in it is deleted.* Click **OK**. (**Cancel** leaves it where it is.)
4. The book leaves the shelf and appears under **Removed novels** near the bottom of the page.

**The common mistake** is clicking **Remove from shelf** on the wrong cover because the order changed since you last looked. If it happens, put the book back (below). No harm done.

### Removed novels: putting a book back

The **Removed novels** list appears at the bottom of the Library only when you have removed at least one book. Each line shows the title, the date it was removed, and **Put back on the shelf**. Click that link and the book returns to the shelf exactly as it was, with every word, record and conversation.

### A novel that is ready to bring in

Sometimes the Library shows a card above your shelf that says something like **I Declare War is ready**. This is a novel that was prepared for you from earlier planning: a story bible, notes, chats and spreadsheets, already turned into records and facts. The card says what it was built from and how much it holds. For example: *212 records, 495 facts, 541 conversation turns and 919 source passages*.

- A **record** is one page in the Story Brain, such as a character. A **fact** is one entry on a record. (See [Records and facts: the idea every screen rests on](#records-and-facts-the-idea-every-screen-rests-on).)
- A **conversation turn** is one message in a chat that was brought in.
- A **source passage** is a stretch of an original planning document, kept word for word so you can always check where a fact came from.

The card's small print makes a promise: every fact keeps the status it had in the planning (decided, developing, still open, an example, or a suggestion never accepted), and nothing was decided for you.

**To bring it in:** click **Bring in (title)**. The button says *Bringing it in…* for a few seconds, then the novel opens on **Where was I**, and it joins your shelf.

**Not now** hides the card for good on this computer. Use it only if you are sure you don't want that novel here.

**Looks broken but isn't:** on a second computer that already shares this novel with your first, the card does not appear. That is on purpose: the novel comes from your other computer instead (see the next section), so the two computers hold one book, not two copies.

### On your other computers

If you write on two computers that share novels through a common folder (set up in **Settings** under **Your other computers**), the Library shows a section called **On your other computers** whenever the other computer has shared a novel this one doesn't have yet.

Each line shows the title, which computer shared it and when, and **Bring to this computer**. Click it, and the novel opens here. From then on the two computers keep it as one book.

**The common mistake:** starting the same novel fresh on the second computer, or bringing it in a second way, instead of using **Bring to this computer**. That gives you two separate books that never stay in step.

### Bring in an archive

An **archive** is a single file that holds one whole novel with all its history. You make one in **Settings** with **Save an archive of this novel**, for safekeeping or to move a book to another computer.

To open one, use the file chooser under **Bring in an archive**. Before anything is added, a box shows what the archive contains. Then:

- **Import** brings it in, when the novel isn't already in your library.
- **Import as a separate copy** appears when a version of this novel is already here. Your current novel is not changed; the archive comes in beside it.
- **Cancel** closes the box with nothing added.

Nothing already in your library is ever overwritten.

## Three ways to start a new novel

The **Start a novel** section of the Library asks for a working title, offers a box for one sentence, and gives three ways to begin. There is no questionnaire. You can start with a title alone.

### The working title and the one sentence

- **Working title.** Required, because the book needs a name on its cover. It can change any time in **Settings**. If you click a start button with the title empty, the Library says *Give the novel a working title* and waits.
- **One sentence about it, if you have one (optional).** Your whole story in one line. Writers call this a **logline**. It becomes the book's *one sentence*, which lives in **Novel setup** under **The heart of the book**, marked Developing (in pencil). Leave it empty if you don't have one yet.

**Example.** Title: *The Keeper of Gull Point*. Sentence: "A lighthouse keeper who has never left her island must sail to the mainland to save the light she hates."

### Start writing

**What it does:** makes your novel with a first chapter (*Chapter 1*) and a first blank scene (*Scene 1*), and opens that scene in **The Writing Room**, ready for your first line. The page says *Write anything. A sentence counts.*

**When to use it:** when you want to find the story by writing it, or when an opening line is already in your head. Planning can come later, or never.

### Set it up first

**What it does:** makes the novel with the same first chapter and blank scene, then opens **Novel setup**, where you tell the program what kind of book this is. See [Novel setup: telling Story Canon what kind of book this is](#novel-setup-telling-story-canon-what-kind-of-book-this-is).

**When to use it:** when you plan before you write, or when you already know your genre and the methods you like (such as *Save the Cat*). Your choices decide which tools and blanks the program offers.

### Start from a manuscript or story bible

**What it does:** makes the novel with no chapters, and opens **Import and export** on the **Bring something in** tab, where you choose a file to bring in.

- A **manuscript** is your book's text, from Word or another writing program. Story Canon proposes chapters and scenes for you to check.
- A **story bible** is a document of notes about your story: characters, places, decisions. Story Canon shows each passage beside where it would go, and you tick what you want.

**When to use it:** when you already have pages or notes somewhere else. Nothing comes into your novel until you have looked it over, and your words come in exactly as written.

### Which way should you start?

| If you... | Choose |
| --- | --- |
| Want to write today and plan later, or never | **Start writing** |
| Plan first, or know your genre and methods | **Set it up first** |
| Already have chapters or planning notes in a file | **Start from a manuscript or story bible** |

None of these locks you in. A novel started with **Start writing** can be set up tomorrow. A novel set up first can be written in today.

**The common mistake** is treating the choice as important. It only decides which screen you see first.

## About you: telling Story Canon who you are

**What it is:** a short set of questions about you as a writer. The answers shape how the collaborator (the optional writing partner built into Story Canon) speaks with you, and the name the program uses for you.

**Where it is:** the first time you open Story Canon, a card at the top of the Library says **Before we begin, tell Story Canon about you**. After that, the same questions are in **Settings**, in the section called **About you**.

**What it asks.** Every answer is optional.

| Question | What to put there |
| --- | --- |
| **What should I call you?** | Your first name, a nickname, or a pen name. Story Canon uses it on screen, for example in the marker *Dana, it's on you now* for work you keep for yourself. |
| **Your pronouns** | Type your own, or click one of the suggestions under the box: *she/her*, *he/him*, *they/them*, *she/they*, *he/they*, *ze/zir*, *any pronouns*, *just my name*. Nothing is ever guessed from your name. |
| **How do you find your story?** | Click *I plot first*, *I find the story as I write* or *Somewhere in between*, or use your own words. |
| **How long have you been writing?** | Years, books finished, or "this is my first." |
| **How do you like to be helped and taught?** | For example: "Teach the idea fully before you show me an example, and don't talk down to me." |
| **Anything that drains you, or that the collaborator should stay away from?** | Topics, habits or tones you don't want. |
| **Anything else about you as a writer** | Whatever else you'd like it to know. |

**How to answer, step by step:**

1. Type into any boxes you like. Click a suggestion under a box to fill it in; you can still edit the words after.
2. On the first-time card, click **Save**. Or click **Not now** to skip; the card won't ask again.
3. In **Settings**, the **About you** answers are kept with the rest of the page when you click **Save settings** at the bottom.

**Why it matters.** The collaborator reads these answers before every conversation. It calls you by your name, uses your pronouns, and adjusts how it explains things. The card says where your answers go: they stay on this computer, go to the collaborator only when you talk with it, and change nothing about your story.

**Your name, in two places.** **Settings** also has a **Your name** line under **This novel**. When About you has a name, that line simply says which name Story Canon is using for you. **Author name on this book** is separate again: it is the name printed on the title page when you export, so you can put a pen name there.

**The common mistake** is skipping this because it feels like a form. It takes a minute, and it is the difference between a partner who talks over your head and one who talks to you.

**Example.** A first-time novelist writes "This is my first book. Define craft words when you use them." Later, the collaborator mentions a *beat*, and adds in plain words that a beat is one turning point in the story.

## Novel setup: telling Story Canon what kind of book this is

**What it is:** a page of optional choices about this novel: its genres, the kind of story it is, how it is told, and the methods you use to build it. Your choices decide which blanks, views and questions the program offers this book.

**Why it exists.** A murder mystery needs clue tracking. A romance needs a heat level. A fantasy needs magic rules. Showing every possible blank to every book would make every page feel like a tax form. Novel setup lets the program show your book what your book needs, and nothing else.

**Where it is:**

- **Set it up first** in the Library opens it straight away.
- On **Where was I**, until you have made any choice, a note says *Whenever you like: tell the program what kind of story this is...* with a **Set up this novel** link.
- Any time: **Settings**, then **Open novel setup** in the first line of the page.

The page title says *How (your title) is set up*. The **Settings** link at its top left takes you back.

**When to use it:** whenever you like. Before the first word, after a few chapters, or halfway through revision when you realize the book is really a romance.

**The common mistake** is treating setup as homework to finish before writing. Every part is optional, and it changes whenever the story does.

### Saving in Novel setup

There is no Save button. Every tick, menu choice and typed box is kept the moment you make it (for typed boxes, when you click away from the box). A bar along the bottom of the window says *Every choice saves as you make it*, and after a change it adds the time, such as *Saved at 3:19:42 PM*.

### The heart of the book

The first section holds the two lines that say what your book is.

- **The one sentence.** Your whole book in about twenty-five words. The gray words inside the empty box suggest how: *The whole book, squeezed until only its central irony is left.*
- **The one paragraph.** Five sentences: the setup, three disasters, and the ending. A **disaster** here is a turning point that makes things much worse and forces a new choice.

Beside each label is its status, such as **DEVELOPING**. These two lines are kept in the **Story world** record in the Story Brain (one record per novel that holds the book's big picture). Changing them here or there changes the same thing. **Open the story world** takes you to that record.

**Example.** One sentence: "A lighthouse keeper who has never left her island must sail to the mainland to save the light she hates." That one line already tells you the ending has to answer: does she come to love it, or let it go?

### Genres and their roles

**Genres** are the familiar kinds of book a bookseller shelves by: Mystery, Romance, Fantasy, Cozy Mystery, Romantic Comedy and about thirty more. Under each name is one line saying what it is.

1. Tick a genre. A small menu appears beside it, set to **Secondary**.
2. Change the menu to set its **role**:
   - **Primary**: what the book mainly is. The cover in the Library shows primary genres.
   - **Secondary**: also strongly part of the book.
   - **Influence**: a genre you borrow from without taking on its rules. A literary novel with a thriller's pace might list Thriller as an influence.
3. To remove a genre, untick it.
4. For a genre that isn't listed, type it in the box under the list (*A genre or subgenre of your own*) and click **Add**. It is marked *yours*.

**What genres switch on:**

- **What these genres ask of your story.** Under the list appear the questions each primary or secondary genre asks, such as *Comedy: Does each comic set piece escalate from the one before?* Influences don't add questions. These are questions to think about, not tests.
- **Worldbuilding.** Choosing Fantasy, Science Fiction, Urban Fantasy, Paranormal, Paranormal Romance, Magical Realism, Dystopian, Gothic or Horror adds worldbuilding kinds to the Story Brain, such as Kinds of beings, Magic and powers, and Languages. See [Every kind and every field](#every-kind-and-every-field).

**A nudge you may see.** If your tone is *Comic*, *Romantic* or *Suspenseful* but the matching genre isn't ticked, a note asks whether the book is a comedy (or romance, or suspense), with a link such as **Mark Comedy as the primary genre**. Ignore it if the tone is only a flavor.

### Story types and their three ingredients

Under the heading **Story types** (*Blake Snyder, Save the Cat*) is a second list, separate from genre. Blake Snyder, a screenwriting teacher, sorted stories by their core situation rather than their shelf. There are ten: *Monster in the House*, *Golden Fleece*, *Out of the Bottle*, *Dude with a Problem*, *Rites of Passage*, *Buddy Love*, *Whydunit*, *The Fool Triumphant*, *Institutionalized* and *Superhero*.

Under each name is a line saying what it is, and **Needs:** followed by its three ingredients. For example, *Buddy Love* needs an incomplete hero, a counterpart, and a complication.

1. Tick a story type. A role menu appears beside it, just like genres.
2. Below the list, a heading appears: *(Story type): the three ingredients in your story*, with one box for each ingredient and a question to answer, such as *An incomplete hero. What is missing in them?*
3. Type your answer. Beside each box, a menu sets its status: **Decided**, **Developing** or **Example**. It starts at Developing.
4. Click away from the box. The answer is kept in the **Story world** record.

**Why it helps:** the three ingredients are a quick test of whether the story has what its type needs. A Buddy Love story without a real complication has nothing to keep the pair apart.

**Example.** A lighthouse story as *Rites of Passage*: the life problem is grief; the wrong way to attack it is refusing to leave the island; the hard truth is that the light can be kept by someone else.

### How the story is told

This section covers everything about the book apart from its genre. It is a set of groups, each with a short description and a list of choices. Each choice has one line under it saying what it means. Some choices also say **Turns on:** and name the tool or blanks they add.

| Group | What it covers | Examples of what turns on |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Viewpoint** | Whose eyes, and how close: first person, third person limited, omniscient, second person, alternating | Alternating viewpoints turns on viewpoint coverage and the one-head-per-scene question |
| **Tense** (one choice) | Past, present, or mixed | Mixed tense lets you set tense scene by scene |
| **Construction** | One lead, two, or an ensemble | Dual protagonists show two arcs side by side |
| **Information** | What the reader knows, and when: dramatic irony, a hidden truth, an unreliable narrator, misdirection | A hidden truth turns on the secret operations log |
| **Time** | The order of the telling: linear, nonlinear, dual timeline, frame story, flashbacks, the present calling up the past | A dual timeline adds a timeline strand to each scene |
| **Setting** | When and where: now, recent past, historical, near or far future, an invented world, a hidden world inside ours | An invented world, a hidden world or the far future turns on worldbuilding |
| **Readership** (one choice) | Adult, New Adult, Young Adult, Middle Grade | |
| **Tone** | The feeling: comic, cozy, tender, eerie, gritty and more | |
| **Heat level** (one choice) | How much intimacy is on the page, from closed door to explicit | Sensual and above add a heat blank to each scene card |
| **Language** (one choice) | How strong the language is, from clean to strong | |
| **Content to flag** | Subjects a reader may want to know about first | |
| **Romance tropes** | Familiar love-story shapes, such as enemies to lovers | |
| **Technique** | Devices such as running gags, foreshadowing, titled chapters | Running gags add callbacks to each scene card |
| **Form** | The shape of the book: letters, series, novella, linked stories, a retelling | A retelling adds source records |

**Groups that take one answer** say *One choice.* in their description and use round buttons. Choosing a new answer replaces the old one. To clear the answer, click the chosen one again.

**Add your own** under any group lets you type a choice of your own, such as a tone the list doesn't have. Click **Add** (or **Cancel**). It joins that group, marked *yours*, and is ticked for you.

### My storytelling toolkit

**What it is:** the frameworks and methods you use to build this novel. A **framework** or **method** is one teacher's way of thinking about stories. Each one in the list has its name in bold and a line saying the job it does, such as *Weiland (character arcs): The arc: the Lie, the Ghost, the Want and the Need*. If it adds blanks, a line says **Adds:** and where, such as *Goal and motive (Ingermanson) on each character*.

The list includes beat sheets (*Save the Cat (Brody for novels)*, *The Hero's Journey*, *Story Circle*, *Seven-Point Structure*, *Three-Act Structure*), character and scene methods (McKee, Weiland, Snowflake by Ingermanson, Scene and Sequel by Swain, The Moral Premise by Williams, the Writers Helping Writers thesauri by Ackerman and Puglisi), any methods of your own, and **I find my story as I write**.

1. Tick a method to use it. None is required, and using several together is normal.
2. When you tick the first beat sheet, a message says, for example, *Save the Cat (Brody for novels) is now the beat sheet for this novel.* The story strip at the top of the window fills in with acts and beats.
3. A box called **How I use it** appears under each ticked method (*your own note, yours to change*). Write what the method does for you in this book. Click away to keep it.
4. A method of your own is marked *yours*, with an **Edit** link that opens **My methods**.
5. **Make a method of my own** opens **My methods**, where you can add a method from any teacher or book, with its own beats, questions and blanks.

**I find my story as I write** is for writers who don't outline. Tick it with no beat sheet and the book has no beat sheet: the story strip shows your chapters instead of beats.

**The common mistake** is ticking every method because they all sound useful. Each adds blanks to your pages. Tick the ones you actually think in. You can add more any time.

**Example.** A writer who plots with *Save the Cat* and builds characters with Weiland ticks those two. In **How I use it** beside Weiland she writes, "Only for my two leads."

### The beat sheet that drives the board

When two or more beat sheets are ticked, a line appears at the bottom of the toolkit: **The beat sheet that drives the board:** with a menu. The beat sheet chosen there is the one your Board, your story strip and **I'm stuck** use. The others stay available as other ways to look at the book.

Beside it, **Offer my placed scenes on the matching beats** (ticked at first) means: when you switch beat sheets, scene cards you had placed are offered on the matching beats of the new sheet, in pencil, waiting for you to decide. A message says so when it happens.

### What your choices switch on

Near the bottom of the page, this list sums up every blank and tool your current choices have added, one line each, such as *Save the Cat (Brody for novels): On the board (Save the Cat) on each beat*. Genres, which add questions rather than blanks, are listed under the genres instead.

**When it helps:** when you meet a group of blanks on a record page and wonder where it came from. A group named *Goal and motive (Ingermanson)* came from ticking Snowflake.

### What changing setup does to what you wrote

The page promises: *Nothing you have written is ever hidden or removed by a change here.* Here is what that means in practice.

- **Turning a method off** removes its empty blanks from your forms. Any blank that already holds something you wrote stays on the page, with its answers.
- **Before your first choice in Novel setup,** a brand-new novel shows Weiland's arc blanks (the Ghost, the Lie, the Want and the Need) on arcs and the *Save the Cat* board blanks on beats, so there is something to start from. After your first choice, only what you have chosen shows. (A novel that starts from your starting point, below, with methods already ticked, counts as set up from the start.) If empty arc blanks you were using disappear, tick **Weiland (character arcs)**. This is working as designed, not a lost answer.
- **Genres and tones** never remove anything. They add questions and, for some genres, worldbuilding kinds.

## Plant the seed: six questions for a blank novel

**What it is:** a card on **Where was I** for a brand-new novel. It asks six short questions to give a blank book its first roots. The card's heading is **Plant the seed**.

**When you see it:** in a novel with no characters yet, until you plant it or click **Not now**. A novel brought in from planning never shows it, because it already has characters.

**The six questions** (the gray words inside each box hint at an answer):

| Question | Hint in the box | Where your answer goes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Who is this story about?** | Their name, or a working name. | A new character record with that name, with *Role* set to *Lead*. If you answer the other character questions but leave the name empty, the record is called *The lead*. |
| **Who are they, in a sentence?** | Where they are in life when the story finds them. | That character's *Who they are* |
| **What do they want?** | Something concrete: you would know it if they got it. | That character's *Goal* |
| **What stands in the way?** | A person, a force, or something inside them. | That character's *Conflict* |
| **What do they stand to lose?** | If they fail, what is gone for good? | That character's *What they stand to lose* |
| **How might it end?** | A guess is fine. You can change it any time. | The **Story world** record's *How it might end* |

**Every answer goes in as Developing, in pencil.** Nothing is decided until you decide it.

**How to plant it:**

1. Click **Where was I** on the right edge.
2. Answer any of the six questions. Skip the rest.
3. Click **Plant it**. (It stays gray until at least one box has words.)
4. Story Canon opens the **Story Brain** at your characters, where your new lead is waiting. Click the name to see the answers on the record.

**Not now** puts the card away for good in this novel. Your novel is fine without it.

**Why it helps:** a blank novel is the hardest page there is. Six small answers turn it into a person who wants something, with something in the way. That is enough to write a first scene.

**The common mistake** is waiting until every answer is perfect. These are pencil answers. Write the first thing that feels true; you can decide later.

**Looks broken but isn't:** the *Goal* and *Conflict* answers appear under a group called **Goal and motive (Ingermanson)** on the character's page, even if you haven't ticked Snowflake in Novel setup. A group that holds your words always shows.

**Example.** Who: *Wren Talley*. In a sentence: *The last keeper of Gull Point light, forty, who has not left the island in twenty years.* Wants: *To keep the light burning past its decommission date.* In the way: *The Coast Guard, and her fear of the mainland.* Could lose: *The only home she has known.* Might end: *She hands the light to a stranger and sails away.*

## Stages and drafts: two different questions

On every book's cover in the Library, and on **Where was I** just under the **Write** button, you will see two labels side by side, such as *Plotting and planning* and *First draft*. They answer two different questions. On Where was I, a line under them says so: *The stage is what you are doing with the book now. The draft is which full pass of the manuscript you are on.*

### Stage: what you are doing with the book now

A **stage** is a label for where the book is in its life. The usual list is: *Idea*, *Plotting and planning*, *First draft*, *Second draft*, *Third draft*, *Revising*, *Editing*, *With beta readers* (trusted early readers), *With an editor*, *Querying* (sending to literary agents), *On submission* (an agent is offering it to publishers), *Published* and *Resting*.

- **Where to change it:** the stage menu on the book's cover in the Library, or the **Stage** menu on **Where was I**.
- **What it does:** it is a label to help you keep track. It doesn't lock, hide or change anything. A new novel starts with no stage (*Choose a stage*).
- **Your own stages:** in **Settings**, under **For all my novels**, the box **Stages a book can be in** holds the list, one per line. Rename, add or remove lines, then click **Save settings** at the bottom.

### Draft: which full pass you are on

A **draft** is one complete pass through the manuscript. Your first draft is the first full version; revising it from start to finish makes a second. Story Canon numbers them for you, starting at *First draft*.

- **Where it shows:** on **Where was I** as *Draft: First draft*, with a **Drafts** link beside it, and in gray on the book's cover when it differs from the stage.
- **What it does:** when you finish a draft, the **Drafts** page keeps an exact copy of the whole book as it stood, so you can compare any scene with any earlier draft. The Drafts chapter of this guide covers that page.

### How stage and draft move together

They are separate, but they help each other in two ways:

- **A book that arrives mid-way.** If you set the stage to *Second draft* or *Third draft* before you have kept any draft here, the draft you are in takes that name too. A book you bring in already on its third draft is called *Third draft* from the start.
- **Finishing a draft.** When you keep a finished draft, a book whose stage is a draft name (or has no stage) moves to the next draft's name. A book whose stage is something else, such as *Revising*, keeps its stage.

**The common mistake** is reading *Plotting and planning* beside *First draft* as a contradiction. It isn't. It means: you are planning, and when you write, it will be your first draft.

**Example.** *The Keeper of Gull Point* is at the stage *With beta readers*, and its draft is *Second draft*. The book is out with readers; the version they have is the second full pass.

## A starting point for new novels

**What it is:** a way to save this novel's working preferences as your defaults, so every new novel starts the way you like to work instead of from nothing.

**Where it is:** the last button in Novel setup, **Make this my starting point for new novels**.

**What carries over to new novels:**

| Carries over | Stays with this novel only |
| --- | --- |
| Your name from **Settings**, and the wording of the *it's on you now* marker for work you keep | Genres |
| Your storytelling toolkit, and the beat sheet that drives the board | Story types and their ingredients |

**How to do it:**

1. Open the novel whose setup you like, then **Novel setup** (Settings, then **Open novel setup**).
2. Scroll to the bottom and click **Make this my starting point for new novels**.
3. A message at the top says *Saved as your defaults. New novels start this way; your other novels are unchanged.*

From then on, every novel you start in the Library begins with those choices. You can change anything in a new novel's own setup.

**Why it helps:** if you always plot with *Save the Cat* and Weiland, you set that up once, not for every book.

**The common mistake** is expecting it to change your existing novels. It doesn't. It only affects novels you start after you click it. To change an existing novel, open its own Novel setup.

**Example.** After setting up *The Keeper of Gull Point* with Save the Cat and Weiland, the writer clicks **Make this my starting point for new novels**. Next spring she starts *Salt and Sugar*. It opens with Save the Cat as its beat sheet and Weiland ticked, and no genres chosen, ready for her to say what this one is.
