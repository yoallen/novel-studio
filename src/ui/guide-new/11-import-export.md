# Bringing things in and sending things out

This chapter covers moving work between Story Canon and the rest of the world. Read it when you have writing or planning that lives somewhere else (a manuscript started in Word, a story bible, a plotting spreadsheet, a copy your editor marked up), or when you need a file made from your book (a Word document for an agent, a PDF to print, an e-book for a friend's reader). You do not need any of it to start writing; come back when you have a file in hand.

Two words first. To **import** is to bring a file from somewhere else into Story Canon. To **export** is to make a file from your book to use somewhere else. Both happen on one view, and both keep the same promises:

- **Nothing comes into your novel until you have looked it over.** Every import shows you a preview first.
- **Your words come in exactly as written,** and go out exactly as written. Every file is checked word for word.
- **Importing never overwrites anything,** and bringing in the same document twice never makes duplicates.
- **Exporting never changes your novel.**

## The Import and export view

**Where it is:** **Import and export** is one of the views in the list on the right edge of the window, below the word *and*.

Across the top are three smaller tabs. The one you are on is darker; the others look like links.

| Tab | What it is for |
| --- | --- |
| **Bring something in** | Importing a file, and the lists of imports you started or finished. |
| **Send something out** | Exporting your book or your planning, and your plotting sheet. |
| **Your documents** | Every planning document you brought in, kept whole and readable. |

While the program stays open, Story Canon remembers which tab you were on, so the view opens there next time.

Moving a whole novel to another computer is not done here. That uses an archive, made in Settings (see [Backups and archives](#backups-and-archives)).

## Bring something in: the four kinds of file

On **Bring something in**, the page opens with "Nothing goes into your novel until you have looked it over. Your words come in exactly as written, and every planning passage keeps a link back to the page it came from."

**How to bring in a file:**

1. Click **Import and export** in the list on the right. The **Bring something in** tab is first.
2. Choose what you have. There are four choices, each a round button with a short description under it (see the table below).
3. If you chose **A planning document**, a small dropdown appears, **It is a**, with **story bible**, **archive of decisions** or **other planning document**. Pick the closest. It only names what kind of document it is.
4. Click **Choose a file** and pick the file. While Story Canon reads it, the button says **Reading the file…**.
5. The preview opens. Look it over (see [Looking over an import before it goes in](#looking-over-an-import-before-it-goes-in)) and click the button at the bottom to bring it in.

| Choose | For | File types |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Chapters of my manuscript** | Your book's text, from Word or another writing program. | Word (.docx), Rich Text (.rtf), Markdown (.md), OpenDocument (.odt), plain text (.txt) |
| **A planning document** | A story bible, an archive of decisions, character notes. | .docx, .rtf, .md, .txt, .odt |
| **A plotting spreadsheet** | Your beat sheet in Excel. | Excel (.xlsx) |
| **My editor's changes** | A Word file you exported from Story Canon that someone revised with Track Changes. | .docx |

If you pick an Excel file under any choice, Story Canon knows it is a spreadsheet and treats it as one.

A few words in that table, explained. A **story bible** is the document many writers keep with everything decided about their book: characters, places, rules, history. An **archive of decisions** is a running record of choices made over time. A **beat sheet** is a list of a book's beats in order with roughly where each falls. **Track Changes** is the Word feature that records each edit (who deleted what, who added what) so the writer can accept or refuse it.

### Chapters of my manuscript

**What it is.** Story Canon reads the file, finds where chapters and scenes begin, and proposes how to split it. You check the split, fix anything, and click **Add these chapters to my novel**. The chapters go in after the ones you already have, word for word. Italics, bold and other formatting come with them.

**Why it matters.** You do not have to start over to use Story Canon. A book begun somewhere else comes in whole, already divided into scenes you can plan around.

**Do you need it?** Only if you started the book somewhere else, or wrote a chapter on a computer that does not have Story Canon.

### A planning document

**What it is.** Story Canon breaks the document into **passages** (one paragraph or labeled block each) and shows each passage beside the record and field where it would go. You tick the passages you want in your records. The whole document is kept in **Your documents** either way, so passages you leave unticked are still there and still searchable.

**Why it matters.** If you kept notes about your characters, world or plot in Word, they come into the Story Brain with how sure you were about each one, and every fact keeps a link back to the exact passage it came from.

**Do you need it?** Only if you planned outside Story Canon. Many writers never use it.

### A plotting spreadsheet

**What it is.** Story Canon reads an Excel beat sheet (one beat per row, with where each falls in the book) and saves where it puts each beat as another **placement**: a way of saying where each beat falls, kept beside the one that comes with Save the Cat. (**Save the Cat** is a well-known way of shaping a story into fifteen beats, from Blake Snyder's screenwriting book and Jessica Brody's version for novels.) Or, if you choose, it turns the sheet into a beat sheet of your own.

**Why it matters.** If you plotted in a spreadsheet, perhaps from a class or a template, its numbers come with you, and you can switch between them and Save the Cat's.

**Do you need it?** Only if you have such a spreadsheet. The sheet needs a header row whose first cell is "Beats" (or "Beat", "Stages", "Steps", "Points" or "Plot points"), with one beat on each row below it. Columns headed with "250 words per page" or "300 words per page" give the word counts. Without such a header row, Story Canon says "No beat rows were found. The importer looks for a header row that starts with "Beats"."

### My editor's changes

**What it is.** Story Canon compares the Word file with the scenes it came from, shows each tracked change with who made it, and lets you accept or turn down each one. The editor's comments can come in too. Nothing changes until you decide.

**Why it matters.** You can work with an editor or critique partner who uses Word, and keep writing in Story Canon, without copying changes over by hand.

**Do you need it?** Only if someone edits your book in Word.

**The common mistake** is bringing in an edited Word file that did not come from Story Canon's own export. Story Canon finds each scene in the file by invisible markers its Word export puts at the start and end of every scene. A file without them gets the message "This Word file has no scene markers, so its changes cannot be matched to scenes." Always start from a Word file you made on **Send something out**. To bring in some other Word file, use **Chapters of my manuscript** instead.

## Looking over an import before it goes in

Every import opens a preview first. At its top is **All imports** (back to the list) and the file's name. Any warnings show in shaded boxes, such as "Pictures in the document were not imported." or "Tables were brought in as plain lines, one row per paragraph." At the bottom, a bar that stays in view holds three things:

- **The button that brings it in.** Its words depend on the kind: **Add these chapters to my novel**, **Add the checked passages**, **Use this sheet** (or **Make it my beat sheet**), or **Make my decisions**.
- The reminder "One step. You can undo it from the Story Trail." The whole import is one change, and one **Undo** in the Story Trail takes it all back (see [Story Trail: the history of every change](#story-trail-the-history-of-every-change)).
- **Don't import this**, which drops the import. Your novel is not touched.

If you leave the preview without deciding, the import waits under **Waiting for you to look over** on **Bring something in**. Click its name to pick up where you left off.

### Import preview: a manuscript

The preview opens with a line such as "Here is how the file reads: 2 chapters, 4,812 words. Rename, join or leave out anything before it goes in. The words themselves are never changed."

Under it, a shaded note says exactly where the chapters will land. On a book with no chapters yet: "These become the first chapters of your novel." On a book with one chapter: "They go in after your last chapter ("The Lamp"), so the first one becomes chapter 2. Your chapter stays just as it is. You can move chapters later in the Writing Room."

Then each proposed chapter, with its scenes listed under it:

| On each chapter | What it does |
| --- | --- |
| The tick box at the left | Ticked, the chapter goes in. Untick it to leave it out. Left-out chapters fade. |
| The title box | The chapter's title. Click in and type to rename it. |
| **Part of the chapter before** | Joins this chapter to the one above it, for when a break was misread as a new chapter. |

| On each scene | What it does |
| --- | --- |
| The tick box | Include or leave out this scene. |
| The title box | The scene's name. Rename it here. |
| The word count and first line | So you can recognize it. |
| **Same scene as before** | Joins this scene to the one above it, for when a stray break split one scene in two. |

Warnings you may see, and what they mean:

- "No chapter headings were found, so everything is in one chapter. Join or split it after you accept, or mark chapter starts in the file and import again." Story Canon looks for chapter headings. In Word, that means lines set in a Heading style. Mark each chapter start as a heading and import again, or split the chapter later in the Writing Room.
- "Text before the first chapter is kept as its own chapter. Leave it out if it is a title page."
- "3 very short scenes (under 40 words). These may be epigraphs or stray breaks; join them to the scene before if so." An **epigraph** is a short quotation at the start of a chapter or book.
- "Paragraph styles read as ordinary paragraphs:" followed by style names. Those paragraphs came in as plain text, with their words unchanged.

At the bottom: "4,812 words will go in. Checked afterwards, word for word." After you click **Add these chapters to my novel**, a page headed "Done:" and the file's name shows how many chapters, scenes and words were added and the result of that check, with **Go to the Writing Room** and **Bring in something else**.

Bringing in a manuscript does not count toward your daily word goal. It is not new writing.

**Example.** You wrote two chapters of *The Keeper of Gull Point* in Word before you found Story Canon. The preview finds "Chapter One: The Lamp" with two scenes and "Chapter Two: The Boat" with two. The second scene of chapter one is only nine words, a stray line left by an extra page break, so you tick **Same scene as before** to join it to the first. Then you click **Add these chapters to my novel**.

### Import preview: a planning document

The preview opens with "Each passage keeps your label, and its exact words stay in the document. Checked passages go into the record shown beside them."

**Document date.** A date box sits at the top, with "Your later decisions stay ahead of anything dated earlier." Story Canon uses this date to keep your decisions in the right order: something you decided last month is not replaced by a document written last year. If the document has no date, a warning says "The document has no date. Set one before applying, so your later decisions stay ahead of it." Set it to the day the document was last true.

**New records it would make.** If a heading names a character or set piece you do not have yet, the preview lists it under **New records it would make**, with a small label saying its kind and a box to fix its name before it is made.

**Which passages you see.** Three small tabs:

- **Going in**, with a count: passages that are ticked.
- **Staying in the document**, with a count: passages that are not ticked. They stay readable and searchable in **Your documents**.
- **All**.

A gray note beside them may say how many passages are **already in your records** from an earlier import. Those are skipped automatically, never added twice. If you bring in a document you imported before, a warning says how many passages are new or changed.

**Each passage** is grouped under its top heading and shows:

| Part | What it is |
| --- | --- |
| The tick box | Ticked passages go in when you click **Add the checked passages**. |
| A small gray line | The headings above it in the document, such as "Characters > Ana Ruiz". |
| The passage | Its label in bold (if it had one), then its words. Long passages are shortened here, but go in whole. |
| The status dropdown | How sure the passage is. See [Imported passages: how labels become statuses](#imported-passages-how-labels-become-statuses). |
| **into** and the record dropdown | Which record it goes into. **Stays in the document** means none. A beat shows as "(beat)" and a new record as "(new)". |
| **Which part…** | Which field on that record it fills. |
| A gray note, sometimes | Why it was placed this way, or a caution, such as "You decided this later. That later decision stays current; this one is kept as history." |

**How Story Canon decides where a passage goes.** It reads your headings. A passage under a heading that names one of your records ("Ana Ruiz") goes to that record. A passage under a Save the Cat beat's name ("Catalyst") goes to that beat. A passage under a heading that names nothing it knows stays in the document unticked, for you to place by choosing a record in the **into** dropdown.

**Things that look odd but are working as designed:**

- **Going in (0).** If your document's headings do not name records you already have, nothing is ticked at first. Click **Staying in the document**, choose a record for each passage you want, and tick it.
- **"Headings that match more than one record, left for you to place."** A heading could mean two records, so Story Canon will not guess. Place those passages yourself.
- **"This document follows the Save the Cat beats. Applying will turn the beats on for this novel so these passages have a home."**
- **"This document may be newer than a decision you made here yourself. Choose which stands before including it."** The passage is left unticked so your own decision is never replaced without your say.
- **The Done list says "kept whole, every passage searchable."** A planning document is always kept, even if you added no passages. That line is not an error.

After you click **Add the checked passages**, the Done page counts the passages added, by status, and how many stay in the document, "where search and "Where did this come from?" still find them." (**Where did this come from?** is a link on a record that shows the passage a fact came from.) Click **Open the Story Brain** to see them.

**Example.** Your old notes file has a heading "Cyrus Hale" with the paragraph "Decided. He was on the harbor the night Eli drowned." Cyrus already has a record, so the passage is ticked, going **into** Cyrus Hale, status **Decided**. You set the document date to the day you last edited the file and click **Add the checked passages**.

### Imported passages: how labels become statuses

When your document labels a passage (for example by starting it with "Decided." or "Still open:"), Story Canon reads the label exactly and turns it into the matching status:

| Your document says | It comes in as |
| --- | --- |
| Decided | **Decided** |
| Developing, Decided in spirit, What it is | **Developing** |
| Still open, Open, Over to me, My move, My turn, Now I write, Hand it to me | **Still open** |
| Example, One way it could be done, One way I could play it | **Example** |
| Here's a suggestion, Editorial possibility | **Suggestion** |
| Set aside | **Set aside** |

A passage with no label shows **No label** in the dropdown. For a character, an unlabeled passage comes in as **Developing**, with the note "No label in the document; it comes in as Developing. Change it if it is more or less settled."

You can change any passage's status in its dropdown before you bring it in. The label in your document is never changed. If you renamed the statuses in Settings, the dropdown uses your words (see [Settings and your other computers](#settings-and-your-other-computers)).

**Why it matters.** A story bible is full of things you were sure of and things you were only trying out. If they all came in as plain text, a maybe would turn into a fact without anyone deciding it. Reading your own labels keeps each idea exactly as settled as you said it was.

### Import preview: a plotting spreadsheet

The preview shows, from top to bottom:

- **Make this a beat sheet of my own**, a tick box. Ticked, each row of the sheet becomes a beat, placed where the sheet puts it, and every novel can then use it, like Save the Cat. Unticked, the sheet only adds another placement for your existing beats.
- A line saying how many rows matched your Save the Cat beats. A row that did not match shows a **no match** label.
- Warnings, such as a word count that does not agree with its page number: "Possible typo; 27,500 is suggested."
- **Use the numbers at 250 words per page** or **300 words per page**, when the sheet has both. This is the sheet's **page density**: how many words it counts as one page.
- **Use the suggested corrections for flagged numbers.**
- **Keep each beat's description**, so the sheet's notes stay with each beat as your own teaching.
- **Name for this placement choice**, the name it will have in lists.
- A table: **Row in the sheet**, **Beat**, **Words** at the chosen page density, and **Notes**.

Click **Use this sheet** (or **Make it my beat sheet**). The Done page tells you where to see it: Narrative Zoom, on the **Where beats fall** tab, with **Open Narrative Zoom** (see [Narrative Zoom](#narrative-zoom)).

### Import preview: an editor's changes

A line counts the changes, the scenes they are in, and any comments: "14 changes in 3 scenes, and 2 comments. Nothing changes until you decide. Anything you leave undecided stays as you wrote it."

- **Keep the editor's comments as notes beside each scene**, a tick box. Each comment is kept as a thought attached to its scene, so it waits in the scene plan under **Attached to this scene**, with who wrote it and the words it was near.
- For each changed scene: its name and chapter, with **Accept all** and **Turn all down**.
- For each change: a few words before it, the removed words struck through, the added words highlighted, a few words after, and who made it and when. Then **Accept** and **Turn down**. Click a pressed button again to undo your choice.
- If you changed a scene yourself since you exported it, a note warns "You have changed this scene since you exported it. The editor's changes were made to the earlier text."
- If the editor changed text without Track Changes on, **Compare the editor's text with yours** opens the two together (struck words are only in yours, highlighted words only in the editor's), with a tick box, **Use the editor's version of this scene**.

Click **Make my decisions**. Each scene's earlier version is kept in its history, so you can still go back with [Earlier versions of a scene](#earlier-versions-of-a-scene).

**Example.** Your critique partner returns "The Keeper of Gull Point.docx" with Track Changes on. The preview shows eight changes in "The empty boat." You accept the six that tighten sentences, turn down the two that change Marisol's voice, keep the comments as notes, and click **Make my decisions**.

## The import lists

On **Bring something in**, under the four choices, two lists may appear.

**Waiting for you to look over** holds imports you opened but did not finish. Each shows its file name, its kind (manuscript, planning document, plotting sheet, or editor's changes), and when you started it. Click the name to finish.

**Done** holds the last twenty finished imports. Each row shows the file name, its kind, and what became of it:

| Words | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **added** and a date | It was brought in on that date. |
| **kept whole, every passage searchable** | A planning document. It is in **Your documents** whether or not passages went into records. **Open it** opens it there. |
| **not brought in** | You chose **Don't import this**. Nothing was added. |
| **not confirmed** (a small label) | The word-for-word check after the import did not pass. Look at what came in before relying on it. |

## Your documents: every planning document, kept whole

**What it is.** **Your documents** lists every planning document you brought in, with how many passages it has and its date. Click a name to read it. With none yet, it says "No documents yet. Bring one in from the first tab."

**Why keep the whole document?** Because a passage you did not need today may matter later, and because **Where did this come from?** on any record can open the exact passage a fact came from.

Inside a document:

- **All documents** goes back to the list.
- A line counts the passages, "kept word for word," with the document's date.
- **Find in this document** narrows the passages to ones containing what you type.
- Each passage shows where it sits (its headings and paragraph number), then its words.
- Under a passage that became part of a record, a small label names the record, the field and its status, such as "Cyrus Hale, history: Decided."

When you arrive from Search, the document opens scrolled to the passage you found, marked with a pink bar at its left.

## Send something out: making a file from your book

**What it is.** **Send something out** makes a file from your book, your planning, or both, in any of nine formats.

**Why it matters.** Sooner or later the book has to leave the program: to a critique group, an editor, an agent, a printer, an e-reader. Every file is read back after it is made and checked against your chapters, so you never send a file with a chapter missing.

**Do you need it?** Every writer does, eventually. Most only ever use the **Working manuscript** and **Editorial manuscript** presets.

**Where it is:** **Import and export**, then the **Send something out** tab.

The page opens with "Exporting never changes your novel. Each file is read back after it is made and checked against your chapters, word for word." Then four areas from top to bottom: **Start from**, **What goes in**, **How it looks**, and the summary with the **Export** button. **Your plotting sheet** is at the very bottom.

**How to export, step by step:**

1. Under **Start from**, choose the preset closest to what you need.
2. Under **What goes in**, check what is ticked: the manuscript, any planning sections, and which chapters.
3. Under **How it looks**, check the format and the choices under it, including **Title page**.
4. Read the summary and any shaded notes above the **Export** button.
5. Click **Export**, then choose where to save the file.

### Start from: the export presets

A **preset** is a ready-made set of choices for a common job. Picking one fills in everything below it. You can then change anything; once you do, no preset is selected, which is fine. The page opens with **Working manuscript** chosen.

| Preset | What it makes |
| --- | --- |
| **Working manuscript** | Word, every chapter, easy to read and edit. No title page. |
| **Editorial manuscript** | For an editor or agent: Word, double-spaced 12 point, title page, your name and title in the header, # between scenes. |
| **Beta reader copy** | Word with a title page. The story only. No planning notes, nothing private. |
| **Print review** | A Letter-size PDF to read and mark up on paper. |
| **E-book** | EPUB for an e-reader, with a title page. |
| **Character profiles** | Every character, field by field, with your statuses. Word document. No manuscript. |
| **Planning binder** | Characters, beats, places, set pieces, open decisions and setups and payoffs, in Word with a title page. No manuscript. |

Presets you saved yourself appear here too, with a **Remove** link (see [Saving your own export preset](#saving-your-own-export-preset)).

### What goes in

**The manuscript** (ticked at first) includes your chapters. Under it:

- **Version**, only when you have kept a draft: **The book as it is now**, or any kept draft, "as kept." Choosing a kept draft exports the whole book as it stood then.
- **Every chapter** or **Only the chapters I pick** (see [Choosing chapters to export](#choosing-chapters-to-export)).

Then the planning sections, each a tick box. Tick any to add them after the manuscript, or on their own with the manuscript unticked:

| Section | What it holds |
| --- | --- |
| **Character profiles** | Every character, field by field. |
| **Beat sheet** | Your beats in order, with what you planned for each. |
| **Places** | Every place record. |
| **Set pieces** | Your big planned sequences. |
| **Open decisions** | What is still undecided. |
| **Setups and payoffs** | Each clue and where it is planted and pays off. |
| **Timeline** | Your scenes in story order. |
| **Soundtrack** | Your songs and where they play. |
| **Every other record** | All remaining records. |

With any section ticked, two more choices appear:

- **Include examples, labeled "Example"**, ticked at first. Examples stay clearly marked so a reader never takes one for a decision.
- **Include private writing aids (casting, theme music, playlists)**, unticked at first. These are the private fields on a character's last page. Leave it unticked for anything you send to another person.

### Choosing chapters to export

1. Under **The manuscript**, click **Only the chapters I pick**.
2. A list of every chapter appears, numbered and all ticked, under "Untick any chapter to leave it out."
3. Untick the chapters you do not want.

Click **Every chapter** to go back to the whole book. The file's name says which chapters it holds, such as "The Keeper of Gull Point (chapters 1, 2, 3).docx". A file with planning sections and no manuscript has "planning" in its name. (When **Version** is set to a kept draft, the chapter list is not offered; the kept draft goes out whole.)

**When to use it:** sending the first three chapters to a contest or an agent, or one chapter to your writing group.

### How it looks: format, headings, scene breaks

| Choice | What it does |
| --- | --- |
| **Format** | The kind of file. See [Every export format](#every-export-format). |
| **Chapter headings** | How each chapter starts: **Number and title** ("Chapter 2: The Boat"), **Number only** ("Chapter 2"), or **Title only** ("The Boat"). The dropdown shows each style using one of your own chapter titles, or "Its Title" if your chapters have no titles of their own. A chapter with no title of its own just says "Chapter 2" in any style. |
| **Between scenes** | What prints where one scene ends and the next begins. It starts as `* * *`. Type anything up to twenty characters, such as `#`. |
| **Title page** | Adds a page with the title and the author name. Not offered for plain text, Markdown or Story Canon data files. The name is your pen name if you set one in Settings, under **Author name on this book** (see [Settings and your other computers](#settings-and-your-other-computers)). |
| **Manuscript format** | Double-spaced, 12 point, name and title in the header: the layout agents and editors expect. Offered for Word, Rich Text, OpenDocument and PDF. |
| **Show scene titles** | Prints each scene's name above it. Off at first, since scene names are usually working labels. |

### Every export format

| Format | Best for | What it cannot keep |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Word (.docx)** | Editors, agents, critique partners, your own editing in Word. The only format whose edits can come back in with **My editor's changes**. | Nothing. |
| **PDF** | Printing, or reading on any device. | It cannot be edited as text. A few rare characters may print as blanks; you are warned which. |
| **E-book (.epub)** | Kindle, Kobo, Apple Books and other e-readers. **EPUB** is the standard e-book file. | Manuscript layout and page numbers. E-readers choose their own page size. |
| **OpenDocument (.odt)** | LibreOffice and other free word processors. | Nothing. |
| **Rich Text (.rtf)** | Older writing programs, and almost any word processor. | Nothing. |
| **Markdown (.md)** | Simple text with light formatting, for websites and notes apps. | Underline, centered paragraphs, page layout. |
| **Plain text (.txt)** | The simplest copy, readable anywhere. | Italics, bold, underline, headings and page layout. |
| **Web page (.html)** | Opening in a web browser or posting online. | Page layout and manuscript format. |
| **Story Canon data (.json), for other programs** | A structured copy for software, not for reading. | Not meant for people. To move a novel between computers, use an archive instead (see [Save an archive of this novel](#save-an-archive-of-this-novel)). |

### Before you export: the summary above the Export button

Just above the **Export** button, a short summary updates as you choose, such as "3 chapters and Character profiles, as Word (.docx)." Under it, shaded notes warn you about anything this particular file would lose, or include by surprise:

- What the format cannot keep, counted for your book: "Plain text does not keep italics (12 passages). Your manuscript itself is unchanged."
- "2 notes left for later, [[in double brackets]], are still in the text and will print as written. They are listed on Where was I." A **note for later** is a reminder you typed inside your pages between double square brackets (see [The Writing Room](#the-writing-room)). Notes print exactly as typed, so clear them first, or leave them on purpose for a working copy.
- "Part titles (such as "Part One") are not printed yet. The chapters export without them."

Read these before you click. They are the reason nothing surprises you after the file is sent.

### After Export: the check and where the file goes

1. Click **Export**. The button reads **Making the file…**.
2. In the Windows program, a save window opens, starting in your Documents folder, inside **Story Canon**, then **Exports**. Choose a place and a name, and click **Save**.
3. A notice shows the file name, its size, and the result of the check, such as "Checked: all 3 chapters are in the file, in order, word for word."
4. Under it, one sentence says where the file went: "Saved to" and the exact path, with **Show it in its folder**, which opens that folder in Windows.

**If you close the save window without saving,** the notice says "The file was made but not saved, because the save window was closed," with **Save it**. Click it to choose a place after all.

**If the check fails,** the notice is shaded as a warning and starts "Not confirmed." with what is missing or different. Do not send that file; export again, or try another format.

(In a test version of Story Canon running in a web browser, the file goes where the browser puts downloads, usually the Downloads folder, and the notice says so, with **Save it again**.)

**The common mistake** is sending a working copy to an agent. Before you send the book to anyone, choose **Editorial manuscript** (or **Beta reader copy** for a friend), check that **Title page** is ticked, and read the notes about notes for later.

**Example.** An agent asks for the first three chapters. You choose **Editorial manuscript**, click **Only the chapters I pick**, untick everything after chapter 3, and read the summary: "3 chapters, as Word (.docx)." A note says one note for later is still in the text, so you go fix it, come back, and click **Export**. The notice says "Checked: all 3 chapters are in the file, in order, word for word."

### Saving your own export preset

If you set up choices you will want again, such as "Contest entry: chapters 1 to 3, editorial format":

1. Make your choices.
2. Type a name in the box **Save these choices as…**, beside the **Export** button.
3. Click **Save these choices**.

Your preset now appears under **Start from**, with a **Remove** link. Saved presets belong to this novel.

## Your plotting sheet

**What it is.** At the bottom of **Send something out** is **Your plotting sheet**: "Your beat sheet layout, filled in with this novel's length, both page densities, and what is planned at each beat." It is an Excel file with one row per beat, showing where each beat falls in words and pages, what you planned for it, and how many scene cards are placed there.

**Why it matters.** It turns your target length into concrete numbers: the Midpoint of a 90,000-word book is around word 45,000. Many writers print it and keep it beside the desk.

**Do you need it?** Only if you like working from a spreadsheet. Narrative Zoom shows the same placements inside the program.

**How to make it:**

1. In the dropdown, choose how beats are placed:
   - **Novel percentages (Brody)** places each beat at a percentage of a novel, from Jessica Brody's *Save the Cat! Writes a Novel*, which adapted the beats for novels.
   - **Screenplay proportions (Snyder, 110 pages scaled)** uses Blake Snyder's original page numbers from *Save the Cat!*, written for a 110-page screenplay, scaled to your book's length.
   - Any placement you brought in from a spreadsheet also appears, by the name you gave it.
2. Click **Export the sheet (.xlsx)**. It saves the same way as any export, and the check says, for example, "Checked: all 15 beats are in the sheet."

If you are writing a novel and are unsure, choose **Novel percentages (Brody)**.

**Where the numbers come from.** Word counts come from **Target length in words** in Settings, and page counts from **Words per page for page estimates** (see [Settings and your other computers](#settings-and-your-other-computers)). With no target set, the sheet uses the words you have drafted so far once that is at least 20,000 words; before that, it uses a typical novel length of 90,000 words.

**This looks wrong but is not:** a sheet planned for 90,000 words when your book will be much shorter or longer means no target length is set. Set one in Settings and export the sheet again.

The sheet needs a beat sheet. On a novel without one, Story Canon says "Choose a beat sheet for this novel first."

**Example.** You set **Target length in words** to 80,000 for *The Keeper of Gull Point*, choose **Novel percentages (Brody)**, and click **Export the sheet (.xlsx)**. The sheet shows the Catalyst (the empty boat on the rocks) at about word 8,000, and that you have two scene cards placed there.
