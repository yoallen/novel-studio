# The words Story Canon uses, and getting help

This chapter teaches the small set of words that every screen in Story Canon uses, such as *record*, *field* and *Decided*. It then shows you how to read a record at a glance, and every way to get help when something puzzles you. Read it right after [Welcome to Story Canon](#welcome-to-story-canon), before you start your first novel. Every later chapter uses these words freely, so when one of them feels fuzzy, come back here.

## Why the words come first

Story Canon gives a few ordinary words a particular meaning. Once you know them, every screen makes sense. If you skip them, a sentence such as "click **Decide this** beside the fact in the field" reads like a riddle.

Each word below is explained using only the words before it, so read them in order the first time. Every one comes with an example from the same book, *The Keeper of Gull Point*, a mystery with a heart:

- **Marisol Vega**, 41, is the keeper of the Gull Point lighthouse. She has never left the island.
- Her younger brother **Eli** drowned twelve years ago. That loss is her wound.
- The story starts moving when an empty boat washes onto her rocks.
- **Ana Ruiz** is the new doctor in the harbor town of **Port Ellery**. **Cyrus Hale** is the harbor master, who knows more than he says.

## The shared words, in the order you need them

### Novel

A **novel** is one book you are working on. Story Canon keeps each novel separate: its pages, its plan, its people and its history all belong to it and to nothing else.

- **Why it matters:** you can work on several books without them ever mixing. Switching books never shows you the other book's notes.
- **Example:** *The Keeper of Gull Point* is one novel. If you also have a half-finished romance, that is a second novel, with its own everything.

### Library

The **Library** is your shelf of novels. It is the first screen you see, and the only one you see before a novel is open. Each novel on the shelf shows its title, its word count and the next thing to do in it. Click a title to open it. To come back to the shelf from inside a novel, click **Library** in the header (the row of buttons across the top of the page).

- **The common mistake:** looking for your novels inside **Settings** or **Import and export**. They are always on the Library shelf.
- **Example:** your Library shows *The Keeper of Gull Point*, 14,200 words, with "Next:" and the scene you planned to write.

The chapter [Your first hour: the Library and a new novel](#your-first-hour-the-library-and-a-new-novel) explains everything on the Library screen.

### View

A **view** is one of the main screens of a novel, picked from the list of names down the right edge of the window: **Where was I?**, **The Writing Room**, **Story Brain**, **Narrative Zoom**, **Conversations**, then **Search**, **Story Trail**, **Import and export**, **Settings** and **Help**. The view you are on is lit cream and bold in that list. [Welcome to Story Canon](#welcome-to-story-canon) says what each one is for.

- **Why it matters:** this guide often says "in the Story Brain view" or "open the Writing Room". That always means: click that name in the list on the right.
- **Example:** to look up Port Ellery, you open the **Story Brain** view. To write about it, you open **The Writing Room** view.

### Scene

A **scene** is one stretch of your written book: a piece of continuous action in one place and time, written as prose. In Story Canon a scene is something you write on, in **The Writing Room**. Each scene has a title and its own word count.

- **Why it matters:** Story Canon saves, counts, plans and checks your book scene by scene.
- **Example:** "The empty boat" is a scene: Marisol, at dawn, climbing down the rocks to a boat with nobody in it.

### Chapter

A **chapter** is a group of scenes, in order. In the Writing Room, the column on the left lists your chapters, each with its scenes under it.

- **The common mistake:** thinking you must decide your chapters before you write. You can start with one chapter and one scene and split things up later.
- **Example:** Chapter 1 holds three scenes: "The light at dusk", "The empty boat" and "Port Ellery in the rain".

### Record

A **record** is one thing in your story that gets its own page in the **Story Brain**. A character is a record. So is a place, a relationship, a clue, a question you have. Think of a record as an index card with a name written across the top.

- **Why it matters:** everything you know about one person or place sits in one place, so you stop hunting through old notebooks for the color of her coat.
- **Example:** Marisol Vega is a record. So is the lighthouse, and so is Port Ellery.

### Kind

A **kind** is the sort of record something is. Character is a kind. Place is a kind. Clue is a kind. Each record belongs to exactly one kind, which you choose when you add it (for example with **Add character** in the Story Brain).

- **Why it matters:** the kind decides what a record offers. A character asks about goals and fears; a place asks what it is like and who is usually there.
- **Example:** Marisol and Cyrus Hale are both of the kind *Character*. The lighthouse is of the kind *Place*.

### Field

A **field** is one labeled place to write on a record. Each kind of record offers its own set of fields, grouped under headings. A character has fields such as *Role*, *Who they are* and *What they stand to lose*.

- **Every field is optional.** An empty field is never a mistake, and no book needs all of them.
- **How to write in one:** click **Add** at the right of the field's label, type, and click **Save**. The full steps are in [The Story Brain](#the-story-brain).
- **Example:** Marisol's record has a field called *What they stand to lose*.

### Fact

A **fact** is one entry written in a field. It is more than its words: each fact carries a **status** (how sure you are, explained next), the date and time it was written down, and, when it didn't come from you, its **source** (where it came from). These are shown in a narrow column to the right of the words, called the **margin**, along with buttons for what you can do with the fact.

A field can hold more than one fact. That is on purpose: you can keep two or three possible answers side by side while you make up your mind.

- **Why it matters:** because each fact remembers when it was written and who said it, you can always tell your own decision from an idea you once copied in.
- **Example:** Marisol's *What they stand to lose* field holds two facts: "The lighthouse itself" and "The last thing she has of Eli". Both stay until you choose.

### Status

A **status** is how sure you are about a fact. There are six, and Story Canon always calls them by these words. Each one looks different on a record, the way ink looks different from pencil, so you can tell them apart without reading a word:

| Status | What it means | How it looks on a record |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Decided** | You have made up your mind. | Ink blue words, with a small blue square in front |
| **Developing** | You are still working on it. | Pencil gray words, with a dashed underline |
| **Still open** | A question that matters, which you have not answered yet. | A blank line with a gray prompt in slanted type |
| **Example** | An illustration of what an answer could look like. Never part of your story. | Sepia (warm brown) slanted words, after a small boxed label reading "Example" |
| **Suggestion** | Someone else's idea, waiting for your choice. | A white slip of paper tucked in at a slight angle, in a handwriting typeface |
| **Set aside** | Something you have let go of, but kept. | Folded away under its field; when shown, faded, with a line through it |

When you write a fact yourself, you choose Decided, Developing, Still open or Example. New facts start as Developing. Suggestions only arrive from somewhere else, and Set aside is what you do to a fact you no longer want. How each status behaves, and the buttons for each, are in [The Story Brain](#the-story-brain).

- **Example:** "Eli drowned twelve years ago" is **Decided**. "Who owned the empty boat?" is **Still open**.
- **Not the same as the home page's page.** Your novel's home page, **Where was I?**, has a page called **Still open**. That page gathers what you are carrying in your head (open questions, latest decisions, unsorted thoughts). The status **Still open** is a label on one fact.

(You can rename the status words in **Settings**, under **Words for decisions**. The looks stay the same. This guide always uses the words above.)

### Canon

**Canon** is what is officially true in your story. In Story Canon, canon is everything you have marked **Decided**. Developing facts might become canon. Still open questions are not canon yet. Examples and set-aside facts never are. A suggestion becomes canon only if you decide it.

- **Why it matters:** the rest of the program builds on canon. The continuity checks (tools in Narrative Zoom that catch slips, such as a character knowing something before they could have learned it) and the collaborator (next) treat Decided facts as true and everything else as possible.
- **The common mistake:** marking something Decided just so the page looks finished. If you aren't sure, leave it in pencil. Pencil costs nothing.
- **Example:** "Marisol has never left the island" is canon. "Maybe Ana grew up in Port Ellery" is not, until you decide it.

### The collaborator

**The collaborator** is a writing partner built into Story Canon, powered by Claude, an AI made by the company Anthropic. It is optional, and it is off until you turn it on, novel by novel, in **Settings**, using your own Claude API key (a private code, like a password, that you get from Anthropic and pay for by use).

Before it answers, it reads your decisions, your open questions and what you told Story Canon about yourself, so it knows your book and how you like to be helped. It never types into your manuscript and it never decides anything.

Some screens, and planning you bring in, mention an outside AI by name, such as ChatGPT or Claude. Those are chats your planning came from. Inside Story Canon, the collaborator is the one partner. It has its own chapter: [Conversations and the collaborator](#conversations-and-the-collaborator).

- **Example:** you ask the collaborator, "Why would Cyrus hide what he knows about the boat?" It answers in the conversation, and anything it thinks belongs on a record arrives as a suggestion (next).

### Suggestion and slip

A **suggestion** is an idea about your story that came from someone other than you: the collaborator, or an outside chat (with ChatGPT or Claude, say) that you brought in with your planning. On a record, a suggestion is drawn as a **slip**: a small sheet of paper tucked in between the pages at a slight angle, in a handwriting typeface, rather than written on the page itself.

- **Why it matters:** you see the idea right where it applies, but it never counts as yours, and never becomes canon, until you choose it.
- **What you can do:** take it in, keep it waiting, or let it go. The buttons are taught in [The Story Brain](#the-story-brain).
- **The common mistake:** saying "sure, sounds good" to an AI in a chat and assuming that settled it. It didn't. If you want the idea, accept it on the record, on purpose.
- **Example:** a slip on Cyrus Hale's record reads "He was the last person to see Eli alive." It is the collaborator's idea. It waits.

### Beat

A **beat** is a turning point in a story: a place where the story has to change direction, so that afterward the hero can't go back to how things were. A **beat sheet** is a list of these turning points in order, with a rough idea of where each one falls in the book (for example, "about 10% of the way in"). An **act** is a large section of the story; most beat sheets group their beats into three acts: the beginning, the middle and the end.

In Story Canon, each beat of your beat sheet is a record of the kind *Beat*, and you see them laid out in **Narrative Zoom**. A beat sheet is optional. If you find your story as you write, you can work without one.

- **The common mistake:** treating a beat as a slot to fill with an event. A beat is a *turn*. If nothing is different for the hero afterward, the beat isn't there, however much happens.
- **Example:** *Save the Cat* is a well-known beat sheet, first written for films by Blake Snyder and later adapted for novels by Jessica Brody. Its beat called the **Catalyst** is the moment something happens that kicks the story into motion. In *The Keeper of Gull Point*, the Catalyst is the empty boat washing onto Marisol's rocks. Her quiet routine is broken, and she can't pretend it isn't.

### Scene card

A **scene card** is your plan for one scene, like the index cards novelists pin to a corkboard. It is a record of the kind *Scene card*. It holds what the scene must do, whose goal drives it, what stands in the way and how it turns. You can place a scene card on a beat, to say "this scene happens here".

A scene card and a scene are two different things. The **scene** is the written page, in the Writing Room. The **scene card** is the plan for it. A card can exist long before its scene does, and you can write a scene with no card at all.

- **Example:** the scene card "The empty boat" says: Marisol wants to know whose boat it is; Cyrus arrives and tows it away before she can look inside. It is placed on the Catalyst. The scene with the same name is the prose you write from that plan.

### Page and All pages

A record is laid out like a small notebook with **pages**. Under the record's name is a row of page tabs, each with a number and a name, and at the end of the row, **All pages**. Each page holds the fields that belong together. One page shows at a time; **All pages** shows every page at once, one after another.

- **Why it matters:** a character can have dozens of fields. Pages keep them in sensible groups so a record never becomes one long scroll.
- **Example:** Marisol's record has a page called **Inner life**, where her wound (Eli's drowning) is written.

How to read the pages, and why their numbers can change, is in [Record pages: the numbered tabs](#record-pages-the-numbered-tabs).

### Capture

**Capture** is the button at the right of the header for catching a passing thought without losing your place. You press it (or **Ctrl+Shift+N**), type the thought, choose whether it is a thought, a question or an idea, and save. The thought is kept exactly as you wrote it, and you are back where you were. It works from every view of a novel.

- **Example:** halfway through a scene you think, "Does Ana know how to swim?" You capture it as a question and keep writing.

The full steps are in [Writing Room: Capture while writing](#writing-room-capture-while-writing).

### Help and F1

**Help** is this guide, inside the program. Press **F1** on your keyboard to open it, or click **Help** (the last name in the list on the right inside a novel, or the **Help** button in the Library). The details are in [Getting help](#getting-help).

- **Example:** you are not sure what a beat sheet is. Press **F1**, type *beat* in **Search the guide**, and click the section you want.

### Why it matters

**Why it matters** is the small link under a field on a record. Under many fields is one gray line saying what the field is for, followed by **Why it matters**. Click it to fold out more: whether most books need the field, why it helps, the common mistake and an example. Click **Less** to fold it away.

- **Example:** under Marisol's *What they stand to lose* field, **Why it matters** explains that stakes are what make a reader afraid for a character.

## Reading a record

When you open a record in the Story Brain, you see its name in large letters, a line of small controls, the status legend, and then the row of page tabs with the page itself below. The Story Brain chapter walks through every part from top to bottom: [The Story Brain](#the-story-brain). Two parts are worth knowing on your first day: the legend and the pages.

### Reading a record: the status legend

Just under the record's name is a line of six small samples, one for each status:

*Decided (inked)*, *Developing (pencil)*, *Still open (a blank line)*, *Example, never canon*, *Suggestion (a slip, tucked in)*, *Set aside (faded, kept below)*

Each sample is drawn the way that status looks on the page, so the legend is a key you can glance at whenever you forget which look is which. The last one is faded, with a line through it. "Kept below" means a set-aside fact is not on the field itself: it is folded away under the field, behind the link **Show earlier and set-aside versions**, until you open it. If you renamed the status words in **Settings**, the legend shows your words.

**Example.** On Cyrus Hale's record, one line is ink blue and two are pencil gray. A glance at the legend tells you: one decided, two still being worked on.

### Record pages: the numbered tabs

Under the legend is the row of page tabs. Each tab shows a page number in a small circle and the page's name.

- **The page you are on** is the tab joined to the page, in bold purple.
- **A page number turns ink blue** (a filled blue circle) when that page has something written on it. Hold the mouse over a tab and it says *Something is written on this page* or *Nothing on this page yet*.
- **Turning pages:** click a tab, or use the links at the bottom of each page, which name the page before and after.
- **All pages**, at the end of the row, shows every page one after another, each with its number and name as a heading. Use it to read a whole record straight through, or to look for something when you don't know which page holds it.
- **Story Canon remembers** which page you were on for each record while the program is open.

**Other long screens use the same tabs.** Records are not the only notebooks. Where was I?, Settings, Novel setup, the Story Brain's **Questions and ideas**, **Choices ahead** and **Suggestions waiting**, several continuity checks in Narrative Zoom, and **Send something out** are split into numbered pages the same way, with **All pages** at the end of the row and links to the page before and after. When a page tab carries a number in brackets, such as **Questions (4)**, that number counts what waits on the page; each chapter of this guide says what it counts.

**Page numbers move, so look for the name.** A page with nothing to show is left out of the row, and the pages after it close up. Some pages hold only fields that come with a **method** (one teacher's way of building a story, such as Robert McKee's), and those pages appear only once you turn that method on for the book in Novel setup (the page where you tell Story Canon what kind of book this is; see [Your first hour: the Library and a new novel](#your-first-hour-the-library-and-a-new-novel)). For example, a character's **What opposes them** page holds only McKee's fields. In a book that doesn't use McKee, the page is left out, and every page after it has a number one lower. On top of that, when you open a record inside **Narrative Zoom**, it shows one or two extra pages in front of its own, about where it sits in the story, so its numbers move again. That is why this guide always sends you to a page of a character or a scene card by its name, such as "the **Arc** page", and never by its number. Screens that don't change shape, such as **Where was I?** and **Settings**, keep their numbers, and the guide uses them there.

**The common mistake** is thinking a record is empty because its first page is. Look at the numbers: a blue number means something is written on that page.

**Example.** Marisol's record has a blue number beside **Inner life** and beside **Arc**, and a plain one beside **Looks and voice**. You know where her wound is written, and you know nobody has described her yet.

## Getting help

Story Canon teaches in two places: in **Help**, which holds this whole guide, and right beside your work, in small folds you open when you want them. You never need to read anything before you start. Open the teaching when something puzzles you.

### Opening Help inside a novel

1. Press **F1** on your keyboard, from any view. Help opens at the start of the guide. (While a sprint, a timed stretch of writing, is running, **F1** does nothing, because a sprint keeps you on the page.)
2. Or click **Help**, the last of the small names in the list on the right.

To leave Help, click any other name in the list on the right, or the pink **Return to Writing** ribbon if you came from the page.

### Opening Help from the Library

You can read the guide before you have a novel at all.

1. In the Library, click **Help** at the top right of the window, on the purple edge, or press **F1**.
2. The whole guide opens over the shelf, with the same search and chapter list as inside a novel.
3. Click **Close Help**, or press **Esc**, to go back to the shelf.

**Example.** On your first day, before starting *The Keeper of Gull Point*, you click **Help** in the Library and read this chapter.

### What is on the Help screen

- **Search the guide** (the box at the top left). Type two or more letters, such as *beat* or *decided*. A list of matching sections appears under the box, each with the chapter it is in and a few words around the match. Click a section name to jump there. Clear the box to see the chapter list again.
- **The chapter list** (under the search box). Click a chapter to open it. The chapter you are reading is shown in bold, and its main sections are listed beneath it.
- **The chapter itself** (the large area on the right). Links inside the guide stay inside Help.

**The common mistake** is scrolling through chapters looking for a word. Type it in **Search the guide** instead.

### Teaching beside your work

These folds sit right where you need them, and none opens on its own.

| You want to know | Reach for | Where it is |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What one field is for | **Why it matters** | Under the field. It starts with how many books need the field (**Most books need this**, **Useful for many books** or **Only for some books**), then why it helps, **The common mistake** and an **Example**. **Less** folds it away. |
| How two similar fields differ | **Not the same as its neighbors.** | A short note under one of the two fields, such as *Goal* and *What they think they want* |
| What a kind of record is for | **About** fold, such as **About characters: what they are and how to use them** | At the top of a record's first page, and at the top of each list of records in the Story Brain. It is open the first time you meet each kind; click its heading to fold it, and it stays folded for that kind. |
| Every field of one kind, in one place | **Read the full chapter in Help** | At the end of every **About** fold. It opens Help at the matching part of [Every kind and every field](#every-kind-and-every-field), and the heading glows pink for a moment so you see where you landed. |
| What a continuity check is telling you | **How this view works** | At the top of each continuity check in Narrative Zoom. See [Catching problems: the continuity checks](#catching-problems-the-continuity-checks). |
| Anything else | **F1**, then **Search the guide** | Anywhere |

**Example.** You wonder whether Ana needs anything in *What they stand to lose*. You click **Why it matters** under the field and see **Most books need this**: without stakes, a reader has no reason to be afraid for her.

The last chapter of this guide, [Every kind and every field](#every-kind-and-every-field), gathers all the field teaching in one place, one section per kind, so you can read a kind straight through before you start one.
