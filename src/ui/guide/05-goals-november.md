# Goals, sprints and November

This chapter is about the number side of writing: how Story Canon counts your words each day, a daily goal for ordinary days, a **writing push** such as November and 50,000 words, how Story Canon works out what each day needs, what happens on the days you fall short, and how to plan a lighter day or a rest day without feeling you have failed. It also covers **sprints** (writing against a clock), **door closed** (no judging until a day you choose), and notes you dictate away from the desk. Read it when you want a goal, before a November push, or the first time the push line on Where was I? puzzles you.

All of it is optional. With no goal at all, Story Canon still shows how many words you wrote each day, and never judges a day.

## Goals: where your progress shows

Your progress appears in three places:

- **Where was I?**, your novel's home page, on its page **1 Today**: the **drafting progress panel**, with your push drawn as one pen line and a few figures under it. Above everything on that page is **your book map**, which shows where you are in the story itself. This is the main one, and most of this chapter is about it.
- **The Writing Room**, in the small line under the page: *1,204 words in this scene, today's goal 1,743*. Today's goal appears there only once you have a goal.
- **The Writing Room binder**, at the top: the whole book's word count, and "of" your target length if you set one.

With no goal and no push, the panel shows this month's figures and says *Set a daily word goal, or a writing push such as November, and this shows how you are tracking.*, with a **Settings** link.

## Goals: what counts as a day's words

Every number in this chapter starts from one idea: **a day's words are the words you typed that day, minus the words you cut.**

- Typing new words adds to the day.
- Cutting words takes away from it. A day of heavy cutting can come out below zero; point at that day on the push line and it says how many words were cut.
- **Notes for later**, `[[like this]]`, never count (see [Writing Room: notes for later](#writing-room-notes-for-later)).
- **Not writing, so never counted:** bringing in a manuscript, accepting changes an editor sent back, restoring an earlier version of a scene, and choosing **Keep the saved version** after a crash. The words are in your book (they count in **The whole book**), but none of them is you writing that day.
- **Days follow your computer's clock and time zone.** Words typed at 11:58 at night count for that day; words at 12:02 count for the next.
- **Words count when they become a saved version of the scene**: about twenty seconds after you stop typing, or when you leave the scene. If **Today** looks behind, wait a moment.

**Why it matters.** A tracker you can't trust is worse than none. Counting only what you typed keeps the numbers honest: a day with 3,000 imported words and 200 written ones shows 200.

## Goals: an everyday goal for ordinary days

**What it is.** An **everyday goal** is the daily target that applies on any day that isn't part of a writing push. Writers who use one usually choose a modest number of words per day, such as 500 or 1,000.

**Do you need it?** Only if a daily number helps you. If you only want a number for one stretch, such as November, use a writing push instead and leave the everyday goal at **No goals**.

**How to set one.**

1. Click **Settings** on the right edge of the window, then the page tab **2 Goals and focused efforts**.
2. In the section **Guidance and goals**, open the **Everyday goal** dropdown and choose **Words per day**.
3. In **Goal amount**, type your number, such as 500.
4. Click **Save settings** at the bottom of the window.

Where was I? now measures every day this month against 500. The top line says *Today, 74 words of your 500.*, and pointing at any day says whether you met it.

**The choices in the dropdown.** A gray line under the dropdown says what the one you picked does.

| Choice | What it does now |
| --- | --- |
| **No goals** | No daily number. Where was I? still shows what you wrote each day |
| **Words per day** | A number of words each day. Where was I? tracks it every day you write |
| **Scenes per week** | Kept as your goal in Settings. Where was I? counts words for now, so days are measured in words |
| **Minutes per day** | Kept as your goal in Settings. Where was I? counts words for now |
| **Milestones** | No daily number. A box, **My milestones**, holds the milestones you are working toward, one per line, such as *Act One drafted by March*. They are kept in Settings for you to read and tick off yourself |

**The common mistake** is setting the everyday goal to your best-day number. A goal you meet four days out of five builds a habit; one you miss four days out of five builds dread. Set the number you can reach on a tired Tuesday.

**Example.** *The Keeper of Gull Point* moves slowly through the autumn. You set **Words per day** to 300. Most days you write 400 or 500, and the top line says the day's goal is met. On the day you write 150, it says *150 words of your 300*, and the next day's goal is still just 300. An everyday goal never carries a shortfall forward; only a writing push does that.

## Goals: reading the drafting progress panel

The panel on Where was I? starts with a heading in slanted purple type: the month's name, or your push's name (or *Your writing push* if you left the name empty). Under it is a sentence about today. During a push it names today's share of the push, such as *Today's share of Fall Draft is 1,743 words. You've written 600.*; on an ordinary day with an everyday goal it reads *Today, 74 words of your 500.* During a push with a word goal comes **the push line**, and under it a few figures in plain numbers.

The panel is about your writing days and your push. Where you are in the story itself is a different measure, **your book map**, at the top of Where was I? (see [Where was I? Your book map](#where-was-i-your-book-map)). The two are kept apart on purpose.

### Goals: the push line

**What it is.** One hand-drawn pen line from the first word of your push on the left to its goal on the right, marked *goal, 50,000 words*. Words, and only words, move you along it: the words you have written in the push over the push's goal.

**Why it is separate from your book.** A push is a promise about words, not a place in the story. A 30-word push and a 50,000-word push are both pushes, a push can start in the middle of a book, and reaching its goal doesn't mean you reached the end of the story. So the push line never names a beat. Where you are in the story is shown on your book map, at the top of the page.

**What you see on it.**

| What you see | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| A solid purple line | Ink: the words of the push you have written |
| A faint dotted line | Pencil: the words still ahead |
| Small marks with *a quarter*, *halfway*, *three quarters* | A quarter, half and three quarters of the push's goal. Written in ink once you pass them, in pencil before |
| *goal, 50,000 words* at the end | The push's goal |
| The pink dot, with *you are here, day 12* | Where the push's words have taken you. Before a push begins it says where the push starts; after the goal it reads, for example, *52,100 words, 2,100 extra*, and the line runs on past the goal |
| A small tick under the line | Where one day's writing ended. Big days spread the ticks apart; small days bunch them up |
| A small wingback chair | A rest day you set. The chair is drawn straight on: a tall back, wings that flare out past rolled arms, a cushion and short legs |
| Nothing at all | A day with no words. Nothing moved, so nothing is marked, and nothing is held against you |

**A day that cuts words** never walks you backwards on the line. The line holds at the farthest point you have reached, and the figures underneath give the exact count.

**Why it matters.** A column chart answers "how many words did I write on the 9th?" The push line answers the question you carry around during a push: *how far into this push am I?* Seeing the halfway mark just ahead of your dot is a reason to sit down tonight.

**The common mistake** is reading the push line as your place in the story. Halfway through a push is not the Midpoint of your book. For the story, look at your book map.

**Example.** On day 12 of a 50,000-word push called *Fall Draft* you have written 19,600 words in the push. The purple line runs from the left edge past *a quarter* and stops at your pink dot 39% of the way along, with *halfway* waiting in pencil ahead. Behind the dot are nine small ticks for the days you wrote and a little wingback chair where you took your rest day on the 7th. The 4th has no tick: you didn't write, and the line simply goes on. (Fall Draft and its 50,000 words are only an example: a push can have any name, any length and any dates.)

### Goals: pointing at a day

Point at a day's tick or a wingback chair to see that day's exact story, for example:

- *November 3: 900 words of 1,661*
- *November 8: 2,400 words, goal met*
- *November 7: 0 words, a rest day you set*

A screen reader reads every day of the push this way, in order, whether or not the day has a mark.

### Goals: the figures under the push line

| Figure | What it says | When it shows |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **So far** (or **October so far**, without a push) | Words written in this push or this month, and "of" the push's goal | Except before a push begins |
| **To finish on time** | About how many words a day you need from here, and how many days are left, today included | During a push with a word goal |
| **Against the plan** | How far ahead or behind you are, counted to the end of yesterday | During a push, when you are 50 or more words ahead or behind |
| **Days in a row** | How many days in a row you have written | Except before a push begins |
| **The whole book** | Every word in the manuscript, and "of" your target length if you set one | When there is no push. During a push, your book's total is shown with your book map, at the top of the page |

Each is explained in full below. Under the figures, during a push, are **Set a lighter day** and the link to close the door.

## Goals: a writing push, such as November

**What it is.** A **writing push** is a stretch of days with a word goal. The classic is November and 50,000 words, the challenge many writers take on each year. You can make a push of any length, such as a two-week retreat or a spring quarter.

**Why it matters.** A push turns a big number into a small daily one, and then keeps that daily number honest as the days go by. If you fall short, it tells you exactly what each remaining day needs. If you get ahead, it lets you ease off.

**Do you need it?** Only for a stretch with a target. You can have an everyday goal and a push at once: the push's daily goal applies on the push's days, and the everyday goal the rest of the time.

### Goals: setting up a November push

1. Click **Settings** on the right edge of the window, then the page tab **2 Goals and focused efforts**.
2. Find the section **A writing push**. Under **Plan a writing push**, click **November, 50,000 words**. Story Canon fills in **Name it** *November*, **First day** November 1, **Last day** November 30, **Words to write in it** 50,000. (The other choices are **30 days from today**, which starts at 30,000 words, and **My own dates**.)
3. Change anything you like.
4. Read the line under the boxes: *That is about 1,667 words a day for 30 days.* That is your daily goal on the push's days only. The rest of the time, your everyday goal applies.
5. Click **Save settings** at the bottom of the window.

To remove the push, click **No push** in the same section, then **Save settings**.

### Goals: before the push starts

Where was I? starts showing the push **two weeks before its first day**, so you can see it coming. The heading says **November**, and the panel says *November starts on November 1. 50,000 words in 30 days is about 1,667 a day.* Under it: *Today, 74 words. The line below fills in once November begins.* Today's words still count toward your everyday goal, if you have one.

This is the time to set any lighter or rest days you already know about (see [Goals: lighter days and rest days](#goals-lighter-days-and-rest-days)) and to decide whether to close the door for the month (see [Goals: door closed](#goals-door-closed)).

### Goals: during the push

From November 1, the push line measures November's 50,000 words, and every figure is about the push. The sentence at the top changes with the day:

- *Today, 600 words of your 1,743.* while you are working toward today's goal.
- *Today, 1,800 words of your 1,743. The day's goal is met.* once you reach it. **To finish on time** then switches to what the days after today need.
- *Today is a rest day you set.* on a rest day, with *You wrote 412 words anyway, and they all count.* if you did.

### Goals: after the push ends

For a week after the last day, the panel shows the push's **recap** in place of the push line: the manuscript lying on the desk, *You wrote 51,204 words.* in the coach's pen, *That's this: 205 pages that did not exist on the first day of November.*, how many days you wrote and your biggest day, and the push's full shelf of books with its bookend. If no words went down in the push, it says so plainly and kindly, and nothing more. After that week, the panel goes back to the current month. The push keeps its shelf on page **4 The shelf** (see [Where was I? The shelf](#where-was-i-the-shelf)).

## Goals: the push coach

**What it is.** During a writing push, the **Today** page of Where was I? carries a coach: a short section above your drafting progress that helps you through the push, not just counts it. Tracking tells you where you are. The coach helps you keep going. It works for a push of any length, from a few days to a year.

**What it shows.**

- **The day,** with your push's own name: *November, day 9 of 30*, or before the push, *November starts in 5 days*.
- **A note for the day,** on a slip of paper in plum: its heading in the coach's handwriting, the same pen that writes the beats on your book map, and its sentences in Caslon italic. A heading and a few sentences, chosen for where you are in the push or for what just happened. Where a note teaches a craft idea, a line under it credits the teacher it comes from, such as *From Stephen King, On Writing.* The notes are written in Story Canon's own words; nothing is quoted.
- **A line about a writer, about writing, or just for fun.** *About a writer* tells how a real novelist worked or kept going. *About writing* is a true, encouraging fact about how books get made. *Just for fun* is a light, true fact for days your brain needs a rest, mostly about words and books. Every one comes from a checked source.
- **Where you are in the book.** The beat your next unwritten scene sits at, with one line from your beat sheet about what that beat asks.
- **A milestone** when you cross one: the first tenth of your goal, a quarter, halfway, three quarters, ninety percent, and the goal itself. Milestones are shares of the push, not of the book, so none of them names a beat: a push may be a whole short novel or one stretch of a long one. A milestone is written at the top of the coach's note, in the same pen. A milestone stays on the page the day after, so you don't miss it.
- **Your pace, in plain numbers.** Ahead, behind or on pace against an even share of the push, what today asks, and how much of it is still to go. If you have run sprints on this computer, it also says how many sprints that is at your own sprint pace. It never scolds: being behind is already spread over the days left.
- **Next milestone,** with how many words to go, in a small line under the day. How far along you are is drawn just below the coach, on the push line.
- **I'm stuck.** Click it and the coach hands you the next scene card you haven't written, in story order, with what the scene must do, its turn and its conflict if you filled them in, and one question to start on. **Write this scene** opens the page.
- **Turn off the coach,** if you'd rather work without it. You can turn it back on in Settings.

### Goals: how the coach chooses

**By phase, not by day number.** Every push moves through the same phases, however long it is: the first day, early momentum, the first slump when the excitement fades, the middle, rising pressure, the dark stretch, the home stretch, the day before the last, and the last day. The coach works out the phase from how far through the push you are, so a nine-day push and a hundred-day push both get a beginning, a middle and an end. In the two weeks before a push, the notes help you get ready; for a week after it, they help you rest and come back to the draft, beside a recap of your total, the days you wrote, and your biggest day.

**What happened comes first.** On some days, a note about what you just did takes the day's place: coming back after a day or more away, the day after your biggest day so far, a long run of days in a row, a rest day you set, being well behind, or being well ahead. The first and last days always keep their own notes.

**No repeats.** There are well over a hundred notes and more than a hundred facts. The coach remembers what it has shown you on this computer, across every push and every novel, and shows something new until it has shown everything of that kind. Only then does it come back around, starting with what you saw longest ago. The note and the line are chosen once a day, so the page says the same thing all day.

**Just for fun, on purpose.** The light line comes on rest days, the day after a big day, and every third day or so. The other days alternate between writers and writing.

**Your own notes.** On page **2 Goals and focused efforts** of Settings, under **Coach me through the push**, type notes of your own, one per line: a line that keeps you going, something a mentor told you, why this book matters. Click **Save my notes**. They mix in with the coach's notes on about one ordinary day in four, headed *In your own words*. They are kept on this computer and come with this novel to your other computer.

**Where your sprint pace comes from.** Each sprint you finish on a computer is remembered on that computer: how many minutes, and how many words. The coach divides one by the other. It's an estimate for planning, never a target.

**Why it matters.** A push is mostly won or lost in the middle, on ordinary days when nothing feels exciting. The notes are there for those days, and because they don't repeat, they stay worth reading. The pace line turns a vague worry ("I'm so far behind") into one number for today. And the stuck button removes the hardest question, "what do I write now?", by answering it from your own plan.

**How the coach talks.** Like a writing friend you trust: relaxed, honest, on your side. Some days that means encouragement, some days a piece of craft, and some days, when you have been slipping on a goal you set yourself, a straight word about it and a push to reset. It understands that life happens. It never questions your worth or your talent, only whether today got the effort.

**The common mistake** is treating the notes as rules. They are encouragement and craft reminders, not instructions. If a note doesn't fit your day, write anyway. And if the next scene the coach hands you isn't the one you want to write, skip ahead.

**Example.** It's day 8 of November, and yesterday you wrote nothing. The coach opens with a note about coming back after a day away, and under it, *About a writer*, a line about a novelist who wrote in the early mornings before a day job. The pace line reads *You are 2,100 words behind an even pace. That is already spread over the 23 days left, so today asks 1,758. At your sprint pace, about 640 words in 30 minutes, that is 3 sprints.* *Where you are in the book* says your next scene sits at **Bad Guys Close In**. You click **I'm stuck**. It offers *The ledger*, with the question *What does your viewpoint character expect to happen here, and what happens instead?* You click **Write this scene** and start a sprint.

## Goals: catch-up math, or how a short day is shared

**What it is.** During a push, each day's goal is **an even share of what is still left**. Story Canon takes the words still to write, sets aside the days you have set yourself, and divides the rest evenly over the days that remain, today included.

**What that means in practice.**

- **A short day spreads its difference over the rest of the month.** Each later day asks a few words more, instead of one day asking for a mountain.
- **A big day lowers every later day a little.** Getting ahead buys you slack.
- **Today's goal is worked out from what you wrote before today**, so it never moves while you are writing. Today's words only change tomorrow's goal.

**Worked example.** You are drafting *The Keeper of Gull Point* in November, 50,000 words, so the month starts at 1,667 a day.

| Day | Written | That day's goal | What happened next |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| November 1 | 1,800 | 1,667 | A bit ahead |
| November 2 | 1,700 | 1,663 | Still ahead |
| November 3 | 900 | 1,661 | A short day: 761 words under |
| November 4 | 2,100 | 1,689 | The 761 words were shared over the 27 days left, so each asked about 28 more |
| November 6 | 0 | 1,670 | A day with no words |
| November 7 | 1,500 | 1,740 | November 6's 1,670 words, shared over 24 days, added about 70 to each |

By the morning of November 13, you have written 18,630 words in twelve days. 31,370 are left, over 18 days counting today, so the panel says *Today, 0 words of your 1,743.* and **To finish on time** says *about 1,743 a day, 18 days left*.

**The common mistake** is trying to make up a short day the very next day. The math already spreads it out. Write today's number, not yesterday's and today's.

## Goals: lighter days and rest days

November has days when 1,667 words won't happen: a late shift, a birthday, Thanksgiving. Story Canon lets you plan for them ahead of time, so they are part of the plan instead of a hole in it.

**What it is.** A **set day** is a day in your push whose goal you choose yourself. A **lighter day** has a smaller number, such as 300. A **rest day** has zero. You can also set a **bigger day**, such as 3,000 for a free Saturday. On a set day, your number is that day's goal, and meeting it counts as meeting the day.

**Where it is.** On Where was I?, under the progress figures, the link **Set a lighter day**. It appears only with a push that has a word goal, from two weeks before it starts until its last day.

**How to set a day.**

1. Click **Set a lighter day**. A row opens with **Day**, **Words**, **Set it**, **Make it a rest day** and **Cancel**, and a gray line: *That day's goal becomes your number, and the days you have not set share the difference. Nothing is held against you.*
2. In **Day**, choose the date. You can choose any day from today (or from the push's first day, before it starts) to its last day.
3. Either type the words in **Words** (it starts at 500) and click **Set it**, or click **Make it a rest day**.

The day you set now appears in a list under the figures, such as *November 26: 300 words* or *November 27: rest day*, each with a **Clear** link that puts it back to an ordinary day. The list shows the set days from today on. Once a rest day you set has passed, the push line marks it with a small wingback chair; pointing at any day says whether it was a day you set. **To finish on time** adds *counting the days you set*.

**What happens to the difference.** The days you have not set share it. In the worked example, on November 13 you set November 26 to 300 and November 27 as a rest day. Those two days now carry 300 words instead of about 3,486, so the other 16 days share the rest: today's goal rises from 1,743 to 1,942.

**Why plan ahead.** Spread over sixteen days, the two lighter days cost about 200 words each. Left until the day itself, they would arrive as a sudden gap at the end of the month.

**The common mistake** is waiting until the bad day to admit it. If you know Thanksgiving will be a 300-word day, set it now. The other days absorb it a little at a time.

**Example.** The Port Ellery library has asked you to give a talk on November 14, and family visits on November 22 and 23. On November 1 you set the 14th to 500, and the 22nd and 23rd as rest days. Your daily goal for the other days rises from 1,667 to 1,834, and pointing at those days says they are days you set. On the 22nd, the panel says *Today is a rest day you set.*, your **Days in a row** stays unbroken, and afterwards a little wingback chair sits on the line where you rested.

### Goals: when set days leave words with nowhere to go

If you set so many days so low that the remaining words have no day left to be written in, **To finish on time** says so plainly: *Your set days leave 2,400 words with no day to write them. Raise a set day or clear one.* Click **Clear** beside a set day, or set it again with a bigger number.

If your set days already cover everything left, it says *Your set days cover what is left.* On a day you have not set, the top line then says *Your set days cover what is left, so today is yours to spend.*

## Goals: against the plan, ahead or behind

**What it is.** **Against the plan** compares what you have written with what an even pace would have given you, **up to the end of yesterday**. It says *1,374 behind, spread over the days ahead* or *620 ahead*.

**How it is counted.** The plan as it stood on the first day: the push's goal divided evenly over its days (1,667 a day for November). A past day you set counts as your number, so a rest day is never held against you; what it left undone is shared by the other days, so a real gap still shows. Days you set ahead of time never change this figure.

**When it shows.** Only during a push, only once you are 50 words or more ahead or behind, and not once the goal is reached.

**In the worked example:** twelve days at 1,667 is 20,004 words; you wrote 18,630; so *1,374 behind*. Today isn't counted yet, because today isn't over.

**How to use it.** As information, not a verdict. *Behind* doesn't mean you need one big day; **To finish on time** has already spread it over the days ahead. *Ahead* means you have earned a lighter day, if you want one.

## Goals: days in a row

**What it is.** **Days in a row** is how many days in a row, up to today, you have written at least one word.

- **Today is still open.** If you haven't written yet today, the count runs up to yesterday, and doesn't drop to zero until today ends with no words.
- **A rest day you set never breaks the run.** It doesn't add a day either; it just doesn't count against you.
- When there is no run, it says *Starts with your next words*.

**Why it matters.** A run of days is one of the most reliable ways to finish a draft. The number is there to protect the habit, which is why a planned rest day doesn't cost you the run.

**The common mistake** is writing ten words at 11:55 at night to keep the run alive, every night. If that is what keeps you coming back, fine. If it starts to feel like a chore, set a rest day instead.

## Goals: the whole book and your target length

**The whole book** is every word in your manuscript right now, in every scene that isn't deleted. Unlike the day counts, it includes words you brought in, restored or accepted from an editor. Notes for later aren't counted.

To see it as "of" a target, such as *52,310 of 80,000 words*:

1. Open **Settings** and click the page tab **1 This novel**.
2. In the first section, type a number in **Target length in words (optional)**.
3. Click **Save settings**.

The binder in the Writing Room shows the same total and target at its top.

**Why both numbers.** The push measures your writing days; the whole book measures the book. In November, the first matters more. In March, when you are deciding whether the book is too long, the second does.

## Goals: sprints

**What it is.** A **sprint** is a stretch of focused writing against a clock. While it runs, everything but the page goes away.

**Why it matters.** A deadline you can see makes starting easier: you only have to write until the clock stops, not finish the chapter. Taking everything else off the screen removes the small temptations, like checking the word count or renaming a scene.

**Do you need it?** No, but on a November day it is often the easiest way to get the number down: two 30-minute sprints are most of a day's goal for many writers.

### Goals: starting a sprint

1. In the Writing Room, click **Sprint** above the page. A box opens, headed **Sprint**.
2. Choose **15 minutes**, **30 minutes** or **60 minutes**, or type a number from 1 to 240 in **Other**.
3. Leave **Fill the whole screen** ticked for the window to fill the screen, or untick it to keep the window as it is.
4. Click **Start the sprint** (or press **Enter** in the Other box). **Cancel** or **Esc** closes the box.

The cursor goes straight to the end of the page, so the first words you type land in the scene. Story Canon remembers your length and screen choice for next time. If no scene was open, the sprint opens the one you would get from **The Writing Room** tab.

### Goals: during a sprint

Gone: the binder, the scene plan, the links above the page, the tabs, the story strip across the top, the header (with the Saved indicator and the Capture button), most notices, the word line under the page, and red spelling underlines. Keys that lead away from the page (**Ctrl+K** for Search, **Ctrl+Shift+W** for Return to Writing, **F1** for Help) do nothing until the sprint ends.

Still there:

- **A small, faint clock** in the bottom right corner, in slanted type: *30 min*, then *Under a minute*. Click it to show **Pause** and **End the sprint**. While paused, it says *Paused* and the time left; click it and choose **Resume**.
- **Ctrl+Shift+N** still opens Capture, for a thought that will not wait.
- **A save problem** is the one thing that breaks the quiet: if your writing can't be saved, a warning appears beside the clock.
- **A notice about newer work from your other computer**, if you write on more than one, because ignoring it would cost you later.

### Goals: when the time is up

Nothing stops you mid-sentence. A note appears at the bottom: **Time.** *(N) words in (N) minutes. Finish your sentence; nothing has stopped.* Then choose:

- **Keep going**: the page stays quiet, with no clock. The corner says *Still writing*; click it and choose **Done** when you finish, and a last note gives your count for the whole sprint.
- **Another sprint**: opens the sprint box again.
- **Done**: ends the sprint and brings everything back.

If you end a sprint early with **End the sprint**, the note says **Sprint over.** with your count, and offers **Another sprint** and **Done**.

**How the count works.** The count is every word you typed during the sprint, across every scene, minus what you cut, saved first so your last words count. If you cut more than you wrote, it says *You cut (N) words this sprint. That is writing too.* If you wrote nothing, it says *Time with the page counts too.* Sprint words count toward your day like any others.

**The common mistake** is choosing 60 minutes on a tired day and feeling beaten at minute 20. On a hard day, choose 15. A small sprint you finish beats a big one you abandon.

**Example.** It is November 8 and your goal is 1,750. After supper you click **Sprint**, choose **30 minutes**, and click **Start the sprint**. Marisol rows out to the reef where the boat was found. The clock says *Under a minute*, then the note says *Time. 912 words in 30 minutes.* You click **Another sprint**, and the second one takes you past the day's goal.

**Focus or a sprint?** **Focus** in the Writing Room hides everything too, but has no clock and no end (see [Writing Room: Focus](#writing-room-focus)). A sprint has a clock and a count at the end.

## Goals: door closed

**What it is.** **Door closed** is a setting that stops Story Canon from judging your draft until a day you choose. The phrase is Stephen King's: the first draft is written with the door closed, for you alone, and the door opens for revision.

**What waits while the door is closed:**

- No red spelling underlines on the page.
- No echoes, watch words or long sentences in **Read it back** (reading aloud still works).
- No story review or misspelled names in Narrative Zoom.
- The collaborator stays quiet about problems unless you ask.
- The *Ctrl+Shift+L leaves a note for later* reminder under the page is hidden.

Your plans, notes, search and reading aloud all stay. Anything that waits shows a **Look anyway** link, so you can still check something on purpose.

**Why pair it with a push.** A push is about getting words down. Judging them is a different kind of attention, and it pulls you into fixing. Closing the door for the push keeps the two apart.

**How to close it from Where was I?.**

1. Under your push's figures, find the link **Close the door through November 30** (it always names your push's last day).
2. Click it.

The link is replaced by *The door is closed through November 30. Nothing judges the draft until then.* It takes effect at once; there is nothing to save.

**How to close it from Settings, with or without a push.**

1. Open **Settings**, click the page tab **2 Goals and focused efforts**, and find the section **Door closed**.
2. In **Keep it closed through**, choose a day. It suggests your push's last day, or a day thirty days from now.
3. Click **Close the door**.

**To open it early, or choose another day,** open the same section. It says *The door is closed through November 30.* with **Change the day** (choose a date, then **Save**) and **Open the door now**. These take effect at once too, without **Save settings**. The door opens by itself the day after the date you chose.

**The common mistake** is opening the door "just to fix one typo" on day four. That one typo leads to rereading Chapter 1. Use **Look anyway** for the single thing you need, and leave the door closed.

## Goals: notes from the road

November doesn't only happen at the desk. If you write on more than one computer and have turned on sharing (see [Settings and your other computers](#settings-and-your-other-computers)), each novel gets its own notes file in your shared folder. You can dictate into it from your phone, in Word, one note to a paragraph.

When Story Canon has the novel open, each new paragraph arrives as a captured thought, waiting under **Thoughts waiting to be sorted** on Where was I? (page **2 Still open**), usually within a minute of the file syncing, and a message says *A note from the road came in.* During a sprint that message waits, so the quiet isn't broken; the note still arrives.

**How it fits a push.** A note from the road is a thought, not manuscript, so it never counts toward your day's words. Use it for the line about the fog over Port Ellery that comes to you in the car, then write it into the scene when you sit down. Where to find the file and how to dictate into it are in [Settings and your other computers](#settings-and-your-other-computers).

## Goals: a November, day by day

Here is how the pieces fit together over one month of *The Keeper of Gull Point*.

**October 18.** You open **Settings**, click **November, 50,000 words** under **Plan a writing push**, keep November and 50,000, and click **Save settings**. On Where was I?, a **November** panel appears: *November starts on November 1. 50,000 words in 30 days is about 1,667 a day.* You know Thanksgiving week will be hard, so you click **Set a lighter day** and set November 26 to 300 and November 27 as a rest day. You click **Close the door through November 30**.

**November 1.** You open the book, read **Next** on Where was I?, and click **Write**. You do two 30-minute sprints: Marisol finds the empty boat on her rocks. 1,800 words. The ink on the push line starts to run.

**November 6.** You are down with a cold and write nothing. No tick appears for the day. You don't panic: tomorrow's goal is about 70 words higher, that's all.

**November 13.** The panel says you're 1,374 behind. You read **To finish on time** instead, *about 1,942 a day*, and write that. Ana Ruiz knocks on the lighthouse door for the first time. **Days in a row** says 7.

**November 27.** Rest day. The panel says *Today is a rest day you set.* You walk the harbor wall instead, and dictate one line about Cyrus's boots into your notes from the road.

**November 30.** You write 2,300 words and **To finish on time** says **Done. Everything now is extra.** For a week, the panel shows *November is done*, with your pink dot at the end of the push line, past *goal, 50,000 words*. On December 1 the door opens by itself, and Read it back's echoes and long sentences are waiting for revision.

## Goals: when the numbers look wrong but are working

| What you see | Why |
| --- | --- |
| **Today** is behind what the page says | Words count when they become a saved version, about twenty seconds after you stop typing. Pause or leave the scene |
| Today's goal didn't drop while you wrote | Today's goal is fixed from what you wrote before today. Today's words change tomorrow's goal |
| The goal changed from yesterday | That is catch-up math: a short day raises the days after it a little, a big day lowers them |
| A day with lots of new words shows few | Brought-in, restored or accepted words aren't counted as writing. Cutting words subtracts |
| The panel shows October, though you set up November | The push appears two weeks before it starts. Before that, you see the current month |
| The November panel disappeared in December | It shows for a week after the push ends, then returns to the current month |
| **So far** and **Days in a row** aren't there | The push hasn't begun yet. They return on its first day |
| **Against the plan** isn't there | It shows only during a push, when you are 50 or more words ahead or behind |
| **Set a lighter day** isn't there | It appears only with a writing push that has a word goal, until the push ends |
| A day you set has gone from the list | The list shows set days from today on. Past set days still count, on the push line and in Against the plan |
| Days in a row didn't reset after a missed day | The missed day was a rest day you set, or today isn't over yet |
| Scenes per week or Minutes per day shows no goal line | Those goals are kept in Settings, but Where was I? measures words for now |
| No word line or goal under the page | A sprint is running. It comes back when the sprint ends |
| No goal on the word line on a rest day | A rest day's goal is zero, so there is nothing to show |
