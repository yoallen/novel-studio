# Story structure: beats, scenes and the Board

This chapter covers the plotting side of Story Canon: beat sheets, beats, scene cards, the Board, and how a plan becomes a page in the Writing Room. Almost all of it lives in one room, **Narrative Zoom**, the fourth tab on the right edge of the window.

You don't have to plot to use Story Canon. If you find your story as you write, you can skip this chapter and come back when you want to see the shape of what you've written. Nothing here is required. Frameworks are lenses you pick up, not rules you must obey.

## Beat sheets: what they are and why writers use them

A **beat** is a turning point: a place where the story has to change direction. The moment the hero's ordinary life breaks open is a beat. So is the point halfway through where everything flips, and the low point near the end where all seems lost.

A **beat sheet** is a list of those turning points in order, with a rough idea of where each one falls in the book (for example, "about 10% of the way in"). An **act** is a large section of the story. Most beat sheets group their beats into three acts: the beginning, the middle and the end.

**Why it helps.** A beat sheet gives a long book a skeleton. Middles sag when nothing turns. Endings feel unearned when nothing set them up. A beat sheet tells you where the turns usually go, so you can check that each one is really there.

**The common mistake.** Treating a beat sheet as a form to fill with events. A beat is a *turn*, not a slot. If nothing is different for your hero once the beat is over, the beat isn't there, however much happens on the page.

**Example.** A night-ferry captain named Marisol runs the same crossing every night. At the Catalyst (the beat that kicks the story into motion), the company tells her the route will close in thirty days. That's a turn: she can't go back to the routine. If the company only sends a memo about new uniforms, something happened, but nothing turned.

### Save the Cat, for screenplays and for novels

The beat sheet Story Canon knows most deeply is **Save the Cat**. Blake Snyder created it for screenwriters in his book *Save the Cat!* Jessica Brody adapted it for novelists in *Save the Cat! Writes a Novel*. It has fifteen beats:

| Act | Beats |
| --- | --- |
| Act One | 1. Opening Image, 2. Theme Stated, 3. Setup, 4. Catalyst, 5. Debate |
| Act Two, first half | 6. Break into Two, 7. B Story, 8. Fun and Games |
| Act Two, second half | 9. Midpoint, 10. Bad Guys Close In, 11. All Is Lost, 12. Dark Night of the Soul |
| Act Three | 13. Break into Three, 14. Finale, 15. Final Image |

Some beats also have smaller steps inside them. Setup holds **Stasis = Death**, and the Finale holds five steps. You'll meet them on the beat pages and the Board. See [Smaller steps inside a beat](#smaller-steps-inside-a-beat-stasis-death-and-the-finale).

The descriptions and examples you see on each beat come from **your beat sheet**: the Save the Cat material you brought into the program. Story Canon shows them exactly as your sheet has them, spelling included, and credits each example to the book it comes from.

### The other beat sheets Story Canon offers

When a novel has no beat sheet yet, you can choose one of these:

| Beat sheet | Whose it is | Its shape |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Save the Cat (Brody for novels)** | Blake Snyder, adapted by Jessica Brody | Fifteen beats, placed by percentage of the book |
| **The Hero's Journey** | Christopher Vogler's telling of Joseph Campbell | Twelve stages of departure, ordeal and return |
| **Story Circle** | Dan Harmon | Eight steps: go somewhere unfamiliar, pay a price, come back changed |
| **Seven-Point Structure** | Dan Wells | Decide the ending first, then the opposite beginning, then the turns between |
| **Three-Act Structure** | The classic shape | Setup, confrontation and resolution, with turning points between |

Every one of them works with the Board, the beat pages and the checks in this chapter. A few things are Save the Cat only: the three worlds, the "Save the Cat's questions for this beat" fields, and the two placement models in **Where beats fall**.

### Your own method as a beat sheet

If you learned structure from a teacher or book that isn't on that list, you can build it yourself.

1. Click **Settings** on the right edge. Under **My methods**, click **Open my methods**. (You can also get there from **Novel setup**, with **Make a method of my own**.)
2. Click **Make a method of my own** and give it a **Name**.
3. Under **Its beats**, add each beat: its name, where it starts and ends (**at** and **to**, as percentages of the book), which act it belongs to, and a short description of what the beat is.
4. **Lines up with…** is optional. It tells Story Canon which moment in other beat sheets this beat matches (for example, your "Point of No Return" lines up with Break into Two). That lets the program offer your placed scenes on the right beats if you ever switch sheets.
5. Click **Save this method**.

A method with beats appears in the beat sheet list the next time you choose one.

**The common mistake.** Copying a whole published beat sheet word for word. Write each beat's description in your own words: what it does and how you'll know it's working. That's the version that will actually help you at 2 a.m.

### Choosing a beat sheet for a novel

**Where:** **Narrative Zoom** > **Zoom** tab, on a novel that has no beat sheet yet. The page says "This novel has no beat sheet. Frameworks are lenses you choose, never requirements. Your story works without one."

1. Open the dropdown beside that message. It lists the five built-in sheets, then any of your own methods that have beats.
2. Click **Use this beat sheet**.
3. The beats appear at once: on the Zoom page, on the Board, and on the story strip at the top of the window.

You can change it later in **Novel setup**. If more than one beat sheet is switched on there, the line **The beat sheet that drives the board** picks the one the Board uses. Tick **Offer my placed scenes on the matching beats** before you switch, and your scene cards are offered on the matching beats of the new sheet as "might go here", waiting for you to decide. Nothing is decided for you.

## Narrative Zoom and its four tabs

**Narrative Zoom** lets you look at your book from any distance, like zooming a map: the whole story, one act, one beat, one scene card, and then the page itself.

Across the top of the room are two rows of tabs.

- **The top row** holds the four views of your structure: **Zoom**, **Board**, **Where beats fall** and **Alternate structures**. The current one looks like a raised tab joined to the page.
- **The second row**, labeled *Continuity checks*, holds the checks: **Timelines**, **Viewpoint**, **Who knows what** and the rest. The current one is a filled purple pill. They have their own chapter: [Catching problems: the continuity checks](#catching-problems-the-continuity-checks).
- **I'm stuck**, at the right end of the top row, is a small rescue for blank days. See [I'm stuck: one beat, one sentence](#im-stuck-one-beat-one-sentence).

| Tab | What it's for |
| --- | --- |
| **Zoom** | Walk down from the whole book to one act, one beat, one scene card, and back |
| **Board** | Every beat at once, like a corkboard, with your scene cards pinned under them |
| **Where beats fall** | The numbers: where each beat lands in a book of your length |
| **Alternate structures** | Saved "what if" versions of your structure, to compare and adopt or let go |

Narrative Zoom remembers the tab and the page you were on, so when you come back you land where you left.

### Moving around in Zoom: the breadcrumb and Back

Once you're below the top level, a line of links appears above the page title, such as **I Declare War › Act One › Setup**. This is the **breadcrumb**: the path from the whole book down to where you are. Click any part of it to go back up to that level.

Sometimes a second link appears at the right end of that line: **Back to Setup, where you came from**. It takes you back to the last Zoom page you looked at, when that page isn't already in the breadcrumb. It remembers Zoom pages only, so if you opened a card from the Board, it may name the Zoom page you visited before you went to the Board. That's expected.

## The book overview: acts and the three worlds

**Where:** **Narrative Zoom** > **Zoom**, at the top level (the page titled with your book's name).

This page shows your whole book in four parts:

- **Act One**
- **Act Two, first half**
- **Act Two, second half**
- **Act Three**

Act Two is split in half because the Midpoint divides it: the first half builds the new situation, and the second half makes it fall apart. Beside each act, a small gray line counts what's in it, such as "4 beats, 0 scenes decided, 6 might go here". Click an act's name to open it.

"0 scenes decided" is not a sign that something is missing from your plan. It only means none of your cards in that act is marked **It goes here** yet. Cards marked **It might go here** are counted separately ("6 might go here"). See [Decided or might go here](#decided-or-might-go-here-two-kinds-of-placement).

### The three worlds: Thesis, Antithesis and Synthesis

On a Save the Cat novel, three purple-edged bands sit above the acts:

- **Thesis World**, above Act One
- **Antithesis World**, above Act Two (one band covers both halves)
- **Synthesis World**, above Act Three

Each band gives a one-line meaning. A fold-out under it, **Read the full description from your sheet**, opens the longer description from your own beat sheet. At the bottom of the page, a section called **The three worlds** sums them up.

**What they mean.** Snyder borrowed the words from an old way of arguing: a statement (thesis), its opposite (antithesis), and a new idea that combines them (synthesis). In a story, the three acts are three states of being:

- **Thesis:** the hero's world as it is before the story starts. Who she is and what needs fixing.
- **Antithesis:** the upside-down world she's thrown into at Break into Two. Everything she knew is reversed or tested.
- **Synthesis:** the new self she becomes in Act Three, made from the old world and the upside-down one.

**How they relate to the acts.** The worlds and the acts line up, but they're different ideas. The acts are *where* you are in the book. The worlds are *who the hero is* while you're there. The world changes at the act breaks: Break into Two carries her from Thesis into Antithesis, and Break into Three carries her from Antithesis into Synthesis. That's why those two beats need to be definite. A hero who drifts into Act Two never really left home.

**Why it helps.** When a scene in Act Two feels flat, ask: is this happening in the upside-down world? If the hero is still living her Act One life in the middle of the book, the act break didn't take.

**Example.** Marisol's Thesis World is the night ferry: the same faces, the same crossing, a life that works but is going nowhere. Her Antithesis World begins when she takes the ferry somewhere it was never meant to go, to fight the company's closure. Her Synthesis World is a new crossing she runs on her own terms, using the stubbornness of her old life and the nerve she found in the new one.

**The common mistake.** Treating Synthesis as "back to normal". The hero doesn't go home unchanged. She brings something from both worlds.

The bands appear only for Save the Cat. Other beat sheets show the four acts without them.

### Scenes not on a beat yet

At the bottom of the book overview, **Scenes not on a beat yet** lists the scene cards that have no spot at all: not decided anywhere, and not "might go" anywhere either. Click one to open it, then use **Move…** to place it.

If some cards only *might* go on a beat, the note above the list says how many and that they "show in pencil under their act". They aren't missing; open an act to see them.

## An act page: the beats in one act

**Where:** **Narrative Zoom** > **Zoom** > click an act.

An act page lists the act's beats in order, each with a link and where it falls, such as **3. Setup** "1% to 10%". A moment that falls inside a longer beat says so: **2. Theme Stated** "5%, during Setup".

Under each beat you may see scene cards:

- A card in solid type is **decided**: it goes on this beat.
- A card in pencil (gray with a dashed underline), followed by "might go here", is a **candidate**: it might go on this beat.

Click a beat to open its page, or a card to open the card.

**When to use it.** When you want to work through one part of the book without the noise of the rest. Act Two is where most books lose their way, and its two halves are a good size for one sitting.

## A beat page, from top to bottom

**Where:** **Narrative Zoom** > **Zoom** > an act > a beat. You can also click a beat's name on the Board or in **Where beats fall**.

A beat page has two halves. The top half teaches the beat and shows which scenes sit on it. The bottom half, **Your plan for this beat**, holds your own decisions about what the beat does in *your* book.

### The top of a beat page: where it falls and what it does

- **The title**, such as **Setup**.
- **Where it falls**, in one line: "At 1% to 10% of the book." If you've set a target length (see [Where beats fall](#where-beats-fall-the-numbers-for-your-length)), it adds the words: "around word 900 to 9,000 of 90,000". Once you've written scenes on this beat, it adds where the beat actually starts in your draft so far.
- **What this beat really does**, a fold-out that opens by default. Click the small triangle to fold it away. Inside:
  - your beat sheet's description of the beat,
  - **Where it goes**: your sheet's advice on placement,
  - **Scene type**: how much space your sheet says this beat usually takes,
  - **A question to ask**: a test for whether the beat is working,
  - **An example from your beat sheet**, in slanted type with the ❦ mark and the source credited (for example, "from *The Shining* by Stephen King"). Examples are never part of your story. They show the move so you can find your own version.

On Stasis = Death, this fold-out also points you to the Setup beat, where the "six things that need fixing" have their own list.

### Inside this beat

If the beat has smaller steps inside it, they're listed under **Inside this beat**. Click one to open its own page. See [Smaller steps inside a beat](#smaller-steps-inside-a-beat-stasis-death-and-the-finale).

### Scenes here: placing cards on a beat

**Scenes here** shows the cards decided for this beat, each with a small circle: hollow if it has no writing yet, filled if its scene has words. If none are decided, it says "No scenes placed here yet."

**Could go here** (if it appears) lists the cards that might go on this beat.

Below the lists are two forms.

**To place a card you already have:**

1. Open **Choose a card you already have…**. Cards with no spot are grouped first, under *Not on a beat yet*. Cards already decided somewhere else follow, under *On another beat*.
2. In the next dropdown, choose **It goes here** or **It might go here**.
3. Click **Place it here**.

**To make a new card right on this beat:**

1. Type a few words in **Or a new scene, in a few words**, such as "Marisol reads the closure notice".
2. Choose **It goes here** or **It might go here**.
3. Click **Add scene card**. The new card appears in the list. Click it to fill in its plan.

### Your plan for this beat: the bar across the top

**Your plan for this beat** starts with a few tools you'll see on every record in Story Canon:

- **Kept for you**: a dropdown that marks this beat as yours alone (for example, **Mine to write**). The collaborator, the optional AI writing partner, may talk about a marked beat but never writes or decides it for you. "Not marked" means no mark.
- **Ask about this**: opens a conversation with the collaborator about this beat.
- **The status legend**, one line of samples showing how each kind of entry looks: **Decided (inked)**, **Developing (pencil)**, **Still open (a blank)**, **Example, never canon** and **Suggestion (a slip, tucked in)**.
- **On this page**: links that jump to each section below.
- **About beats: what they are and how to use them**, a fold-out with a short lesson and **Read the full chapter in Help**, which opens [Beats: every field](#beats-every-field).

### In this book: the four fields that look alike

The **In this book** group has seven fields. The first four confuse almost everyone at first, because they all seem to ask "what happens here?" They don't. Each holds a different kind of thinking, and keeping them apart is what lets you change your mind about the event without losing track of what the event has to do.

| Field | What it holds | Status it usually carries |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **What this beat does in this book** | The *job*: what must be different once this beat is over | Decide this early |
| **What it is (your plan)** | The *event*: what actually happens on the page | Pencil until you're sure |
| **How it could happen** | The *options*: several ways the event could be staged, side by side | Pencil, several at once |
| **One way it could be done** | An *illustration*: how a beat like this could be done, from another book or a made-up case | Always "Example, never canon" |

Here's the same beat filled in all four ways. Marisol's Catalyst:

- **What this beat does in this book:** "Marisol's routine ends. From here she must choose between the company and the island."
- **What it is (your plan):** "The company posts a notice: the night route closes in thirty days."
- **How it could happen:** three entries. "She finds the notice taped inside the wheelhouse." "A passenger reads it aloud from his phone, laughing." "The harbor master tells her, and she realizes he's known for weeks."
- **One way it could be done:** "In a heist story, the safe-cracker's last job is cancelled by text while she's already inside the vault. The move: the news arrives at the worst possible moment."

Notice what happens if you change the event. Say the closure notice becomes a fire that wrecks the ferry. The job hasn't changed: her routine still ends, and she still has to choose. That's why the job is decided first and the event stays in pencil.

**How to fill one in:**

1. Click **Add** at the right end of the field.
2. Type your entry in the box that opens.
3. Choose its status in the dropdown: **Decided**, **Developing**, **Still open**, or **One way it could be done** (an example).
4. Click **Save**. A Developing entry "sits beside any other possibilities". A Decided one replaces the earlier decision, which stays in history.

The field names and teaching are in the program too: click **Why it matters** under any field. For the full lesson on each, see [Beats: In this book](#beats-in-this-book).

**The common mistake.** Putting your favorite event in **What this beat does in this book**. "The ferry burns" is an event. "She loses the one thing that kept her from choosing" is a job. Write the job, then test every event against it.

### Disaster, discovery or win

This field labels the turn. A **disaster** leaves the hero worse off against her goal. A **discovery** means she learns something that changes the picture. A **win** means she gets what she was after, for now. Write the one word, or a short sentence if the turn is mixed.

**Why it helps.** Stories move by alternating these. A run of five wins drains tension. A run of five disasters numbs the reader. Labeled beats show you the rhythm at a glance.

The field appears on every beat because every beat turns somehow, but it matters most on the big ones: Catalyst, Break into Two, Midpoint, All Is Lost, Break into Three. Scene cards have the same field. If a card decided on Break into Two, the Midpoint or All Is Lost is marked "win" (decided), the Story review asks whether you meant it: "A disaster is a reversal, never a win. Intended?"

**The common mistake.** Calling a discovery a win. Marisol learning who sold the route isn't the same as stopping the sale.

### Caused by the hero's own efforts?

A check: does this turn happen because of what the hero chose and did? There's no yes or no switch. Click **Add** and write your answer in a few words, such as "Yes: she steers into the storm herself" or "No, and that's fine here: the Catalyst happens to her."

A **passive hero** is one that things happen to: luck, a rescue, or another character drives the turn while she waits. Readers stop caring about a hero who isn't the reason anything changes.

**When it matters.** Most at Break into Two, Break into Three and the Finale, where she has to act. The Catalyst often happens *to* the hero, and that's normal.

**Example.** Lands: Marisol takes the ferry out against orders to reach the stranded islanders. Fails: the coast guard rescues everyone while she watches from the dock.

### Scene type: how much room the beat takes

**Scene type** appears twice on a beat page, and that's on purpose. In the teaching fold-out at the top, it's what your *beat sheet* says this beat usually takes. In the **In this book** group, it's your decision for *your* book.

The three sizes:

- **Single Scene or Single Chapter:** one contained moment.
- **Single Beat:** a quick turn inside a larger scene, sometimes one line. Theme Stated is often a Single Beat: one remark at a dinner table.
- **Multi Scene or Multi Chapters:** a long stretch. Fun and Games can fill a quarter of the book.

Choosing a size changes nothing automatically. It's a note to yourself about pacing: a long stretch can't be rushed into a paragraph, and a quick turn padded into a chapter sags.

### Save the Cat's questions for this beat

Below **In this book**, some beats carry a second group called **Save the Cat's questions for this beat**. These are the extra questions Snyder and Brody ask at particular beats. They belong to the beat sheet, not to the **Board** tab. (The **On this page** links call this group "On the board", its older name. It's the same group.)

Only the beats that need them carry them:

| Beat | Its questions |
| --- | --- |
| Theme Stated | **The line that states the theme** |
| Setup | **Home**, **Work**, **Play**, **Six things that need fixing** |
| Debate | **The Debate question** |
| Break into Two | **The hero's WANT** |
| Midpoint | **False victory or false defeat** |
| All Is Lost | **False victory or false defeat**, **Whiff of death** |
| Break into Three | **The hero's NEED** |
| Final Image | **Hero is changed, or rejects change** |

Most have their own **Why it matters** teaching, and the full lessons are in [Beats: On the board](#beats-on-the-board).

Two of these work as a pair. Save the Cat treats the Midpoint and All Is Lost as mirror images: a false victory at the Midpoint sets up a real low at All Is Lost, and a false defeat sets up the reverse. If you decide the same answer for **False victory or false defeat** on both beats, the Story review asks: "Your Midpoint and All Is Lost are both … Intended?"

### Six things that need fixing, on Setup

In the Setup, Save the Cat asks you to show the problems in the hero's life that the story will later fix or pay off. Brody calls them the "things that need fixing"; Snyder suggests about six. They're often small and specific: a habit, a grudge, a broken window nobody repairs.

**Where:** the **Setup** beat page, in **Save the Cat's questions for this beat**, the field **Six things that need fixing**.

1. Click **Add**.
2. Write one problem, such as "She hasn't spoken to her brother since the funeral."
3. Click **Save**.
4. Click **Add** again for the next one. Each problem is its own entry, so you can decide, pencil or set aside each one separately.

**Why it helps.** Each item is a promise. Later, when you reach the Finale and the Final Image, this list tells you what the ending owes the reader.

**The common mistake.** Listing problems that never come back. If the broken window is never mended (or deliberately left broken), cut it from the list, or give it a payoff.

**Example.** Marisol's list: she eats dinner standing at the wheel; she lies to her daughter about money; she's never once taken a day off; the ferry's horn is broken and she won't report it; she won't learn the new navigation system; she hasn't sung since her husband died. By the Final Image, the horn works and she's singing it.

Stasis = Death doesn't have its own list. Its page sends you back to Setup, because the problems are planted across the whole Setup.

### From my planning, Notes and your own fields

- **From my planning** holds fields that came in with planning you imported, such as **Planning notes (your field)**. "Your field" means it's a field you (or your import) added, not one Story Canon ships with.
- **Notes** is a free box for anything that doesn't fit a field. It saves when you click away.
- **Add your own field** makes a new blank. Type its name (for example, "Weather"), then choose **Just this beat** to add it here only, or **Every beat** to add it to all of them. Tick **A list, with several entries** if it should hold several separate items, like the six things that need fixing. Click **Add field**.

**The common mistake.** Choosing **Every beat** for something only one beat needs. It will then sit, empty, on fifteen pages. **Just this beat** is the right choice for most one-off ideas.

### Connections on a beat

**Connections** ties this beat to other records: a character, a clue, a scene card. Under **Make a connection**, choose a verb (such as **foreshadows** or **plants**), choose what it connects to, and click **Connect**. The line under the form reads the connection back to you as a sentence ("Reads: Setup plants …"), so you can check the direction before you save.

### Remove this beat from the novel

At the very bottom: **Remove this beat from the novel**, with the note "Only if this book does not use it. Your scene cards stay."

Use it when your book deliberately skips a beat (some books have no separate B Story, for example).

1. Click **Remove this beat from the novel**.
2. Read the question that appears. It explains that your notes on the beat go to **Deleted**, where you can restore them, and that cards placed on it go back to "Not on a beat yet".
3. Click **OK** to remove it, or **Cancel** to keep it.

Nothing on the beat is lost. Your cards keep all their plans; they just lose their spot.

## Smaller steps inside a beat: Stasis = Death and the Finale

Some Save the Cat beats have **sub-beats**: smaller steps inside a numbered beat. They have no number of their own. You'll see them:

- on the parent beat's page, under **Inside this beat**,
- on the **Board**, inside the parent beat's box, in slanted type with a line beside them,
- in the **Move…** dialog, indented, as "Stasis = Death (a step inside Setup)",
- in **Where beats fall**, indented under their parent.

You can place cards on a step exactly as on a beat.

### Stasis = Death

A step inside **Setup**, just before the Catalyst (about 9.5% of the book). It's the moment the reader sees that if nothing changes, the hero's life will slowly go wrong. Staying put is no longer safe.

**Example.** Marisol's Stasis = Death: the old engine fails mid-crossing for the third time this month, and she realizes she's been patching it with her own pay.

### The five Finale steps

Brody breaks the Finale into five steps. Story Canon shows them inside the **14. Finale** box. Their pages have no description from your sheet and no percentage ("not set" in **Where beats fall**), because they share the Finale's whole stretch. Here's what each one does:

1. **Gathering the Team:** the hero gathers people, tools and nerve. She may have to repair relationships she damaged in Act Two.
2. **Storming the Castle:** the plan goes into action. (Brody calls this "Executing the Plan".)
3. **Hightower Surprise:** a twist. The plan fails, or the situation turns out to be worse than anyone knew.
4. **Dig Deep Down:** with the plan gone, the hero has only what she's learned. She finds the truth she resisted all book.
5. **Executing the New Plan:** she acts from that truth and wins (or, in a tragedy, chooses the old way and loses).

**Example.** Marisol gathers the islanders who stopped speaking to her (Gathering the Team). They sail to the company hearing (Storming the Castle). The hearing was moved to the mainland that morning (Hightower Surprise). She admits she can't do this alone and asks her daughter to speak (Dig Deep Down). Her daughter's testimony, not her own speech, saves the route (Executing the New Plan).

## Scene cards: the plan for one scene

A **scene card** is your plan for one scene, like the index cards novelists pin to a corkboard. It holds what the scene must do, whose goal drives it, what stands in the way, and how it turns. A **scene**, in the Writing Room, is the written page. A card can exist long before its scene does, and most of your plotting happens on cards.

**Where:** click any card's name, on the Board, a beat page, an act page or a continuity check. It opens in **Narrative Zoom** > **Zoom**.

### The top of a scene card

- **The title**, with **Rename** at the right. Click **Rename**, type the new name, and click **Save** (or **Cancel**).
- **Write this scene** (orange). Makes the card's page in the Writing Room and takes you there. Once a page exists, this button becomes **Open the page**. See [Write this scene: from plan to page](#write-this-scene-from-plan-to-page).
- **Move…** opens the move dialog. See [Moving a card safely with Move…](#moving-a-card-safely-with-move).
- **Scene 3 of 24** (gray): where this card falls in reading order. It appears only for cards with a place in the order, meaning a written scene or a card decided on a beat.

### Placement, and the sections beside it

Under the buttons, the page shows how this card ties into the book:

- **Placement:** "Decided: Catalyst" (click the beat to open it), or "Not on a beat yet." Below it, any "Might go at Fun and Games" lines, in pencil.
- **Viewpoint:** the scene's viewpoint character, once its written scene has one.
- **The plan:** a short summary of the plan fields you've filled in.
- **How we got here:** "Before:" the card just before this one in reading order, and "Caused by" any card connected to this one with **causes**.
- **What must follow:** "Causes" the cards this one causes, and "Next:" the card that follows in reading order.
- **In this scene:** the characters, places and things connected to the card with **appears in**. Click a name to follow that character through the story (see below).
- **Reveals**, **Sets up**, **Pays off**, **Depends on:** other connections, when there are any.
- **The page:** once the scene is written, its opening lines and word count.

Sections with nothing in them don't appear, so a new card looks short.

**Following a character through the story.** Click a name under **In this scene**, and Zoom opens a page that says "Followed through the story in order". **Where it appears** lists every scene and card the character is in, numbered in reading order. **Connections, in story order** lists every tie to them, in the same order. Below that is their full record. Use it to check that a character doesn't vanish for half the book, or that a secret is planted before it's used.

### Your plan for this scene: the field groups

Below the summary is the full card, under **Your plan for this scene**. It has the same bar across the top as a beat page (**Kept for you**, **Ask about this**, the legend, **On this page**), then groups of fields.

Groups you haven't touched are folded, showing a triangle, the group's name and "5 places to write, all optional". Click the name to unfold it. **Every field on a card is optional.**

| Group | What it holds | When you'd use it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **From my planning** | Fields that came in with planning you imported | When you've brought in an outline or story bible |
| **The scene** | The job, the goal, the conflict, the outcome, the value at the start and at the end | Almost every scene |
| **Around it** | How we got here, What must follow, When (story time), Where, Disaster, discovery or win | When a scene's link to its neighbors matters |
| **Story time** | Day in the story, Date, Year, Time of day, Time since the previous scene | Books with a tight clock, long spans or flashbacks |
| **Atmosphere** | Season, Weather, Physical atmosphere, Emotional atmosphere, Sensory details, What the setting does to the plot | Scenes where the place pushes back |
| **What the reader knows** | What the reader suspects by the end, Visible or hidden story | Mysteries, twists, any book with a secret |
| **Scene and sequel** | Scene or sequel, Reaction, Dilemma, Decision | Shown when Scene and Sequel (Swain) is on in Novel setup |
| **Reader response** | What you want the reader to feel | Any book; it feeds the Reader response check |

Some groups appear only when you turn them on in **Novel setup**, because only some books need them:

| Group | Turned on by |
| --- | --- |
| **Emotion on the page** | The Writers Helping Writers thesauri (Ackerman and Puglisi) |
| **Narrator and truth** | An unreliable narrator |
| **Who tells it** | Narration reconstructed by someone else |
| **Timeline strand** | A dual timeline or a frame story |
| **Callbacks** | Running gags |
| **Heat** | A heat level for intimate scenes |

Every field is explained in [Scene cards: every field](#scene-cards-every-field). Below are the ones that most need explaining.

**The common mistake.** Filling every group on every card. Most scenes need **The scene** and little else. Reach for the other groups only where timing, atmosphere, a secret or a viewpoint trick actually matters.

### Value at the start and Value at the end: the turn

A **value** is something your viewpoint character cares about that can flip to its opposite: trust and betrayal, safety and danger, hope and despair, love and loneliness. Robert McKee teaches that a scene works when it **turns** a value: it starts on one side and ends on the other.

The marks **+** and **-** mean the value is standing well (+) or badly (-). Write the value and its charge, such as "Safety +".

1. Open **The scene** group on the card.
2. Under **Value at the start**, click **Add** and write the value and its charge: "Trust in her crew +".
3. Under **Value at the end**, click **Add** and write where it ends: "Trust in her crew -".

Once both are filled, the Board shows them on the card ("Trust in her crew + → Trust in her crew -"), and the Pacing check shows the turn when you hover over a scene. If a written scene starts and ends on the same value, Pacing asks whether you meant it: "Every scene has to turn. Intended?"

**Why it helps.** A scene that ends where it began is filler. Readers feel it even when they can't say why.

**The common mistake.** Naming a mood instead of a value. "A nice afternoon" has no opposite. "Safety" does.

**Example.** Lands: Marisol and her first mate trust each other (+); by the end she's found his letter to the company (-). Fails: they talk about the weather and part as friendly as they met.

### The conflict (><)

The **><** mark in the teaching is shorthand for two forces pushing against each other. Write who is up against whom, what each wants, what's in the way, and who wins. Conflict doesn't have to be a fight: a locked door, a polite refusal or the character's own fear all count.

### When a scene happens: three places, and why

A card has three ways to say when a scene happens, each for a different job:

- **When (story time)**, under **Around it**: words, as loose as you like. "The evening of the second storm." "The next morning." It's the same box as **When (story time)** beside the page in the Writing Room.
- **Day in the story**, **Date** and **Year**, under **Story time**: numbers the program can sort. These are what put scenes in story order on the **Timelines** check.
- **Time of day** and **Time since the previous scene**: detail for continuity.

Words like "the next morning" show on Timelines but can't be sorted. If you want scenes lined up in the order things happen, give them a day number, a date or a year.

### Disaster, discovery or win, on a card

A card's **Disaster, discovery or win** (under **Around it**) labels *this scene's* outcome. The field with the same name on a beat labels the turn of the whole *beat*. A beat that spans many scenes can be a disaster overall while some of its scenes are small wins.

### Scene and sequel: Swain's rhythm

Dwight Swain, a writing teacher, split a novel's units into two kinds that alternate:

- A **scene** is action: the character has a **goal**, meets **conflict**, and ends in a **disaster** (she doesn't get it, or gets it with a cost).
- A **sequel** is aftermath: a **reaction** (the feeling, before any thinking), a **dilemma** (the options, none of them good) and a **decision** (what she'll do).

The decision becomes the goal of the next scene. That's the chain that makes a plot feel driven by choices instead of coincidence.

**Where:** the **Scene and sequel** group on a card. It appears when **Scene and Sequel (Swain)** is switched on in **Novel setup**.

1. In **Scene or sequel**, write "Scene" or "Sequel".
2. For a sequel, fill in **Reaction**, **Dilemma** and **Decision**.
3. Open the next card and make its goal (**The viewpoint character's objective**, under **The scene**) the decision you just wrote.

**When to use it.** When a stretch of the book feels breathless (all scenes, no room to feel) or stalled (all sequels, no action). Labeling a run of cards shows you which.

**The common mistake.** Thinking a sequel is slow summary. It's where the character absorbs the blow and chooses, and it can be the tensest page in the chapter.

**Example.** Scene: Marisol confronts the harbor master; he denies knowing about the closure (disaster). Sequel: she sits in her truck shaking (reaction); she can go to the newspaper and burn every bridge, or keep quiet and lose the route (dilemma); she calls the newspaper (decision). Next scene's goal: get the reporter on the ferry before the company finds out.

### What you want the reader to feel

Under **Reader response**. Click **Add** to see four suggestions, **Profound**, **Funny**, **Relatable** and **Shocking**, or type your own word. Think of it as the colored sticky tab a reader would put on this page to find it again. The **Reader response** check gathers these tabs chapter by chapter.

## The Board: placing scene cards

The **Board** is your corkboard: every beat at once, with your scene cards pinned underneath.

**Where:** **Narrative Zoom** > **Board**. Clicking a beat on the story strip (the thin map of the book across the top of the window) also opens Narrative Zoom, on whichever tab you used last.

### What's on the Board

- **Four rows**, one per part of the book: **Act One**, **Act Two, first half**, **Act Two, second half**, **Act Three**.
- **A box for each beat**, with its number and name at the top, such as **8. Fun and Games**. Click the name to open the beat page.
- **Smaller steps** (Stasis = Death, the Finale steps) inside their beat's box, in slanted type with a line beside them. Cards can go on them too.
- **Cards** under each beat. Each card shows:
  - a small circle: hollow if the scene isn't written yet, filled if it has words,
  - its title (click to open the card),
  - its value turn, if you've filled in both values,
  - "might go here", in gray, if the card only might go there,
  - your **Kept for you** mark, if you set one.
- **+ Add a card** at the bottom of every beat box.
- **Not on a beat yet**, at the bottom of the Board: cards with no spot anywhere, each with a **Place on a beat…** button.

The line at the top of the Board sums it up: "Drag a card onto a beat, or open a beat to add one. A card in pencil only might go there. Nothing moves until you have seen what the move would change."

### Decided or might go here: two kinds of placement

A card can sit on a beat in two ways:

- **Decided** ("It goes here"): solid type. You've made up your mind.
- **Might go here** ("It might go here"): pencil type, with "might go here" under the title. A candidate. A card can be a "might" on several beats at once while you decide.

**Why the difference matters.** The questions the program asks about order (in the Move dialog and the Story review) count only decided cards, so a "might" never raises a problem. The continuity checks still *show* "might" cards, marked "planned, might go here", so you can see your whole plan.

That's also why a card that's only a "might" on Fun and Games doesn't appear under **Not on a beat yet**: it has a candidate spot, just not a decided one.

**The common mistake.** Deciding placements just to make the Board look full. An honest "might" is better than a false "decided". It keeps the checks honest too.

**Example.** In I Declare War, "The cruel exchange" is a "might" on both Bad Guys Close In and All Is Lost. The author hasn't chosen yet, and the Board shows the card under both beats.

### Adding a card from the Board

1. Click **+ Add a card** under the beat.
2. Type a few words in the box (**A new card**).
3. Click **Add**. The card is decided on that beat.

To add a card as a "might", or to add a card to a beat from its page, use the forms under **Scenes here** on the beat page instead.

### Placing a card with Place on a beat…

For a card under **Not on a beat yet**:

1. Click **Place on a beat…** on the card.
2. The dialog **Place "…" on a beat** opens. In **To**, choose the beat.
3. Choose **It goes here (decided)** or **It might go here**.
4. Read the preview, then click **Make this move** (or **Mark it as might go here** for a "might"). See the next section for everything in this dialog.

### Dragging cards

You can drag a card from one beat to another, or from **Not on a beat yet** up to a beat. While you drag, the beat under the pointer is highlighted. When you let go, nothing moves yet: the move dialog opens with that beat already chosen, so you can see what the move would change first.

**If dragging is awkward.** **Not on a beat yet** sits at the bottom of the Board, often far below Act One. Hold **Ctrl** and press **-** to shrink the page until both are on screen (**Ctrl** and **0** puts it back), or use **Place on a beat…**, which needs no dragging at all.

### Moving a card safely with Move…

**Where:** **Move…** on a scene card, **Place on a beat…** on the Board, or dropping a dragged card. All three open the same dialog.

The dialog is titled **Move "…"** for a card that's already decided somewhere, or **Place "…" on a beat** for one that isn't.

- **To**: the beat to move to. Smaller steps are indented and named "(a step inside Setup)". The card's current beat is grayed out.
- **It goes here (decided)** or **It might go here**.

**For a decided move**, the dialog shows a preview before anything changes. This is the **Ripple Effect**: what one move does to the rest of the book. It begins "Nothing has changed yet. This is what the move would do."

- **Questions it would raise:** problems the move would create, such as a card landing before the scene that causes it. "None." means it raises nothing.
- **Questions it would settle:** problems the move would solve.
- **What moves with it:** each card whose place would change, from where to where.
- **What it touches:** everything connected to the card, marked "(direct)" for its own connections or "(follows from)" for things tied to those through causes, setups and payoffs.

Then choose one:

- **Make this move:** makes it. The move is one step in the **Story Trail**, where you can undo it.
- **Save as an alternate:** keeps the move as a "what if" without making it. Type a name in **Name for an alternate** first. See [Alternate structures: trying another shape](#alternate-structures-trying-another-shape).
- **Leave it:** closes the dialog. Nothing changes.

**For a "might"**, there's no preview, because a "might" can't raise a problem. Click **Mark it as might go here**, or **Leave it**.

**Moving a card doesn't move written pages.** If the card's scene is already written, its pages stay where they are in the Writing Room. The program may then ask about order. For example, when Kevin declares war is moved to the Opening Image while its written scene still comes after the Catalyst's, the preview says it "would come after The Catalyst incident (Catalyst), which is a later beat." Move the scene in the Writing Room too, or leave it and treat the card's beat as a note to yourself.

**The common mistake.** Clicking **Make this move** without reading the questions. They're the whole point of the dialog: a move that raises "Readers would meet the effect before the cause" might be a deliberate flashback, or a mistake you'll find in revision.

## Where beats fall: the numbers for your length

**Where:** **Narrative Zoom** > **Where beats fall**.

This tab is a table of numbers: where each beat lands in a book of your length. It doesn't place anything. To place a card, open the beat or use the Board.

### Setting a target length

Until your book has a target length, only percentages show. The note says "Your book has no target length yet, so only the percentages are shown."

1. Type your target in **Target length**, such as 90000.
2. Click **Set the target**.

The table then adds the word and page for each beat. Pages are estimates at 250 and 300 words per page, the two common manuscript page sizes. To change the target later, use **Settings**.

### Reading the table

| Column | What it shows |
| --- | --- |
| **Beat** | Each beat, numbered. Smaller steps are indented. Click to open the beat. |
| **Where** | Its place as a percentage of the book, such as "10%" or "20% to 50%" |
| **Around word** | The word count where it lands in a book of your target length |
| **Page** | The page, at 250 and at 300 words per page |
| **Drafted so far** | How many written scenes sit on it, and their words, or "nothing yet" |

The Finale's five steps say "not set" under **Where**, because they share the Finale's whole stretch. That's expected.

### The placement model

On a Save the Cat novel, a dropdown called **Placement model** chooses which numbers to use:

- **Novel percentages (Brody):** Jessica Brody's placements for novels. For a novel, this is the usual choice.
- **Screenplay proportions (Snyder, 110 pages scaled):** Blake Snyder's page numbers for a 110-page screenplay, scaled to your length. Useful if you think in film structure or are adapting a screenplay.

The two disagree slightly (Snyder's Catalyst comes a little later, for example). Neither is right. Hover over the dropdown to see which of your sheets each one comes from.

### Questions at the top of Where beats fall

If your draft doesn't match your sheet's advice, a box at the top asks about it. For example: "Break into Two starts at 42.6% of the manuscript. Your sheet says: 'Before you get one-quarter of the way through your novel.' Intended?"

**This may look wrong early in a draft, but it's working as designed.** The percentage is measured against what you've *written so far*, not your target. With only two short scenes written, the second one sits halfway through your manuscript. The question settles as the draft grows. You can answer it in the **Story review**, with **It's intentional** and a reason such as "Only two scenes written."

## Alternate structures: trying another shape

An **alternate structure** is a saved "what if": a different arrangement of your cards on the beats, kept beside the real one. Your current structure stays untouched until you choose to adopt the alternate.

**Where:** **Narrative Zoom** > **Alternate structures**.

**When to use it.** When you're torn between two shapes. Should the confession come at the Dark Night of the Soul or at All Is Lost? Save each as an alternate, compare them, then pick.

### Starting an alternate

From scratch, on this tab:

1. Under **Start an alternate**, choose a card in **Choose a card…**.
2. Choose the beat it would go on in **Choose a beat…**.
3. Type a name in **Name for the alternate**. Make the name say what changes, such as "Brawl as the low point". You'll be glad later.
4. Click **Save as an alternate**.

Or from any move: in the move dialog, type a name in **Name for an alternate** and click **Save as an alternate**.

### Comparing, adopting, letting go

Each saved alternate is listed with three buttons:

- **Compare:** shows, "Compared with the story as it stands", the questions it would raise ("It raises no new questions" if none) and the cards it would move.
- **Adopt:** makes the alternate's moves for real. Like any move, it's a step in the Story Trail.
- **Let it go:** deletes the alternate. Your story doesn't change.

**The common mistake.** Saving an alternate with no clear name. Compare lists the cards that would shift from one beat to another, but a card that had no decided spot before doesn't show as a shift. The alternate's name may be your only reminder of what it does.

## Write this scene: from plan to page

When a card is ready, or when you simply want to start, **Write this scene** turns the plan into a page.

**Where:** the orange **Write this scene** button at the top of a scene card. In the Writing Room, the list **Planned, not written yet** on the left offers the same thing for every card, as **Write it**.

1. Open the card.
2. Click **Write this scene**.
3. The Writing Room opens on a new, blank scene with the card's title. The page says "Write anything. A sentence counts."

**Where the new scene goes.** Story Canon puts it where your plan says it belongs: right after the written scene of the nearest card before it in story order. If nothing before it is written yet, it goes at the very start of the first chapter. If the book has no chapters yet, Chapter 1 is made for it. You can always move it with **Up** and **Down** in the Writing Room.

**How the plan follows you.** Beside the page, the **Scene plan** panel shows **From the card**: the beat and what happens, with **Open the full card**. The two stay linked:

- On the card, **Write this scene** becomes **Open the page**, and **The page** section shows the scene's opening lines and word count.
- On the Board, the card's circle fills in once the scene has words.
- In the Writing Room, **Show in Narrative Zoom** takes you from the page back to its card.

**The common mistake.** Waiting until every field on the card is filled. The Writing Room's **Drafting readiness** list may say "Still open below. You can write anyway." Believe it. Many answers show up only once you write.

## I'm stuck: one beat, one sentence

**I'm stuck** is for the day the whole book feels too big. It shows you one beat and asks for one sentence.

**Where:** **I'm stuck**, top right of Narrative Zoom. The Writing Room has its own **I'm stuck** too.

What you see:

- "Read only this beat. Do not look at the others." Opened from the Narrative Zoom button, it starts at the first beat. Opened from a scene in the Writing Room, it starts at that scene's beat and says so.
- The beat's name and **What this beat does**, from your beat sheet, with its example.
- **One sentence of what happens at this beat**, a single line to type in.
- **Save the sentence**.
- "Or the next smallest step": **Fill in one line about a character** (opens the Story Brain) and **Write one line in a narrator's voice, complaining about anything** (opens Capture).
- **A different beat**, a dropdown at the bottom right, if this one doesn't spark anything.
- **Close**.

**How to use it:**

1. Click **I'm stuck**.
2. Read the beat. Only that one.
3. Type one sentence about what might happen there, and click **Save the sentence** (or press **Enter**).
4. The dialog says "Saved. That counts." Your sentence is now on that beat, in pencil, under **What it is (your plan)**. Click **See it there** to look.

**Why it works.** A blank book asks you a hundred questions at once. This asks one. Your sentence is saved as Developing, never decided, so it costs nothing to be wrong.

**Example.** On a gray day, Marisol's author opens **I'm stuck**, reads only the Midpoint, and types "The islanders vote to keep the ferry, and Marisol realizes she wanted them to vote no." She didn't know that until she wrote it.
