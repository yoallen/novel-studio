# Narrative Zoom

This chapter covers the plotting side of Story Canon: what story structure is, how a beat sheet works, and everything in **Narrative Zoom**, the view where you plan beats and scene cards, place cards on a corkboard, see where each beat lands in a book of your length, and try other shapes for your story without losing the one you have. Read it when you want to plan, when the middle of your book feels lost, or when you've written a good way in and want to see the shape of what you have.

You don't have to plot to use Story Canon. If you find your story as you write, you can skip this chapter and come back later. Nothing here is required. A beat sheet is a lens you pick up, not a rule you must obey.

## Story structure, for a writer who has never plotted

### What story structure is

**Story structure** is the shape of a story: the order of the big changes, and roughly where in the book each one comes. Every story that works has a shape, whether or not its writer planned one. A hero lives an ordinary life. Something breaks it open. She tries to cope, things get worse, she hits bottom, and she finds a way through, changed.

Writers who study structure have noticed that readers expect these changes at roughly the same places. The story usually breaks open somewhere in the first tenth of the book. The middle has a big turn near the halfway point. The lowest moment comes about three quarters of the way in. When a change comes far too late, or never comes, readers feel it as "the middle drags" or "the ending came out of nowhere", even when they can't say why.

**Why it matters.** A novel is long. Without a sense of its shape, it's easy to write two hundred pages where nothing turns, or to rush the ending because you didn't know you were there. Knowing the shape lets you check that each change is really on the page.

**Do most writers need it?** Not before they start. Many writers find the shape while drafting and use structure later, in revision, to see what's missing. Others plan it first. Story Canon works for both.

### What a beat, an act and a beat sheet are

A **beat** is one turning point in the story: a place where the story has to change direction. The moment the hero's ordinary life breaks open is a beat. So is the turn halfway through, and the low point near the end.

An **act** is a large section of the story. Most structures use three acts: the beginning (Act One), the middle (Act Two) and the end (Act Three). Story Canon splits Act Two in half at the midpoint, because the middle is long and the two halves do different work, so you'll see four rows: **Act One**, **Act Two, first half**, **Act Two, second half** and **Act Three**.

A **beat sheet** is a list of beats in order, with a rough idea of where each one falls in the book, such as "about 10% of the way in". Teachers of writing have published several beat sheets. They name the beats differently, but they describe much the same journey.

**The common mistake.** Treating a beat sheet as a form to fill with events. A beat is a *turn*, not a slot. If nothing is different for your hero once the beat is over, the beat isn't there, however much happens on the page.

**Example.** Marisol Vega has kept the Gull Point lighthouse for years and has never left the island. Her Catalyst (the beat that kicks the story into motion) is the morning an empty boat washes onto her rocks. That's a turn: someone was in that boat, and she can't go back to pretending the world ends at her shoreline. If instead a supply boat drops off the usual crates, something happened, but nothing turned.

### Save the Cat, the beat sheet Story Canon knows best

The beat sheet Story Canon knows most deeply is **Save the Cat**. Blake Snyder created it for screenwriters in his book *Save the Cat!*. Jessica Brody adapted it for novelists in *Save the Cat! Writes a Novel*. It has fifteen numbered beats:

| Act | Beats |
| --- | --- |
| Act One | 1. Opening Image, 2. Theme Stated, 3. Setup, 4. Catalyst, 5. Debate |
| Act Two, first half | 6. Break into Two, 7. B Story, 8. Fun and Games |
| Act Two, second half | 9. Midpoint, 10. Bad Guys Close In, 11. All Is Lost, 12. Dark Night of the Soul |
| Act Three | 13. Break into Three, 14. Finale, 15. Final Image |

Here is the whole sheet in one pass, using *The Keeper of Gull Point*:

1. **Opening Image:** a snapshot of the hero before anything changes. Marisol eats supper alone on the lighthouse gallery, lamp lit, radio off.
2. **Theme Stated:** someone (never the hero) says what the story is really about. Ana Ruiz, the new doctor in Port Ellery, tells her, "You can't keep a light for people you won't let in."
3. **Setup:** the hero's ordinary world, and everything wrong with it.
4. **Catalyst:** the event that breaks the ordinary world open. The empty boat on the rocks.
5. **Debate:** the hero resists. Marisol tells herself it's the coast guard's problem.
6. **Break into Two:** the hero chooses to step into a new world. Marisol rows to Port Ellery to find out whose boat it was.
7. **B Story:** a new relationship that carries the theme. Ana.
8. **Fun and Games:** the hero in the new world, the part readers came for. Marisol asks questions all over town and learns that people answer.
9. **Midpoint:** a big turn that raises the stakes, often a false victory or a false defeat.
10. **Bad Guys Close In:** pressure from outside and doubt from inside. Cyrus Hale, the harbor master, starts closing doors.
11. **All Is Lost:** the lowest point.
12. **Dark Night of the Soul:** the hero sits with the loss.
13. **Break into Three:** the hero finds the truth she needed and chooses to act on it.
14. **Finale:** the hero acts, changed.
15. **Final Image:** a snapshot that mirrors the first one and shows the change.

Two beats also have smaller steps inside them. Setup holds **Stasis = Death**, and the Finale holds five steps. They're explained in [Smaller steps inside a beat: Stasis = Death and the Finale](#smaller-steps-inside-a-beat-stasis-death-and-the-finale).

The descriptions, placement advice and examples you'll see on each Save the Cat beat come with Story Canon. The screens call this "the beat sheet", and each example is credited to the book or film it comes from. When a question quotes the sheet's placement advice, it begins "Your sheet says".

### The other beat sheets Story Canon offers

When a novel has no beat sheet yet, you can choose one of these:

| Beat sheet | Whose it is | Its shape |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Save the Cat (Brody for novels)** | Blake Snyder, adapted by Jessica Brody | Fifteen beats, placed by percentage of the book |
| **The Hero's Journey** | Christopher Vogler's telling of Joseph Campbell | Twelve stages of departure, ordeal and return |
| **Story Circle** | Dan Harmon | Eight steps: go somewhere unfamiliar, pay a price, come back changed |
| **Seven-Point Structure** | Dan Wells | Decide the ending first, then the opposite beginning, then the turns between |
| **Three-Act Structure** | The classic shape | Setup, confrontation and resolution, with turning points between |
| **McKee's Five Parts** | Robert McKee, *Story* | Inciting incident, progressive complications, crisis, climax and resolution |

Every one of them works with the Board, the beat pages, the story strip and the checks. Their beats come with a short plain description, a usual placement ("Usually around 10% of the book.") and, on some beats, a question to test it. A few things belong to Save the Cat only: the three worlds, the **Questions for this beat** page on a beat, the sheet's examples, and the choice of two placement models in **Where beats fall**.

**Which should you pick?** If you've never used one, pick **Save the Cat (Brody for novels)**. It's written for novels and Story Canon teaches it most fully. If you already think in another shape, pick that one.

### McKee's Five Parts

**What it is.** Robert McKee, in his book *Story*, describes one design that shapes every level of a story: a scene, a sequence of scenes, an act, and the whole book. It has five parts. **McKee's Five Parts** is that design as a beat sheet.

1. **Inciting Incident.** The event that radically upsets the balance of the hero's life, for better or worse, and wakes a conscious desire to set it right. McKee places it within the first quarter of the story. It raises the question the climax will answer.
2. **Progressive Complications.** Scene by scene, it gets harder. The hero acts, and the world answers in a way they didn't expect. McKee calls the space between what a character expects and what happens **the gap**. Each complication costs more than the last, until there is no going back.
3. **Crisis.** At the moment of greatest pressure, the hero faces a true **dilemma**: the best of two bad choices, or two good things that can't both be kept. The choice shows who they really are.
4. **Climax.** The action the hero takes on that choice, and the change it causes. McKee's test is that the change is *absolute and irreversible*.
5. **Resolution.** Whatever follows the climax: the change spreading through the hero's world, and a moment for the reader to feel it.

**Why it works.** The five parts are about pressure and choice, not page counts. That makes them a good partner for Save the Cat: Snyder tells you *where* the turns usually fall, and McKee tells you *what each turn must do*. Story Canon ties McKee's Crisis to Save the Cat's Break into Three, and his Climax to the Finale, so a scene placed on one sheet finds its place on the other.

**The common mistake** is a crisis that isn't a real choice: the hero is rescued, or luck decides. Then the climax shows nothing about who they are. If the hero could pick the right answer without losing something, it isn't a dilemma yet.

**Example.** In *The Keeper of Gull Point*, the Crisis comes when Marisol learns that Cyrus Hale hid the truth about the boat to protect Eli's memory. She can expose him and save the lighthouse, or keep the secret and lose it. Both choices cost her something she loves. That is a crisis.

You can use McKee's Five Parts as the novel's beat sheet, or keep Save the Cat as the beat sheet and compare the two on the **Alternate structures** tab.

### Your own method as a beat sheet

If you learned structure from a teacher or book that isn't on that list, you can build it yourself. Story Canon calls this a **method**: a way of working from any teacher or book, which can carry its own beats, its own questions and its own fields. Your methods belong to you, not to one novel, so every novel can use them.

1. Click **Settings** on the right edge of the window, then the page tab **6 All my novels**. Under **My methods**, click **Open my methods**. (You can also get there from **Novel setup**, the page where you choose the methods a novel uses, with **Make a method of my own**.)
2. Click **Make a method of my own** and give it a **Name**. You can also fill in **Where it comes from (teacher, book, course)** and **What it does for me**.
3. Under **Its beats**, choose where to begin from the **Start from** choices: **A blank page** to build every beat yourself, or **Save the Cat**, **The Hero's Journey**, **Story Circle**, **Seven-Point Structure**, **Three-Act Structure** or **McKee's Five Parts** to copy that sheet's beats as a starting point you can then change. (Leave **Its beats** empty if your method is a way of thinking with its own questions and fields rather than a beat sheet.)
4. For each beat, fill in its name, where it starts and ends (**at** and **to**, as percentages of the book), which act it belongs to, and **What this beat is and the job it does**. **A test it must pass** is optional: a question to check the beat against. Use the arrows to reorder beats, and **Add a beat** for the next one.
5. **Lines up with… (optional)** tells Story Canon which shared moment this beat matches, such as the midpoint or the darkest point. That's how scenes placed on one beat sheet find their places when you switch to another. A beat with nothing chosen simply isn't carried across.
6. Click **Save this method**.

A method with beats appears in the beat sheet list the next time you choose one. The rest of the My methods page (questions and fields a method can add) is covered in [Settings and your other computers](#settings-and-your-other-computers).

**The common mistake.** Copying a whole published beat sheet word for word. Write each beat's description in your own words: what it does and how you'll know it's working. That's the version that will actually help you at two in the morning.

### Choosing a beat sheet for a novel

This is the one place in the guide that says where a beat sheet is chosen and changed. Other chapters link here.

**What it is.** Each novel uses one beat sheet at a time: the one whose beats appear on the Board, on the story strip and in **Where beats fall**. A novel can also have none, if you find the story as you write.

**Where you choose one.** There are two places, and they do the same thing:

- **In Narrative Zoom**, while the novel has no beat sheet. The **Zoom** tab (and the **Board** tab) says "This novel has no beat sheet. Frameworks are lenses you choose, never requirements. Your story works without one."
  1. Open the dropdown beside that message. It lists the five built-in sheets, then any of your own methods that have beats.
  2. Click **Use this beat sheet**.
  3. The beats appear at once: on the Zoom page, on the Board, and on the story strip at the top of the window.
- **In Novel setup** (open **Settings**, then **Open novel setup**), on its page **5 My storytelling toolkit**. Tick a beat sheet, such as **Save the Cat (Brody for novels)**. If the novel had none, a message says it "is now the beat sheet for this novel". Your own methods that have beats are listed here too, marked *yours*.

**Where you change it.** In Novel setup, on the same page **5 My storytelling toolkit**.

1. Tick the beat sheet you want to move to, as well as the one you have. Once more than one is ticked, a line appears: **The beat sheet that drives the board**, with a dropdown.
2. Before you switch, tick **Offer my placed scenes on the matching beats** if you want your scene cards carried across. They are offered on the matching beats of the new sheet as "might go here", waiting for you to decide.
3. Choose the new sheet in **The beat sheet that drives the board**.

Nothing is decided for you, and nothing placed on the old sheet is lost. If you untick the sheet that drives the board, the next ticked one takes over.

**Where you change a sheet's beats.** The built-in sheets can't be edited, but you can make your own from any of them. That happens in **My methods**, as [Your own method as a beat sheet](#your-own-method-as-a-beat-sheet) explains: start from a copy of the built-in sheet and change what you like. The Story Brain's Beats page has an **Open My methods** link for this. To skip one beat in this book only, use **Remove this beat from the novel** on the beat (see [Remove this beat from the novel](#remove-this-beat-from-the-novel)).

**The common mistake.** Switching beat sheets to fix a sagging middle. The sheet is a lens; if the middle sags, the new sheet will show the same gap under another name. Try **Alternate structures** first.

## The story strip across the top of the window

**What it is.** The **story strip** is a thin map of your whole book. Once a novel is open, it runs along the very top of the window, on the purple cover above the page, from the first page of the book (left) to the last (right). It is there on every view. (The row with the novel's title, the **Saved** indicator and **Capture** is a different thing: it sits at the top of the page itself, just below the strip.)

**Why it matters.** It tells you at a glance where you are in the shape of the book, and how much of your plan is placed, without opening anything.

**Do you need it?** You don't do anything with it. It draws itself from your beat sheet and your scene cards, or from your chapters if you have no beat sheet.

### Story strip: what the marks mean

**With a beat sheet**, the strip shows your acts and beats:

| Mark | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| Slanted words above the line (*Act One*, *Act Two, first half*, *Act Two, second half*, *Act Three*) | The names of the acts, each over a faint band showing how much of the book it covers |
| A thin white tick | One beat that is a moment, placed where it falls in the book |
| A faint shaded band with a line at its left | One beat that covers a stretch of the book, such as Fun and Games |
| A small solid cream box above a beat | One scene card **decided** on that beat (or on a smaller step inside it) |
| A small hollow box, an outline only | One scene card that only **might go** there: in pencil, its spot not decided |
| A pink pin: a thin pink line with a round head | Where the scene you have open falls in the book |

Up to five boxes show above any one beat. A beat with more cards still shows five.

**With no beat sheet** (you find the story as you write), the strip shows your chapters instead, each one as wide as its share of your words, with its name in slanted type above it (very short chapters go unlabeled). One box stands for one scene in that chapter, and the pink pin stands on the chapter you are writing in.

**The pin.** "The scene you have open" is the scene last opened in the Writing Room, even while you are on another view. With a beat sheet, the pin shows only when that scene's card is decided on a beat; a card that only *might* go somewhere has no fixed spot to pin.

### Story strip: pointing and clicking

Hold the mouse over a beat, and a small label gives its name and what is there, such as *Catalyst, 1 placed, 2 might go here*. Click any beat (or chapter) to open Narrative Zoom, on whichever tab you used last.

**Example.** Above the Catalyst on the strip of *The Keeper of Gull Point* is one solid box (the card "The empty boat", decided there) and two hollow ones (two other cards that might go there). While you write "The empty boat" in the Writing Room, the pink pin stands at the Catalyst.

### Story strip: when it looks wrong but is working

- **No pink pin.** No scene has been opened in the Writing Room yet, or the open scene's card is not decided on a beat (it only might go there, or the scene has no card).
- **The strip shows only "Chapter 1".** Your novel has no beat sheet yet. Choose one as [Choosing a beat sheet for a novel](#choosing-a-beat-sheet-for-a-novel) explains, and the acts and beats appear.
- **The strip is empty.** The novel has no chapters and no beat sheet yet.
- **The strip disappeared.** A writing sprint is running, or the Writing Room's **Focus** is on. Both hide everything but the page, and the strip comes back when they end.

## Finding your way around Narrative Zoom

**Narrative Zoom** lets you look at your book from any distance, like zooming a map: the whole story, one act, one beat, one scene card, and back out again.

Across the top of the view are two rows of tabs.

- **The top row** holds the four views of your structure: **Zoom**, **Board**, **Where beats fall** and **Alternate structures**. At its right end is **I'm stuck**, a small rescue for blank days (see [I'm stuck: one beat, one sentence](#im-stuck-one-beat-one-sentence)).
- **The second row**, labeled *Continuity checks*, holds the checks that look for problems across the book. See [The continuity checks bar](#the-continuity-checks-bar).

| Tab | What it's for |
| --- | --- |
| **Zoom** | Walk down from the whole book to one act, one beat, one scene card, and back |
| **Board** | Every beat at once, like a corkboard, with your scene cards pinned under them |
| **Where beats fall** | The numbers: where each beat lands in a book of your length |
| **Alternate structures** | Saved "what if" versions of your structure, to compare and adopt or let go |

Narrative Zoom remembers the tab and the page you were on, so when you come back you land where you left.

### Zoom: the breadcrumb and Back

Once you're below the top level, a line of links appears above the page title, such as **The Keeper of Gull Point › Act One › Catalyst**. This is the **breadcrumb**: the path from the whole book down to where you are. Click any part of it to go back up to that level.

Sometimes a second link appears at the right end of that line: **Back to Setup, where you came from**. It returns you to the last Zoom page you looked at, when that page isn't already in the breadcrumb. It remembers Zoom pages only, so if you opened a card from the Board, it may name the Zoom page you visited before you went to the Board. That's expected.

## The Zoom tab

### Zoom: the book overview

**Where:** **Narrative Zoom** > **Zoom**, at the top level (the page titled with your book's name).

This page shows your whole book in four parts: **Act One**, **Act Two, first half**, **Act Two, second half** and **Act Three**. Beside each act, a small gray line counts what's in it, such as "5 beats, 2 scenes decided, 3 might go here". Click an act's name to open it.

"0 scenes decided" doesn't mean something is missing. It only means none of the cards in that act is marked as decided yet. Cards that only might go there are counted separately. See [Decided or might go here: two kinds of placement](#decided-or-might-go-here-two-kinds-of-placement).

### The three worlds: Thesis, Antithesis and Synthesis

On a Save the Cat novel, three bands sit among the acts:

- **Thesis World**, above Act One
- **Antithesis World**, above Act Two (one band covers both halves)
- **Synthesis World**, above Act Three

Each band gives a one-line meaning. A fold-out under it, **Read the full description from the beat sheet**, opens the longer description from the beat sheet. At the bottom of the page, a section called **The three worlds** sums them up.

**What they mean.** Snyder borrowed the words from an old way of arguing: a statement (thesis), its opposite (antithesis), and a new idea that combines them (synthesis). In a story, the three acts are three states of being:

- **Thesis:** the hero's world before the story changes it. Who she is and what needs fixing.
- **Antithesis:** the upside-down world she's thrown into at Break into Two. Everything she knew is reversed or tested.
- **Synthesis:** the new self she becomes in Act Three, made from the old world and the upside-down one.

**How they relate to the acts.** The worlds and the acts line up, but they're different ideas. The acts are *where* you are in the book. The worlds are *who the hero is* while you're there. Break into Two carries her from Thesis into Antithesis, and Break into Three carries her into Synthesis. That's why those two beats need to be definite. A hero who drifts into Act Two never really left home.

**Why it helps.** When a scene in Act Two feels flat, ask: is this happening in the upside-down world? If the hero is still living her Act One life in the middle of the book, the act break didn't take.

**Example.** Marisol's Thesis World is the lighthouse: the same lamp, the same tide tables, a life that works because no one else is in it. Her Antithesis World is Port Ellery, where she has to knock on doors and ask strangers for help. Her Synthesis World is a lighthouse with the door open: still the keeper, still stubborn, but someone is allowed to stay.

**The common mistake.** Treating Synthesis as "back to normal". The hero doesn't go home unchanged. She brings something from both worlds.

The bands appear only for Save the Cat. Other beat sheets show the four acts without them.

### Scenes not on a beat yet

At the bottom of the book overview, **Scenes not on a beat yet** lists the scene cards that have no spot at all: not decided anywhere, and not "might go" anywhere either. Click one to open it, then use **Move…** to place it, or drag it on the Board.

If some cards only *might* go on a beat, the note above the list says how many and that they "show in pencil under their act". They aren't missing. Open an act to see them.

### Zoom: an act page

**Where:** **Narrative Zoom** > **Zoom** > click an act.

An act page lists the act's beats in order, each with where it falls and how much room it takes, such as **3. Setup** "1% to 10%, several scenes" and **4. Catalyst** "10%, one scene". A moment that falls inside a longer beat says so: **2. Theme Stated** "5%, during Setup, one scene". The sizes are Jessica Brody's; see [Scene type: how much room the beat takes](#scene-type-how-much-room-the-beat-takes).

Under each beat you may see scene cards:

- A card in ink is **decided**: it goes on this beat.
- A card in pencil (gray, with a dashed underline), followed by "might go here", only might go on this beat.

Click a beat to open its page, or a card to open the card.

**When to use it.** When you want to work through one part of the book without the noise of the rest. Act Two is where most books lose their way, and each of its halves is a good size for one sitting.

### Zoom: a beat page

**Where:** **Narrative Zoom** > **Zoom** > an act > a beat. You can also click a beat's name on the Board or in **Where beats fall**.

A beat page is the beat's record (chapter 01 introduced records, fields and pages), with two extra pages in front: one that teaches the beat, and one that shows which scene cards sit on it. Everything is in one row of numbered pages, so you turn from the lesson to your cards to your own plan without leaving the page.

#### Beat page: the top

- **The title**, such as **Catalyst**.
- **Where it falls**, in one line: "At 10% of the book." If you've set a target length (see [Where beats fall: the numbers for your length](#where-beats-fall-the-numbers-for-your-length)), it adds the words: "around word 9,000 of 90,000". Once you've written scenes on this beat, it adds where the beat actually starts in what you've written so far.
- **Kept for you**: a dropdown that marks this beat as yours alone, such as **Mine to write**. The collaborator (the optional AI writing partner) may talk about a marked beat but never writes or decides it for you. **Not marked** means no mark.
- **Ask about this**: opens a conversation with the collaborator about this beat. See [Conversations and the collaborator](#conversations-and-the-collaborator).
- **The legend**, one line of samples showing how each status looks on the page: **Decided (inked)**, **Developing (pencil)**, **Still open (a blank line)**, **Example, never canon**, **Suggestion (a slip, tucked in)** and **Set aside (faded, kept below)**, the last one faded with a line through it.
- **Suggestions waiting for you**, if the collaborator has suggested anything for this beat. Accepting one turns to the page where it landed.

#### Beat page: its pages

Under the top is the row of page tabs, then **All pages** at the end:

| Page | What it holds | Shown when |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **1 About this beat** | The beat sheet's lesson: what the beat does, where it goes, a question to ask, an example, how much room the beat takes in your book, what Jessica Brody teaches about it, and any smaller steps inside it | Always |
| **2 Scenes here** | The scene cards on this beat, the cards that could go here, and the forms to place or add a card | Always |
| **3 In this book** | Your plan for the beat in *your* book: its job, your event, the options you're weighing, and three checks | Always |
| **4 Questions for this beat** | The extra questions Save the Cat asks at this particular beat | Only on beats that have questions |
| **5 Notes and connections** | A free notes box, ties to other records, and fields of your own | Always |

Each page's heading names the beat, such as **1 About this beat: Catalyst** or **3 In this book: Catalyst**, so you always know which beat you are looking at, even after you scroll past the title.

A page number turns ink blue when something is on that page: **1 About this beat** always, **2 Scenes here** once a card is placed or might go here, and the others once you've written something on them. One page shows at a time. The links at the bottom of each page go to the page before and after, and **All pages** shows every page at once, one after another. Story Canon remembers which page you were on for each beat while the program is open.

Pages with nothing to show are left out, and the numbers close up. Questions for this beat exists only on the eight Save the Cat beats that carry extra questions, so on a beat like Catalyst the pages are **1 About this beat**, **2 Scenes here**, **3 In this book** and **4 Notes and connections**.

**The common mistake** is reading page 1 and thinking the beat has nowhere to write. Page 1 is the beat sheet's lesson. Your own plan starts on **In this book**.

#### Beat page: About this beat

This page teaches the beat. Under the heading **What this beat really does**:

- the beat sheet's description of the beat,
- **Where it goes**: advice on placement,
- **A question to ask**: a test for whether the beat is working, on the beats that have one,
- **An example from the beat sheet**, set apart with the source credited (for example, "from *The Shining* by Stephen King"). Examples are never part of your story. They show the move so you can find your own version.

Then, on Save the Cat beats, two more sections:

- **How much room it takes**: whether this is a single-scene or a multi-scene beat, and how that fits what you have planned. Taught in full in [Scene type: how much room the beat takes](#scene-type-how-much-room-the-beat-takes).
- **What Brody teaches**: what the beat does and where Jessica Brody puts it, the moves she teaches for it, and **Brody's checklist**, her questions for checking the beat once it is written. The section ends with its credit: *From Jessica Brody, Save the Cat! Writes a Novel, The Save the Cat! Beat Sheet. Her teaching, in Story Canon's words.* Your own sheet's lesson, above it under **What this beat really does**, is kept exactly as you wrote it; Brody's teaching sits beside it, never in place of it.

On Stasis = Death, this page also points you to the Setup beat, where the six things that need fixing have their own list.

If the beat has smaller steps inside it, they're listed at the bottom of this page under **Inside this beat**. Click one to open its own page.

#### Beat page: Scenes here

This page lists the cards decided for this beat, each with a small circle: hollow if its scene has no writing yet, filled if it has words. If none are decided, it says "No scenes placed here yet." A card decided on one of the beat's smaller steps is listed here too.

**Could go here**, if it appears, lists the cards that might go on this beat.

Below the lists are two forms.

**To place a card you already have:**

1. Open **Choose a card you already have…**. Cards with no spot are grouped first, under *Not on a beat yet*. Cards decided somewhere else follow, under *On another beat*.
2. In the next dropdown, choose **It goes here** or **It might go here**.
3. Click **Place it here**.

This places the card straight away, without the preview the Move dialog gives. If you're moving a card that's already decided somewhere else and want to see what the move would change first, use **Move…** on the card instead (see [Moving a card safely with Move](#moving-a-card-safely-with-move)).

**To make a new card right on this beat:**

1. Type a few words in **Or a new scene, in a few words**, such as "Marisol finds the boat at dawn".
2. Choose **It goes here** or **It might go here**.
3. Click **Add scene card**. The new card appears in the list. Click it to fill in its plan.

#### Beat page: In this book

From here on, the pages are the beat's own record, where you write what the beat does in *your* book. At the top of **In this book**, a fold-out called **About beats: what they are and how to use them** gives a short lesson, with **Read the full chapter in Help**, which opens the beat fields in [Every kind and every field](#every-kind-and-every-field).

#### In this book: four fields that look alike

The **In this book** page has seven fields. The first four confuse almost everyone at first, because they all seem to ask "what happens here?" They don't. Each holds a different kind of thinking, and keeping them apart is what lets you change your mind about the event without losing track of what the event has to do.

| Field | What it holds | Status it usually carries |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **What this beat does in this book** | The *job*: what must be different once this beat is over | Decide this early |
| **What it is (your plan)** | The *event*: what actually happens on the page | Developing until you're sure |
| **How it could happen** | The *options*: several ways the event could be staged, side by side | Developing, several at once |
| **One way it could be done** | An *illustration*: how a beat like this could be done, from another book or a made-up case | Always Example |

Here's Marisol's Catalyst filled in all four ways:

- **What this beat does in this book:** "Marisol's sealed life cracks open. From here she can't keep the world on the far side of the water."
- **What it is (your plan):** "At dawn an empty boat is on her rocks, its line cut clean."
- **How it could happen:** three entries. "She finds it at first light, on her rounds." "The fog lifts and it's simply there, bumping the rocks." "Cyrus radios to ask if she's seen a boat, before it has even arrived."
- **One way it could be done:** "In a western, a riderless horse walks into town. The move: something arrives with no one in it, and the absence is the question."

Notice what happens if you change the event. Say the empty boat becomes a child's shoe washed up in the kelp. The job hasn't changed: her sealed life still cracks open. That's why the job is decided first and the event stays Developing.

**How to fill one in:**

1. Click **Add** at the right end of the field.
2. Type your entry in the box that opens.
3. Choose its status in the dropdown: **Decided**, **Developing**, **Still open** or **Example**.
4. Click **Save**. A Developing entry "sits beside any other possibilities". A Decided one replaces the earlier decision, which "stays in history".

Every field also has its own teaching: click **Why it matters** under it.

**The common mistake.** Putting your favorite event in **What this beat does in this book**. "The boat washes up" is an event. "She can't keep the world out any longer" is a job. Write the job, then test every event against it.

#### Disaster, discovery or win

This field labels the kind of turn. A **disaster** leaves the hero worse off against her goal. A **discovery** means she learns something that changes the picture. A **win** means she gets what she was after, for now. Write the one word, or a short sentence if the turn is mixed.

**Why it helps.** Stories move by alternating these. A run of five wins drains tension. A run of five disasters numbs the reader. Labeled beats show you the rhythm at a glance.

The field appears on every beat because every beat turns somehow, but it matters most on the big ones: Catalyst, Break into Two, Midpoint, All Is Lost, Break into Three. Scene cards have the same field. If a card decided on Break into Two, the Midpoint or All Is Lost has "win" decided in this field, the Story review (one of the continuity checks) asks whether you meant it: "A disaster is a reversal, never a win. Intended?"

**The common mistake.** Calling a discovery a win. Marisol learning who cut the boat's line isn't the same as stopping him.

#### Caused by the hero's own efforts? The passive hero check

A **passive hero** is one that things happen to: luck, a rescue or another character drives the turn while she waits. Readers stop caring about a hero who isn't the reason anything changes.

This field is a check against that. Does this turn happen because of what the hero chose and did? There's no yes or no switch. Click **Add** and write your answer in a few words, such as "Yes: she rows out into the storm herself" or "No, and that's fine here: the Catalyst happens to her."

**When it matters.** Most at Break into Two, All Is Lost, Break into Three and the Finale, where she has to act. The Catalyst often happens *to* the hero, and that's normal.

**Example.** Lands: Marisol takes the lighthouse launch out against Cyrus's orders to reach the stranded boat. Fails: the coast guard finds everyone while she watches from the gallery.

#### Scene type: how much room the beat takes

**What it is.** Jessica Brody, in *Save the Cat! Writes a Novel*, gives every beat one of two sizes:

- **A single-scene beat** is one scene or one chapter. Brody calls it one piece of information. Opening Image, Theme Stated, Catalyst, Break into Two, B Story, Midpoint, All Is Lost, Break into Three and Final Image are single-scene beats.
- **A multi-scene beat** spans several scenes or chapters. Setup, Debate, Fun and Games, Bad Guys Close In, Dark Night of the Soul and Finale are multi-scene beats.

Stasis = Death has no size of its own: it is a moment inside the Setup. The five Finale steps take their room from the Finale.

**Why it works.** Brody's beats come in pairs of action and reaction. A single-scene beat is an action, a hinge the story turns on. The multi-scene beat after it is the reaction, the stretch where the hero lives with what just happened. The Catalyst is followed by the Debate. The All Is Lost is another Catalyst, and the Dark Night of the Soul is another Debate. Inside the Finale, the High Tower Surprise and Dig Deep Down repeat the pair once more. Knowing which beats are hinges and which are stretches tells you how many scene cards each one should hold before you write a word.

**How it fails, in both directions.** A hinge stretched across several chapters loses its punch: a Catalyst that dawns on the hero slowly is no longer a blow. A stretch squeezed into one scene breaks its promise: a Fun and Games of one scene never delivers the premise the reader came for.

**A note on the B Story.** The B Story *beat* is single-scene, because it is the introduction of the B Story character. The B story as a whole runs through the rest of the book; in Story Canon that thread is a **storyline** record, and it can span as many scenes as it needs.

**Where you see it.**

- On an act page, beside each beat: "one scene" or "several scenes".
- On **About this beat**, under **How much room it takes**. A single-scene beat says so. A multi-scene beat gives its stretch of the book, such as "from 20% to 50% of the book", and, once you have set a target length in **Settings**, the words that stretch comes to: "At your target of 80,000 words, that is about 24,000 words". Once you have written at least three scenes, it adds roughly how many scenes that is at your usual scene length (the middle length of your written scenes): "roughly 11 scenes at your usual length of 2,200 words". Then a line counts the scene cards you have placed on the beat: "In your book: 2 scene cards so far."
- On **In this book**, the field **Scene type**, where you record your own decision for this book.

**When it asks a question.** Story Canon asks, never orders:

- A single-scene beat asks when it holds more than one scene card **and** those cards fall in more than one chapter. Brody allows one scene or one chapter, so several written scenes in the same chapter do not count against it. The question reads: "Catalyst is a single-scene beat and holds 3 scene cards. A single-scene beat lands in one scene or one chapter. Is all of this the Catalyst, or is some of it the Debate?" It names the beat the extra scenes most likely belong to.
- A multi-scene beat asks when it holds exactly one scene card: "Fun and Games is a multi-scene beat with 1 scene card. It spans several scenes or chapters. Is it getting the room it needs?"
- An empty beat never asks. No cards means you have not planned it yet.

The question shows on the beat's **About this beat** page, in pencil with a dashed line beside it, and in **Continuity checks** > **Story review**, where you can answer it: dealt with, intentional (with your reason), or not now.

**Example.** Lands: Kevin's Catalyst is one scene, the dog wrecking the garden and the war declared, and his Fun and Games runs many chapters of escalating pranks. Fails: the Catalyst unfolds across three chapters of Kevin slowly getting annoyed, and Fun and Games is a single prank.

#### Beat page: Questions for this beat

Some beats carry one more page of fields. Its tab says **Questions for this beat**; the heading inside credits where the questions come from, **Save the Cat's questions for this beat**. These are the extra questions Snyder and Brody ask at particular beats. The page appears only when Save the Cat is your beat sheet (or the novel is not set up yet).

Only the beats that need them carry them:

| Beat | Its questions |
| --- | --- |
| Theme Stated | **The line that states the theme** |
| Setup | **Home**, **Work**, **Play**, **Six things that need fixing** |
| Debate | **The Debate question** |
| Break into Two | **The hero's WANT** |
| Midpoint | **False victory or false defeat** |
| All Is Lost | **False victory or false defeat**, **Whiff of death** |
| Break into Three | **The hero's NEED** |
| Final Image | **Hero is changed, or rejects change** |

**The hero's WANT** and **The hero's NEED** are a pair. The Want is what the hero thinks she's after; the Need is what will actually heal her. Marisol's Want is never to need anyone. Her Need is to let someone stay.

**False victory or false defeat** is another pair. Save the Cat treats the Midpoint and All Is Lost as mirror images: a false victory at the Midpoint sets up a real low at All Is Lost, and a false defeat sets up the reverse. If you decide the same answer on both beats, Story Canon asks: "Your Midpoint and All Is Lost are both … Intended?"

**Whiff of death** is something at All Is Lost that hints at death: a real one, or the end of something. For Marisol, it might be finding Eli's old life jacket stowed in the boat.

#### Six things that need fixing, on Setup

In the Setup, Save the Cat asks you to show the problems in the hero's life that the story will later fix or pay off. Brody calls them the "things that need fixing"; Snyder suggests about six. They're often small and specific: a habit, a grudge, a broken thing nobody repairs.

**Where:** the **Setup** beat, its **Questions for this beat** page, the field **Six things that need fixing**.

1. Click **Add**.
2. Write one problem, such as "She hasn't opened Eli's room in twelve years."
3. Click **Save**.
4. Click **Add** again for the next one. Each problem is its own entry, so you can decide, pencil in or set aside each one separately.

**Why it helps.** Each item is a promise. Later, at the Finale and the Final Image, this list tells you what the ending owes the reader.

**The common mistake.** Listing problems that never come back. If a problem is never mended (or deliberately left broken), cut it from the list or give it a payoff.

**Example.** Marisol's list: she eats supper standing up at the lamp; she lets the mainland calls ring out; the foghorn is broken and she won't report it; she won't set foot in the dinghy; she hasn't opened Eli's room; she hasn't sung since he died. By the Final Image, the foghorn sounds and she's singing under it.

Stasis = Death doesn't have its own list. Its page sends you back to Setup, because the problems are planted across the whole Setup.

#### Beat page: Notes and connections

The last page holds the things every record has:

- **Notes**, a free box for anything that doesn't fit a field. It saves when you click away.
- **Connections**, which tie this beat to other records: a character, a clue, a scene card. Under **Make a connection**, choose a connection word (such as **foreshadows** or **plants**), choose what it connects to, and click **Connect**. The line under the form reads the connection back to you as a sentence ("Reads: Setup plants …"), so you can check the direction before you save.
- **Fields you, an import or a method added.** If you brought in planning documents, any fields that came with them sit here, marked "(your field)", meaning a field you or your import added rather than one Story Canon ships with. See [Bringing things in and sending things out](#bringing-things-in-and-sending-things-out). Fields that a method of your own adds to beats also sit here, under the method's name, while that method is chosen for the novel in Novel setup.
- **Add your own field**, which makes a new field. Type its name (for example, "Weather"), then choose **Just this beat** to add it here only, or **Every beat** to add it to all of them. Tick **A list, with several entries** if it should hold several separate items. Click **Add field**.

**The common mistake.** Choosing **Every beat** for something only one beat needs. It will then sit, empty, on fifteen records. **Just this beat** is the right choice for most one-off ideas.

#### Remove this beat from the novel

At the very bottom of a beat page, under whichever page you are on: **Remove this beat from the novel**, with the note "Only if this book does not use it. Your scene cards stay."

Use it when your book deliberately skips a beat (some books have no separate B Story, for example).

1. Click **Remove this beat from the novel**.
2. Read the question that appears. It explains that your notes on the beat go to **Deleted** (the place where removed things wait and can be restored) and that cards placed on it go back to "Not on a beat yet".
3. Click **OK** to remove it, or **Cancel** to keep it.

Nothing on the beat is lost. Your cards keep all their plans; they just lose their spot.

### Smaller steps inside a beat: Stasis = Death and the Finale

Some Save the Cat beats have **smaller steps**: steps inside a numbered beat, with no number of their own. You'll see them:

- on the parent beat's page **About this beat**, under **Inside this beat**,
- on the **Board**, inside the parent beat's box, with a line beside them,
- in the **Move…** dialog, indented, as "Stasis = Death (a step inside Setup)",
- in **Where beats fall**, indented under their parent.

You can place cards on a step exactly as on a beat. A card decided on a step shows inside the step on the Board, and on the parent beat's page **Scenes here**.

#### Stasis = Death

A step inside **Setup**, just before the Catalyst (about 9.5% of the book). It's the moment the reader sees that if nothing changes, the hero's life will slowly go wrong. Staying put is no longer safe. Jessica Brody treats it as a moment inside the Setup, not a beat of its own, so it has no size of its own.

**Example.** Marisol's Stasis = Death: the supply boat skips her again, and she realizes she hasn't spoken a word aloud in nine days.

#### The five Finale steps

Brody breaks the Finale into five steps. Story Canon shows them inside the **14. Finale** box. They have no percentage of their own ("not set" in **Where beats fall**), because they share the Finale's whole stretch.

1. **Gathering the Team:** the hero gathers people, tools and nerve. She may have to repair relationships she damaged in Act Two.
2. **Storming the Castle:** the plan goes into action. (Brody calls this "Executing the Plan".)
3. **Hightower Surprise:** a twist. The plan fails, or the situation turns out to be worse than anyone knew.
4. **Dig Deep Down:** with the plan gone, the hero has only what she's learned. She finally acts on her Need instead of her old Lie.
5. **Executing the New Plan:** she acts as the changed person and wins (or chooses a meaningful loss).

**Example.** Marisol asks Ana and two fishermen she's never trusted to help her (Gathering the Team). They take the launch out to the cove where the boat was cut loose (Storming the Castle). Cyrus has moved the evidence, and the storm cuts them off (Hightower Surprise). Marisol, who believes anyone she cares about will leave, tells Ana to stay with the injured fisherman while she goes on alone, then turns back and asks Ana to come with her instead (Dig Deep Down). Together they bring the lamp back up in time to guide the stranded crew home (Executing the New Plan).

### Zoom: a scene card page

A **scene card** is your plan for one scene, like an index card on a corkboard. It holds what the scene must do, whose goal drives it, what stands in the way, and how it turns. The **scene** itself, in the Writing Room, is the written page. A card can exist long before its scene does, and most plotting happens on cards.

**Where:** click any card's name, on the Board, a beat page, an act page or a continuity check. It opens in **Narrative Zoom** > **Zoom**.

#### Scene card page: the top

These stay at the top, whatever page of the card you are on:

- **The title**, with **Rename** at the right. Click **Rename**, type the new name, and click **Save** (or **Cancel**).
- **Write this scene** (orange). Makes the card's page in the Writing Room and takes you there. Once a page exists, this button becomes **Open the page**. See [Write this scene: from plan to page](#write-this-scene-from-plan-to-page).
- **Move…** opens the move dialog. See [Moving a card safely with Move](#moving-a-card-safely-with-move).
- **Scene 3 of 24** (gray): where this card falls in reading order. It appears only for cards with a place in that order, meaning a written scene or a card decided on a beat.
- The same bar a beat has: **Kept for you**, **Ask about this**, the legend, and any **Suggestions waiting for you** (see [Beat page: the top](#beat-page-the-top)).

#### Scene card page: its pages

Under the top is one row of page tabs, then **All pages**. The first page or two show where the card sits in the story; after them come the card's own record pages, where you write its plan.

| Page | What it holds | Shown when |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Where it sits** | Placement, viewpoint, a summary of the plan, and the card's ties to the scenes around it | Always |
| **The page so far** | The opening lines of its written scene, and the word count | Once the card has a page in the Writing Room |
| **The scene** and the card's other pages | Your plan for this scene (see [The scene card's own pages](#the-scene-cards-own-pages)) | Always |

Because **The page so far** appears only once the card has a page, the number on **The scene** and every page after it goes up by one when you click **Write this scene**. Go by the page names, not the numbers. **Where it sits** turns ink blue once the card is decided on a beat, and **The page so far** once its scene has words. Story Canon remembers which page you were on for each card while the program is open.

#### Scene card page: Where it sits

This page shows how the card ties into the book:

- **Placement:** "Decided: Catalyst" (click the beat to open it), or "Not on a beat yet." Below it, any "Might go at Fun and Games" lines, in pencil.
- **Viewpoint:** the scene's viewpoint character (the character whose eyes the reader sees the scene through), once its written scene has one.
- **The plan:** a short summary of the plan fields you've filled in.
- **How we got here:** "Before:" the card just before this one in reading order, and "Caused by" any card connected to this one with **causes**.
- **What must follow:** "Causes" the cards this one causes, and "Next:" the card that follows in reading order.
- **In this scene:** the characters, places and things connected to the card with **appears in**. Click a name to follow it through the story (see below).
- **Reveals**, **Sets up**, **Pays off**, **Depends on:** other connections, when there are any.

Sections with nothing in them don't appear, so on a new card this page is short.

#### Scene card page: The page so far

Once the card has a page in the Writing Room, this page appears, under the heading **The page**: the opening lines of the scene (or "Blank so far." before you write), then its word count.

#### Following a record through the story

Click a name under **In this scene**, and Zoom opens that record's page, with the line "Followed through the story in order." under its name. Any record you open from Narrative Zoom, other than a beat or a scene card, opens this way. Its row of pages starts with one page of its own:

- **1 Across the story**: **Where it appears** lists every scene and card the record is in, numbered in reading order ("Scene 4 of 24"). **Connections, in story order** lists every tie to it, in the same order.
- Then the record's own pages, exactly as in the Story Brain, each numbered one higher than there: for a character, **Who they are** through **Notes, guardrails and writing aids**.

**When to use it.** To check that a character doesn't vanish for half the book, or that a secret is planted before it's used.

**Example.** Follow Ana Ruiz. If she appears in scenes 6, 7 and 9, then not again until scene 21, your B Story has gone quiet for most of Act Two, and the reader may forget why Marisol's choice at the end matters.

### The scene card's own pages

After **Where it sits** (and **The page so far**, when there is one) come the card's own record pages. A scene card is a notebook with up to seven pages of its own, in this order. They are numbered after the pages in front of them:

| Page | What it holds | Shown when |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **The scene** | The scene's job, goal, conflict, outcome and value turn; and around it: how we got here, what must follow, when, where, and disaster, discovery or win | Always |
| **When it happens** | Story time: day in the story, date, year, time of day, time since the previous scene; and which timeline strand | Always |
| **Who tells it** | Who seems to be telling the scene and who really is; what the narrator believes and what is actually true | **Unreliable narrator** or **A narrator who is not who they seem** is ticked in Novel setup, on its page **How the story is told** |
| **Mood and feeling** | Atmosphere: season, weather, physical and emotional atmosphere, sensory details, what the setting does to the plot; emotion on the page; heat | Always |
| **The reader** | What the reader suspects by the end, visible or hidden story, what you want the reader to feel, callbacks | Always |
| **Scene and sequel** | Scene or sequel, reaction, dilemma, decision | Scene and Sequel (Swain) is turned on in Novel setup |
| **Notes and connections** | Notes, connections, fields from your planning or from a method of your own, and your own fields | Always |

At the top of **The scene**, a fold-out called **About scene cards: what they are and how to use them** gives a short lesson. Because pages that have nothing to show are left out, most cards have five of these pages, and the numbers close up. That is why this guide names a card's pages rather than numbering them. Some groups inside a page also appear only when a choice in **Novel setup** turns them on:

| Group | Turned on by |
| --- | --- |
| **Emotion on the page** | **The Writers Helping Writers thesauri (Ackerman and Puglisi)** |
| **Narrator and truth** | **Unreliable narrator** |
| **Who tells it** | **A narrator who is not who they seem** |
| **Timeline strand** | **Dual timeline** or **Frame story** |
| **Callbacks** | **Running gags and callbacks** |
| **Heat** | A **Heat level** of Sensual, Steamy or Explicit |

**Every field on a card is optional.** Every field is listed in [Every kind and every field](#every-kind-and-every-field), and each has **Why it matters** under it. Below are the ones that most need explaining.

**The common mistake.** Filling every page on every card. Most scenes need **The scene** and little else. Reach for the other pages only where timing, atmosphere, a secret or a viewpoint trick actually matters.

#### Value at the start and Value at the end: the turn

A **value** is something your viewpoint character cares about that can flip to its opposite: trust and betrayal, safety and danger, hope and despair, love and loneliness. Robert McKee, a teacher of story, says a scene works when it **turns** a value: it starts on one side and ends on the other.

The marks **+** and **-** mean the value is standing well (+) or badly (-). Write the value and its charge, such as "Safety +".

1. On the card's page **The scene**, under **Value at the start**, click **Add** and write the value and its charge: "Trust in Cyrus +".
2. Under **Value at the end**, click **Add** and write where it ends: "Trust in Cyrus -".

Once both are filled, the Board shows them on the card ("Trust in Cyrus + → Trust in Cyrus -"), and the Pacing check shows the turn. If a written scene starts and ends on the same value, Pacing asks whether you meant it: "Every scene has to turn. Intended?"

**Why it helps.** A scene that ends where it began is filler. Readers feel it even when they can't say why.

**The common mistake.** Naming a mood instead of a value. "A nice afternoon" has no opposite. "Safety" does.

**Example.** Lands: Marisol trusts Cyrus, who taught her to read the tide tables (+); by the end she's seen the cut rope in his office (-). Fails: they talk about the weather and part as friendly as they met.

#### The conflict

**The conflict** is two forces pushing against each other. Write who is up against whom, what each wants, what's in the way, and who wins. Conflict doesn't have to be a fight: a locked door, a polite refusal or the character's own fear all count.

#### When a scene happens: three places, and why

A card has three ways to say when a scene happens, each for a different job:

- **When (story time)**, on **The scene**: words, as loose as you like. "The night of the second storm." "The next morning." It's the same field as **When (story time)** beside the page in the Writing Room.
- **Day in the story**, **Date** and **Year**, on **When it happens**: numbers the program can sort. These are what put scenes in story order on the **Timelines** check.
- **Time of day** and **Time since the previous scene**, also on **When it happens**: detail for continuity.

Words like "the next morning" show on Timelines but can't be sorted. If you want scenes lined up in the order things happen, give them a day number, a date or a year.

#### Disaster, discovery or win, on a card

A card's **Disaster, discovery or win** labels *this scene's* outcome. The field with the same name on a beat labels the turn of the whole *beat*. A beat that spans many scenes can be a disaster overall while some of its scenes are small wins.

#### Scene and sequel: Swain's rhythm

Dwight Swain, a writing teacher, split a novel's units into two kinds that alternate:

- A **scene** is action: the character has a **goal**, meets **conflict**, and ends in a **disaster** (she doesn't get it, or gets it at a cost).
- A **sequel** is aftermath: a **reaction** (the feeling, before any thinking), a **dilemma** (the options, none of them good) and a **decision** (what she'll do).

The decision becomes the goal of the next scene. That chain is what makes a plot feel driven by choices instead of coincidence.

**Where:** the **Scene and sequel** page on a card. It appears when **Scene and Sequel (Swain)** is switched on in **Novel setup**.

1. In **Scene or sequel**, write "Scene" or "Sequel".
2. For a sequel, fill in **Reaction**, **Dilemma** and **Decision**.
3. Open the next card and make its goal (**The viewpoint character's objective**, on **The scene**) the decision you just wrote.

**When to use it.** When a stretch of the book feels breathless (all scenes, no room to feel) or stalled (all sequels, no action). Labeling a run of cards shows you which.

**The common mistake.** Thinking a sequel is slow summary. It's where the character absorbs the blow and chooses, and it can be the tensest page in the chapter.

**Example.** Scene: Marisol confronts Cyrus at the harbor office; he says the boat was never logged (disaster). Sequel: she sits on the seawall, shaking (reaction); she can tell Ana and let her into this, or row home and bolt the door (dilemma); she knocks on Ana's door (decision). Next scene's goal: get Ana to look at the rope.

#### What you want the reader to feel

On page **The reader**. Click **Add** to see four suggestions, **Profound**, **Funny**, **Relatable** and **Shocking**, or type your own word. Think of it as the colored sticky tab a reader would put on this page to find it again. The **Reader response** check gathers these tabs chapter by chapter.

#### Deleting a scene card

At the very bottom of a card, under whichever page you are on, is **Delete**. It asks "Move "…" to Deleted? You can restore it from there." Click **OK** to move the card to **Deleted**, or **Cancel** to keep it.

### Write this scene: from plan to page

When a card is ready, or when you simply want to start, **Write this scene** turns the plan into a page.

**Where:** the orange **Write this scene** button at the top of a scene card. In the Writing Room, the list **Planned, not written yet** offers the same thing for every card, as **Write it**.

1. Open the card.
2. Click **Write this scene**.
3. The Writing Room opens on a new, blank scene with the card's title. The page says "Write anything. A sentence counts."

**Where the new scene goes.** Story Canon puts it where your plan says it belongs: right after the written scene of the nearest card before it in story order. If nothing before it is written yet, it goes at the very start of the first chapter. If the book has no chapters yet, a first chapter is made for it. You can always move it in the Writing Room.

**How the plan follows you.** Beside the page, the scene plan panel shows **From the card**, with **Open the full card**, on its page **1 The plan**. The two stay linked:

- On the card, **Write this scene** becomes **Open the page**, and a new page, **The page so far**, shows the scene's opening lines and word count.
- On the Board, the card's circle fills in once the scene has words.
- In the Writing Room, **Show in Narrative Zoom** takes you from the page back to its card.

More on the Writing Room side is in [The Writing Room](#the-writing-room).

**The common mistake.** Waiting until every field on the card is filled. The Writing Room's **Drafting readiness** list may say some things are still open and that you can write anyway. Believe it. Many answers show up only once you write.

## The Board tab

The **Board** is your corkboard: every beat at once, with your scene cards pinned underneath.

**Where:** **Narrative Zoom** > **Board**.

### What's on the Board

- **Four rows**, one per part of the book: **Act One**, **Act Two, first half**, **Act Two, second half**, **Act Three**.
- **A box for each beat**, with its number and name at the top, such as **8. Fun and Games**. Click the name to open the beat page.
- **Smaller steps** (Stasis = Death, the Finale steps) inside their beat's box, with a line beside them. Cards can go on them too.
- **A count beside each row's name**, such as *Act One 4 cards. A full row is about 10.*
- **Cards** under each beat. Each card shows:
  - a small circle: hollow if the scene isn't written yet, filled if it has words,
  - its **card number**, counted straight through the Board in story order (only cards decided on a beat get a number),
  - its title (click to open the card),
  - its **turn**, such as **+ to -**, from the card's *Value at the start* and *Value at the end*; *(no turn yet?)* when both ends carry the same charge,
  - **><** when *The conflict* is filled in, or *no conflict yet* in gray when it isn't,
  - "might go here", in gray, if the card only might go there,
  - your **Kept for you** mark, if you set one.
- **+ Add a card** at the bottom of every beat box.
- **Not on a beat yet**, at the bottom of the Board: cards with no spot anywhere, each with a **Place on a beat…** button.

The line at the top of the Board sums it up: "Drag a card onto a beat, or open a beat to add one. A card in pencil only might go there. Nothing moves until you have seen what the move would change."

Under it, **How the Board works: Snyder's cards and McKee's turn** opens a short explanation. Click it once to read it; it stays folded the rest of the time.

### From 15 beats to about 40 cards

**What it is.** The beats are landmarks. Blake Snyder's **Board**, from his first book, *Save the Cat!*, is how you fill the road between them. He pinned index cards to a corkboard in four rows, one for each part of the story, with one card for each scene. He aimed for about 10 cards a row, about 40 in all.

**Why it works.** Fifteen beats tell you *where* the story turns. They don't tell you how you get from one turn to the next. Forty cards do. A beat like **Fun and Games** or **Bad Guys Close In** is a stretch of the book, so it holds several cards; a beat like **Catalyst** is one moment, so it often holds one. When a row looks thin, you can see which part of the book still needs scenes.

**A novel is not a screenplay.** Snyder counted for a two-hour film. A novel often needs more scenes, and Jessica Brody's novel version places beats by percentage of the book, not by page. So treat about 40 as a shape, not a quota. The count beside each row is a guide, never a warning. Snyder also never fixed which card number belongs to which beat; if you read that "card 5 is Theme Stated", that's someone else's tidy guess.

**The two marks on every card.**

- **The turn (+ and -).** Robert McKee teaches in *Story* that every scene must change the charge of something at stake in a character's life: safety, love, hope, freedom. It starts positive and ends negative, or the reverse. If nothing changes charge, McKee calls the scene a non-event, and Snyder says the same. Write the value and its charge in *Value at the start* (for example *Safety +*) and *Value at the end* (*Safety -*). The card shows **+ to -**.
- **The conflict (><).** Snyder's mark for who is up against whom in the scene. Fill in *The conflict* and the card shows **><**. A card with no conflict is usually exposition: something the reader is told, not something that happens.

**The common mistake** is filling the Board just to reach 40. Ten empty-hearted cards are worse than six that each turn and each carry a conflict. Use the count to see where the gaps are, then let the marks tell you whether each card earns its place.

**Example.** Act One of *The Keeper of Gull Point* shows *4 cards. A full row is about 10.* Card 2, "The empty boat", shows **+ to -** (*Safety +* to *Safety -*: Marisol's quiet life is over) and **><** (Marisol against the harbor board). Card 1, "The lamp at dusk", shows **+ to +** *(no turn yet?)*: nothing changes charge. That's the question the Board is asking: does the opening image need a turn, or is it meant as a still picture before the story moves? Either answer can be right. You decide.

### Decided or might go here: two kinds of placement

A card can sit on a beat in two ways:

- **Decided** ("It goes here"): in ink. You've made up your mind.
- **Might go here** ("It might go here"): in pencil, with "might go here" under the title. A card can be a "might" on several beats at once while you decide.

**Why the difference matters.** The questions the program asks about order (in the Move dialog and the continuity checks) count only decided cards, so a "might" never raises a problem. The continuity checks still *show* "might" cards, marked as planned, so you can see your whole plan.

That's also why a card that's only a "might" on Fun and Games doesn't appear under **Not on a beat yet**: it has a possible spot, just not a decided one.

**The common mistake.** Deciding placements just to make the Board look full. An honest "might" is better than a false "decided". It keeps the checks honest too.

**Example.** Marisol's card "Ana takes the mainland post" is a "might" on both All Is Lost and Dark Night of the Soul. You haven't chosen yet, and the Board shows the card under both beats until you do.

### Adding a card from the Board

1. Click **+ Add a card** under the beat.
2. Type a few words in the box (**A new card**).
3. Click **Add**. The card is decided on that beat.

To add a card as a "might", use the form under **Scenes here** on the beat page instead.

### Placing a card with Place on a beat

For a card under **Not on a beat yet**:

1. Click **Place on a beat…** on the card.
2. The dialog **Place "…" on a beat** opens. In **To**, choose the beat.
3. Choose **It goes here (decided)** or **It might go here**.
4. Read the preview, then click **Make this move** (or **Mark it as might go here** for a "might"). The next sections explain everything in this dialog.

### Dragging cards

You can drag a card from one beat to another, or from **Not on a beat yet** up to a beat. While you drag, the beat under the pointer is highlighted. When you let go, nothing moves yet: the move dialog opens with that beat already chosen, so you can see what the move would change first.

**If dragging is awkward.** **Not on a beat yet** sits at the bottom of the Board, often far below Act One. Hold **Ctrl** and press **-** to shrink the page until both are on screen (**Ctrl** and **0** puts it back), or use **Place on a beat…**, which needs no dragging at all.

### Moving a card safely with Move

**Where:** **Move…** on a scene card, **Place on a beat…** on the Board, or dropping a dragged card. All three open the same dialog.

The dialog is titled **Move "…"** for a card already decided somewhere, or **Place "…" on a beat** for one that isn't.

- **To**: the beat to move to. Smaller steps are indented and named "(a step inside Setup)". The card's current beat is grayed out.
- **It goes here (decided)** or **It might go here**.

**For a decided move**, the dialog shows a preview before anything changes. This is the **Ripple Effect**: what one move does to the rest of the book. It begins "Nothing has changed yet. This is what the move would do."

- **Questions it would raise:** problems the move would create, such as a card landing before the scene that causes it. "None." means it raises nothing.
- **Questions it would settle:** problems the move would solve.
- **What moves with it:** each card whose place would change, from where to where.
- **What it touches:** everything connected to the card, marked "(direct)" for its own connections or "(follows from)" for things tied to those through causes, setups and payoffs.

Then choose one:

- **Make this move:** makes it. The move is one step in the **Story Trail** (the record of every change to your story), where you can undo it.
- **Save as an alternate:** keeps the move as a "what if" without making it. Type a name in **Name for an alternate** first. See [The Alternate structures tab](#the-alternate-structures-tab).
- **Leave it:** closes the dialog. Nothing changes.

**For a "might"**, there's no preview, because a "might" can't raise a problem. Click **Mark it as might go here**, or **Leave it**.

**Moving a card doesn't move written pages.** If the card's scene is already written, its pages stay where they are in the Writing Room. The preview may then ask about order. For example, if you move the card "Cyrus logs the boat" to the Opening Image while its written scene still comes after the Catalyst's scene, the preview says it "would come after "The empty boat" (Catalyst), which is a later beat." Move the scene in the Writing Room too, or leave it and treat the card's beat as a note to yourself.

**The common mistake.** Clicking **Make this move** without reading the questions. They're the whole point of the dialog: a move that raises "Readers would meet the effect before the cause" might be a deliberate flashback, or a mistake you'll find in revision.

## Where beats fall: the numbers for your length

**Where:** **Narrative Zoom** > **Where beats fall**.

This tab is a table of numbers: where each beat lands in a book of your length. It doesn't place anything. To place a card, open the beat or use the Board.

**Why it helps.** "The Midpoint comes at 50%" is abstract. "The Midpoint comes around word 45,000, page 150" is something you can check against your draft.

### Where beats fall: setting a target length

Until your book has a **target length** (the number of words you expect the finished book to have), only percentages show. The note says "Your book has no target length yet, so only the percentages are shown."

1. Type your target in **Target length**, such as 90000.
2. Click **Set the target**.

The table then adds the word and page for each beat. Pages are estimates at 250 and 300 words per page, the two common manuscript page sizes. To change the target later, use **Settings**.

### Where beats fall: reading the table

| Column | What it shows |
| --- | --- |
| **Beat** | Each beat, numbered. Smaller steps are indented. Click to open the beat. |
| **Where** | Its place as a percentage of the book, such as "10%" or "20% to 50%" |
| **Around word** | The word count where it lands in a book of your target length |
| **Page** | The page, at 250 and at 300 words per page |
| **Drafted so far** | How many written scenes sit on it, and their words, or "nothing yet" |

The Finale's five steps say "not set" under **Where**, because they share the Finale's whole stretch. That's expected.

### Where beats fall: the placement model

On a Save the Cat novel, a dropdown called **Placement model** chooses which numbers to use:

- **Novel percentages (Brody):** Jessica Brody's placements for novels. For a novel, this is the usual choice.
- **Screenplay proportions (Snyder, 110 pages scaled):** Blake Snyder's page numbers for a 110-page screenplay, scaled to your length. Useful if you think in film structure or are adapting a screenplay.

The two disagree slightly (Snyder's Catalyst comes a little later, for example). Neither is right. Point at the dropdown to see where the chosen model's numbers come from: "Jessica Brody, Save the Cat! Writes a Novel: where each beat falls in a novel" for the first, and "Blake Snyder, Save the Cat!: the screenplay page numbers, scaled to a book" for the second. Other beat sheets have one model, their usual placement, so the dropdown doesn't appear.

### Where beats fall: questions at the top

If your draft doesn't match the sheet's advice, a box at the top asks about it. For example: "Break into Two starts at 42.6% of the manuscript. Your sheet says: "Before you get one-quarter of the way through your novel." Intended?"

**This may look wrong early in a draft, but it's working as designed.** The percentage is measured against what you've *written so far*, not your target. With only two short scenes written, the second one sits halfway through your manuscript. The question settles as the draft grows. You can answer it in the **Story review**, with **It's intentional** and a reason such as "Only two scenes written." See [Catching problems: the continuity checks](#catching-problems-the-continuity-checks).

## The Alternate structures tab

An **alternate structure** is a saved "what if": a different arrangement of your cards on the beats, kept beside the real one. Your current structure stays untouched until you choose to adopt the alternate.

**Where:** **Narrative Zoom** > **Alternate structures**.

**When to use it.** When you're torn between two shapes. Should Cyrus's confession come at the Midpoint or at the Hightower Surprise? Save each as an alternate, compare them, then pick. Most writers use this rarely, but when you need it, it saves you from undoing a move by hand.

### Alternate structures: starting one

From scratch, on this tab:

1. Under **Start an alternate**, choose a card in **Choose a card…**.
2. Choose the beat it would go on in **Choose a beat…**.
3. Type a name in **Name for the alternate**. Make the name say what changes, such as "Confession as the Hightower Surprise". You'll be glad later.
4. Click **Save as an alternate**.

Or from any move: in the move dialog, type a name in **Name for an alternate** and click **Save as an alternate**.

### Alternate structures: comparing, adopting, letting go

Each saved alternate is listed with three buttons:

- **Compare:** shows, "Compared with the story as it stands", the questions it would raise ("It raises no new questions." if none) and the cards it would move.
- **Adopt:** makes the alternate's moves for real. Like any move, it's a step in the Story Trail.
- **Let it go:** deletes the alternate. Your story doesn't change.

**The common mistake.** Saving an alternate with no clear name. Compare lists the cards that would shift from one beat to another, but a card that had no decided spot before doesn't show as a shift. The alternate's name may be your only reminder of what it does.

## I'm stuck: one beat, one sentence

**I'm stuck** is for the day the whole book feels too big. It shows you one beat and asks for one sentence.

**Where:** **I'm stuck**, at the right end of Narrative Zoom's top row of tabs. The Writing Room has its own **I'm stuck** too.

What you see:

- "Read only this beat. Do not look at the others." Opened from Narrative Zoom, it starts at the first beat. Opened from a scene in the Writing Room, it starts at that scene's beat and says so ("… sits at this beat.").
- The beat's number and name, and **What this beat does**, from the beat sheet, with its example if it has one.
- **One sentence of what happens at this beat**, a single line to type in.
- **Save the sentence**.
- "Or the next smallest step": **Fill in one line about a character** (opens the Story Brain, where your characters live) and **Write one line in a narrator's voice, complaining about anything** (opens Capture).
- **A different beat**, a dropdown at the bottom right, if this one doesn't spark anything. It lists the numbered beats only.
- **Close**.

**How to use it:**

1. Click **I'm stuck**.
2. Read the beat. Only that one.
3. Type one sentence about what might happen there, and click **Save the sentence** (or press **Enter**).
4. The dialog says "Saved. That counts." Your sentence is now on that beat, as Developing, under **What it is (your plan)**. Click **See it there** to open the beat.

If your novel has no beat sheet, **I'm stuck** opens as **Start here** instead: "Write one sentence of anything. It counts." **Write one sentence** opens Capture.

**Why it works.** A blank book asks you a hundred questions at once. This asks one. Your sentence is saved as Developing, never Decided, so it costs nothing to be wrong.

**Example.** On a gray day, you open **I'm stuck**, read only the Midpoint, and type "The empty boat is the one Eli took out the night he drowned." You didn't know that until you wrote it.

## The continuity checks bar

The second row of tabs in Narrative Zoom, labeled *Continuity checks*, holds the checks that look across your whole book for problems: **Timelines**, **Viewpoint**, **Who knows what**, **Setups and payoffs**, **Storylines**, **Cast**, **Pacing**, **Reader response**, **Soundtrack**, **Names** and **Story review**. They read what you've placed and connected in this chapter (cards on beats, story times, values, connections) and ask questions, never give orders. Each one has its own section in [Catching problems: the continuity checks](#catching-problems-the-continuity-checks).
