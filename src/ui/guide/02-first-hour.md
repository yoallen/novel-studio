# Your first hour: the Library and a new novel

This chapter walks with you through your first hour in Story Canon, in the order you meet things: opening the program, the short questions about you, the Library where your novels live, the three ways to start a novel, Novel setup (where you say what kind of book this is), the six questions that plant a brand-new novel, and the page where you land when it is all done. Read it the first time you open Story Canon, and come back to it whenever you start another book.

## Opening Story Canon for the first time

Open Story Canon from its desktop shortcut. (If you ever open it some other way, the window says *Open the program from its desktop shortcut* and cannot reach your work. Close it and use the shortcut.)

**The first time,** you land in the Library, because there is no novel to open yet. The Library is taught in full below, in [The Library: your shelf of novels](#the-library-your-shelf-of-novels).

**Every time after that,** Story Canon opens the novel you had open last, on its home page, **Where was I?**. You do not have to pass through the Library each day. When you want the Library, you go to it.

### Getting around: the header, the Library link and the book switcher

Once a novel is open, the **header** is the row of buttons across the top of the page, with a thin line under it. Reading from the left, it holds:

1. The pink **Return to Writing** ribbon, once you have been writing.
2. **Library**, a small link. Click it to go to the Library from any screen.
3. The title of the open novel, with a small arrow. Click it and a menu lists all your novels (with each one's stage in gray, if it has one). Click another title to switch books without going through the Library. Each book opens where you left it.
4. On the right: the **Saved** indicator (in the Writing Room only), the name of the view you are in, and the **Capture** button.

Above the page, on the purple cover across the top of the window, runs the **story strip**, a thin map of your whole book, explained in [Narrative Zoom](#narrative-zoom). The list of views runs down the right edge of the window. The first one is **Where was I?**, with the words *your novel's home page* in small type under its name.

### Help, from the very first screen

The Library has its own **Help** button at the top right of the window, on the purple edge. Click it, or press **F1**, and the whole guide opens over the Library. Press **Esc**, or click **Close Help**, to go back. Inside a novel, Help is one of the views in the list on the right, and F1 works there too.

## About you: telling Story Canon who you are

**What it is.** A short set of questions about you as a writer: what to call you, your pronouns, how you work, and how you like to be helped. Your answers shape how the collaborator speaks with you, and the name Story Canon uses for you on screen.

**Where it is.** The first time you open Story Canon, a card near the top of the Library says **Before we begin, tell Story Canon about you**. After you answer it (or skip it), it does not ask again. The same questions live in **Settings**, on page **5 About you**, where you can change them any time.

**Do most writers need it?** You can write a whole book without it. But if you plan to talk with the collaborator at all, it is the best minute you will spend.

### About you: the questions

Every answer is optional. Small suggestions appear under some boxes; click one to fill the box, then edit the words if you like. Under them you will see *or your own words*, because the suggestions are a convenience, never a list you must choose from.

| Question | What to put there |
| --- | --- |
| **What should I call you?** | Your first name, a nickname or a pen name. Story Canon uses it on screen, for example in **Kept for you**, a label you can put on a record to say "this part is mine, the collaborator stays out of it". With your name it reads *(your name), it's on you now*. It is taught in [The Story Brain](#the-story-brain). |
| **Your pronouns** | Type your own, or click one of the suggestions: *she/her*, *he/him*, *they/them*, *she/they*, *he/they*, *ze/zir*, *any pronouns*, *just my name*. Nothing is ever guessed from your name. |
| **How do you find your story?** | Click *I plot first*, *I find the story as I write* or *Somewhere in between*, or use your own words. |
| **How long have you been writing?** | Years, books finished, or "this is my first." |
| **How do you like to be helped and taught?** | For example: "Teach the idea fully before you show me an example, and don't talk down to me." |
| **Anything that drains you, or that the collaborator should stay away from?** | Topics, habits or tones you don't want. |
| **Anything else about you as a writer** | Whatever else you would like it to know. |

### About you: answering, step by step

1. Type into any boxes you like, and leave the rest empty.
2. On the first-time card in the Library, click **Save**. Or click **Not now**: the card goes away and won't ask again. (**Not now** also keeps anything you had already typed.)
3. Later, in **Settings**, turn to page **5 About you** and change the answers, then click **Save settings** at the bottom of the window. In Settings, nothing on that page is kept until you click **Save settings**.

**Where your answers go.** The card says it plainly: every answer stays on this computer, goes to the collaborator only when you talk with it, and shapes nothing but how it speaks with you. Your story is never changed by it.

**Why it helps.** The collaborator reads these answers before every conversation. It calls you by your name, uses your pronouns, and adjusts how it explains things.

**Your name in other places.** **Settings** also has a **Your name** box, on its page **1 This novel**. When About you has a name, the line under that box says Story Canon calls you by the About you name. **Author name on this book** is separate again: it is the name printed on the title page when you export, so you can put a pen name there. Settings is taught in [Settings and your other computers](#settings-and-your-other-computers).

**The common mistake** is skipping this because it feels like a form. It takes a minute, and it is the difference between a partner who talks over your head and one who talks to you.

**Example.** A first-time novelist writes, under **How do you like to be helped and taught?**, "This is my first book. Define craft words when you use them." Weeks later she asks the collaborator about her opening, and when it mentions a beat, it adds in plain words what a beat is.

## The Library: your shelf of novels

**What it is.** The Library is the bookshelf that holds every novel you are working on, with places to start a new one, bring one in, and put one back. It is the only screen where no novel is open.

**How to get there.** Click **Library** at the left of the header, from any screen.

From top to bottom, the Library holds the parts below. Several only appear when they have something to show.

| Part | What it is | When you see it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Help** (top right of the window, on the purple edge) | The whole guide | Always |
| The title page | *Story Canon*, the line *Where your story takes shape, and nothing gets lost.*, and the motto *Story Canon remembers. You decide.* | Always |
| **Before we begin, tell Story Canon about you** | The About you card, above | The first time only |
| **Your novels** | The shelf itself | Always. With no novels, it says *Nothing here yet. Start a novel below, or bring one in from an archive.* |
| **On your other computers** | Novels another computer has shared that are not on this one yet | Only when you share between computers and one is waiting |
| **Your other computers** | Where you turn on sharing between two computers | Always |
| **Start a novel** | A working title, one sentence, and three buttons | Always |
| **Bring in an archive** | Opens a novel saved as a single file | Always |
| **Removed novels** | Books you have taken off the shelf | Only when you have removed one |

### Library: what a book's cover shows

Each novel stands on the shelf as a small cloth-covered book. Everything on its cover:

| On the cover | What it is |
| --- | --- |
| The title, on a white label | Click it to open the novel. It is a button, even though it looks like a printed label. The novel opens on **Where was I?**. |
| The stage menu (such as *Plotting and planning*) | Where the book is in its life. Choose a different stage from it any time. A new novel says *Choose a stage* until you pick one. See [Library: stages and drafts](#library-stages-and-drafts). |
| A draft name in gray (such as *First draft*) | Which full pass of the manuscript you are on. It shows only when it differs from the stage, so a book whose stage is *Second draft* doesn't say it twice. |
| A word count (such as *1,240 words*, or *1,240 words of 80,000*) | Words in your manuscript. The second number appears once you set a target length in **Settings**. |
| A genre (such as *Mystery*) | Your primary genre, once you choose one in **Novel setup** (below). |
| **Next:** and a few words | The next thing to do, such as *Keep writing "The empty boat"*. It is the same suggestion **Where was I?** shows. |
| **Remove from shelf** (small, underlined, at the bottom) | Takes the book off the shelf without deleting anything. See below. |

**The order of the books.** The novel you worked on most recently stands first, at the left. So the order changes as you work. Read the title before you click anything on a cover.

### Library: stages and drafts

On every book's cover, and on **Where was I?** at the top of page **1 Today**, you will see two labels side by side, such as *Plotting and planning* and *First draft*. They answer two different questions. On Where was I? a line says so: *The stage is what you are doing with the book now. The draft is which full pass of the manuscript you are on.*

#### Stage: what you are doing with the book now

A **stage** is a label for where the book is in its life. The usual list is: *Idea*, *Plotting and planning*, *First draft*, *Second draft*, *Third draft*, *Revising*, *Editing*, *With beta readers* (trusted early readers), *With an editor*, *Querying* (sending the book to literary agents), *On submission* (an agent is offering it to publishers), *Published* and *Resting*.

- **Where to change it:** the stage menu on the book's cover in the Library, or the **Stage** menu on **Where was I?**.
- **What it does:** it is a label to help you keep track. It doesn't lock, hide or change anything. A new novel starts with no stage (*Choose a stage*).
- **Your own stages:** in **Settings**, on page **6 All my novels**, under **For all my novels**, the box **Stages a book can be in** holds the list, one stage per line. Rename, add or remove lines, then click **Save settings**.

#### Draft: which full pass you are on

A **draft** is one complete pass through the manuscript. Your first draft is the first full version; revising it from start to finish makes a second. Story Canon numbers drafts for you, starting at *First draft*.

- **Where it shows:** on **Where was I?** as *Draft: First draft*, with a **Drafts** link beside it, and in gray on the book's cover when it differs from the stage.
- **What it does:** when you finish a draft, the **Drafts** page keeps an exact copy of the whole book as it stood, so you can compare any scene with any earlier draft. The Drafts page is taught in [The Writing Room](#the-writing-room).

#### How stage and draft move together

They are separate, but they help each other in two ways:

- **A book that arrives part way through.** If you set the stage to *Second draft* or *Third draft* before you have kept any draft here, the draft you are in takes that name too. A book you bring in already on its third draft is called *Third draft* from the start.
- **Finishing a draft.** When you keep a finished draft, a book whose stage is a draft name (or has no stage) moves to the next draft's name. A book whose stage is something else, such as *Revising*, keeps its stage.

**The common mistake** is reading *Plotting and planning* beside *First draft* as a contradiction. It isn't. It means: you are planning, and when you write, it will be your first draft.

**Example.** *The Keeper of Gull Point* is at the stage *With beta readers*, and its draft is *Second draft*. The book is out with readers; the version they have is the second full pass.

### Removing a novel from the shelf

**What it is.** A way to tidy your shelf. Nothing in the novel is deleted. It moves to a list at the bottom of the Library, and you can put it back any time.

**When to use it.** For a practice novel, a book you have set down for good, or anything you don't want to see every day. Most writers use it rarely.

1. In the Library, find the novel's cover. Check the title on the label; the books move around.
2. Click **Remove from shelf** at the bottom of that cover.
3. A small box asks: *Take "(title)" off your shelf? Nothing in it is deleted. It waits under Removed novels at the bottom of this page, and you can put it back any time.* Click **OK**. (**Cancel** leaves it where it is.)
4. The book leaves the shelf and appears under **Removed novels** near the bottom of the page.

**The common mistake** is clicking **Remove from shelf** on the wrong cover because the order changed since you last looked. If it happens, put the book back (below). No harm done.

### Removed novels: putting a book back

The **Removed novels** list appears at the bottom of the Library only when you have removed at least one book. Each line shows the title, the date it was removed, and **Put back on the shelf**. Click that link and the book returns to the shelf exactly as it was, with every word, record and conversation.

### A novel prepared for you

Now and then a card may appear above your shelf, headed with a book's title and *is ready*. It offers a novel already *Built from your planning documents and conversations.* Click **Bring in (the title)** to add it to your shelf, or **Not now** to hide the card. If you already brought that novel in on your other computer, don't bring it in again here: turn on sharing (next section) so both computers hold the same book. Most writers never see this card.

### On your other computers, and turning on sharing from the Library

If you write on two computers (say, a laptop at home and another at work), Story Canon can keep your novels in step through a folder both computers can see, such as OneDrive. Most writers with one computer can skip this section.

**Your other computers** is a section of the Library, below your shelf. It is here so that a brand-new second computer, with no novel on it yet, can turn on sharing without opening a book. While sharing is off, it explains how sharing works and offers:

1. **Call this computer**: a name, such as *Home* or *Work*, so the other computer can say where a book came from.
2. **Choose the shared folder**: pick a folder both computers can see. (If your window cannot open a folder chooser, type the folder's path into the box instead and click **Share through this folder**.)

Once sharing is on, a message says *Sharing is on. Novels your other computer has shared appear in this Library under On your other computers. Bring each one here once, and from then on it stays one book on both computers.* That list, **On your other computers**, appears just under your shelf whenever the other computer has shared a novel this one does not have yet. Each line shows the title, which computer shared it and when, and **Bring to this computer**. Click it, and the novel opens here. From then on the two computers keep it as one book.

**The common mistake** is starting the same novel fresh on the second computer, or bringing it in from an archive, instead of using **Bring to this computer**. That gives you two separate books that never stay in step.

Everything else about sharing (the notices that appear above a page, choosing which version to keep when both computers changed a book, **Share now**, **Stop sharing**) is taught in [Settings and your other computers](#settings-and-your-other-computers).

### Bring in an archive

**What it is.** An **archive** is a single file that holds one whole novel with all its history. You make one in **Settings** with **Save an archive of this novel**, for safekeeping or to move a book to another computer by hand.

**How to open one:**

1. Under **Bring in an archive**, click the file chooser and pick the archive file.
2. Before anything is added, a box titled *Import "(title)"?* shows what the archive contains, with any warnings.
3. Choose:
   - **Import** brings it in, when the novel isn't already in your library.
   - **Import as a separate copy** appears when a version of this novel is already here. Your current novel is not changed; the archive comes in beside it.
   - **Cancel** closes the box with nothing added.
4. After an import, the novel opens.

Nothing already in your library is ever overwritten. If the file is not an archive at all, the Library says *That file is not a Story Canon archive.* If an archive is damaged, the box says *This archive cannot be imported safely* and lists what is wrong, and offers only **Cancel**.

## Starting a new novel

The **Start a novel** section of the Library asks for a working title, offers a box for one sentence, and gives three ways to begin. There is no questionnaire. You can start with a title alone.

### Start a novel: the working title and the one sentence

- **Working title.** Required, because the book needs a name on its cover. You can change it any time in **Settings**. If you click a start button with the title empty, the Library says *Give the novel a working title first. You can change it any time.* and waits.
- **One sentence about it, if you have one (optional).** Your whole story in one line. Writers call this a **logline**. The small print under the box says *It becomes the book's one sentence. You can change it any time in Novel setup.* It is kept as **Developing**, so it is not settled until you say so. Leave the box empty if you don't have one yet.

**Example.** Working title: *The Keeper of Gull Point*. One sentence: "When an empty boat washes onto her rocks, a lighthouse keeper who has never left her island must let a stranger in to learn what the sea took from her twelve years ago."

### Start writing

**What it does.** Makes your novel with a first chapter (*Chapter 1*) holding a first, empty scene (*Scene 1*), and opens that scene in **The Writing Room**, the view where you write your book. The empty page says *Write anything. A sentence counts.*

**When to use it.** When you want to find the story by writing it, or when an opening line is already in your head. Planning can come later, or never.

### Set it up first

**What it does.** Makes the novel with the same first chapter and empty scene, then opens **Novel setup**, where you tell the program what kind of book this is. See [Novel setup: telling Story Canon what kind of book this is](#novel-setup-telling-story-canon-what-kind-of-book-this-is).

**When to use it.** When you plan before you write, or when you already know your genre and the methods you like (such as *Save the Cat*). Your choices decide which tools and fields the program offers this book.

### Start from a manuscript or story bible

**What it does.** Makes the novel with no chapters at all, and opens **Import and export**, the view for bringing files in and sending them out, on its **Bring something in** tab, where you choose a file.

- A **manuscript** is your book's text, from Word or another writing program. Story Canon proposes chapters and scenes for you to check.
- A **story bible** is a document of notes about your story: characters, places, decisions. Story Canon shows each passage beside where it would go, and you tick what you want.

**When to use it.** When you already have pages or notes somewhere else. Nothing comes into your novel until you have looked it over, and your words come in exactly as written. Bringing files in is taught in [Bringing things in and sending things out](#bringing-things-in-and-sending-things-out).

### Which way to start

| If you... | Choose |
| --- | --- |
| Want to write today and plan later, or never | **Start writing** |
| Plan first, or know your genre and methods | **Set it up first** |
| Already have chapters or planning notes in a file | **Start from a manuscript or story bible** |

None of these locks you in. A novel started with **Start writing** can be set up tomorrow. A novel set up first can be written in today.

**The common mistake** is treating the choice as important. It only decides which screen you see first.

**Example.** The writer of *The Keeper of Gull Point* has an opening image (Marisol on the gallery of the lighthouse, watching an empty boat turn on the tide) but no plan. She types the title and her one sentence, and clicks **Start writing**. Two days later she opens Novel setup and ticks Mystery.

## Novel setup: telling Story Canon what kind of book this is

**What it is.** A page of optional choices about this novel: its genres, the kind of story it is, how it is told, and the methods you use to build it. Your choices decide which fields, views and questions the program offers this book.

**Why it exists.** A murder mystery needs clue tracking. A romance needs a heat level. A fantasy needs magic rules. Showing every possible field to every book would make every page feel like a tax form. Novel setup lets the program show your book what your book needs, and nothing else.

**Where it is:**

- **Set it up first** in the Library opens it straight away.
- On **Where was I?**, until you make your first choice here, a note says *Whenever you like: tell the program what kind of story this is and which methods you use, and it will offer the right tools.* with a **Set up this novel** link.
- Any time: **Settings**, then **Open novel setup** in the box at the top of the page.

The page title says *How (your title) is set up*. The **Settings** link at its top left takes you to Settings.

**Do most writers need it?** Not before they write. It helps most once you know what the book is. Every part is optional.

**The common mistake** is treating setup as homework to finish before writing. It changes whenever the story does: before the first word, after a few chapters, or halfway through revision when you realize the book is really a romance.

### Novel setup: its pages

Novel setup holds a lot of choices, so it is laid out like a small notebook with numbered pages. Under the two lines at the top is a row of page tabs, then **All pages** at the end of the row. One page shows at a time.

| Page | What is on it |
| --- | --- |
| **1 The heart of the book** | The one sentence and the one paragraph |
| **2 Genres** | The genres, their roles, and what these genres ask of your story |
| **3 Story types** | Blake Snyder's ten story types and their three ingredients |
| **4 How the story is told** | Viewpoint, tense, time, tone, heat level and the other groups |
| **5 My storytelling toolkit** | Beat sheets and methods, and the beat sheet that drives the board |
| **6 What your choices switch on** | A summary of every field and tool your choices added. This page appears only once something is switched on |

**How to move around:**

1. Click a tab to turn to that page.
2. Or use the links at the bottom of each page, which name the page before and after, such as **Page 3: Story types**.
3. Click **All pages** to see every page one after another, as one long page.

Story Canon remembers which page you were on, for each novel, while the program is open. Below the pages, whatever page you are on, are the saving bar and the **Make this my starting point for new novels** button.

**Example.** You want to tick *Weiland (character arcs)* for *The Keeper of Gull Point*. You open Novel setup and click the tab **5 My storytelling toolkit**.

### Novel setup: saving

There is no Save button. Every tick, menu choice and typed box is kept the moment you make it (for typed boxes, when you click away from the box). A line near the top says *Every choice here is kept the moment you make it. There is nothing else to save.* A bar along the bottom of the window says *Every choice saves as you make it.*, and after a change it adds the time, such as *Saved at 3:19:42 PM.*

### Novel setup: the heart of the book

The first page, **1 The heart of the book**, holds the two lines that say what your book is.

- **The one sentence.** Your whole book in about twenty-five words. The gray words inside the empty box suggest how: *The whole book, squeezed until only its central irony is left.* If you typed one sentence in the Library, it is already here.
- **The one paragraph.** The gray words say: *Setup, three disasters, and the ending, one sentence each.* A **disaster** here is a turning point that makes things much worse and forces a new choice.

Beside each label is its status, such as **Developing**. These two lines are kept in the **story world**: the one record in each novel that holds the book's big picture. Changing them here or there changes the same thing. **Open the story world** takes you to that record. (The link appears once the record exists, which happens as soon as the book has a one sentence or anything else to keep there.)

**Looks wrong, but isn't.** Emptying one of these boxes here and clicking away does not erase it; the line keeps its last wording. To change it, type the new wording. The earlier wording stays in the record's history.

**Example.** The one sentence for *The Keeper of Gull Point* names the irony at once: a woman who has made sure she needs no one can only find out what happened by letting someone in. The ending now has a question to answer: does she?

### Novel setup: genres and their roles

**Genres**, on page **2 Genres**, are the familiar kinds of book a bookseller shelves by: Mystery, Romance, Fantasy, Cozy Mystery, Romantic Comedy and about thirty more. Under each name is one line saying what it is.

1. Tick a genre. A small menu appears beside it, set to **Secondary**.
2. Change the menu to set its **role**:
   - **Primary**: what the book mainly is. The cover in the Library shows primary genres.
   - **Secondary**: also strongly part of the book.
   - **Influence**: a genre you borrow from without taking on its rules. A literary novel with a thriller's pace might list Thriller as an influence.
3. To remove a genre, untick it.
4. For a genre that isn't listed, type it in the box under the list (*A genre or subgenre of your own*) and click **Add**. It is marked *yours*.

**What genres switch on:**

- **What these genres ask of your story.** Under the list appear the questions each primary or secondary genre asks, such as *Thriller: What is the ticking clock?* Influences don't add questions. These are questions to think about, not tests.
- **Worldbuilding.** Choosing Fantasy, Science Fiction, Urban Fantasy, Paranormal, Paranormal Romance, Magical Realism, Dystopian, Gothic or Horror adds worldbuilding kinds to the Story Brain (the view where your records live), such as kinds of beings, magic and powers, and languages. See [Every kind and every field](#every-kind-and-every-field).

**A nudge you may see.** If your tone (below) is *Comic*, *Romantic* or *Suspenseful* but the matching genre isn't ticked, a note says so, with a link such as **Mark Suspense as the primary genre**. Ignore it if the tone is only a flavor.

**Example.** *The Keeper of Gull Point* ticks Mystery as **Primary** and Literary Fiction as **Influence**: it is a mystery, written with a literary novel's attention to grief.

### Novel setup: story types and their three ingredients

On page **3 Story types**, under the heading **Story types** (*Blake Snyder, Save the Cat*), is a second list, separate from genre. Blake Snyder, a screenwriting teacher, sorted stories by their core situation rather than their shelf. There are ten: *Monster in the House*, *Golden Fleece*, *Out of the Bottle*, *Dude with a Problem*, *Rites of Passage*, *Buddy Love*, *Whydunit*, *The Fool Triumphant*, *Institutionalized* and *Superhero*.

Under each name is a line saying what it is, and **Needs:** followed by its three ingredients. For example, *Buddy Love* needs an incomplete hero, a counterpart, and a complication.

1. Tick a story type. A role menu appears beside it, just like genres, set to **Secondary**.
2. Below the list, a heading appears: *(Story type): the three ingredients in your story*, with one box for each ingredient. Each box is labeled with the ingredient and a question to answer.
3. Beside each box, a menu sets the answer's status: **Decided**, **Developing** or **Example**. It starts at **Developing**. Set it first.
4. Type your answer, then click away from the box. The answer is kept in the story world record, with the status the menu showed at that moment.

**Why it helps.** The three ingredients are a quick test of whether the story has what its type needs. A Whydunit without a real secret has nothing to dig for.

**The common mistake** is changing the status menu after the answer is kept, and expecting the status to change. It doesn't. To change the status of an ingredient you have already written, open the story world (**Open the story world**, above) and change it there.

**Example.** *The Keeper of Gull Point* ticks *Whydunit*. Its three boxes read *A detective. Who investigates, officially or not?*, *A secret. What is hidden, and why does it matter?* and *A dark turn. Where does the investigator break their own rules?* The writer answers: Marisol, who has no badge and no wish to know; what really happened the night Eli drowned, which Cyrus Hale has kept for twelve years; and the night Marisol breaks the one rule of her life and rows to the mainland.

### Novel setup: how the story is told

Page **4 How the story is told** covers everything about the book apart from its genre. It is a set of groups, each with a short description and a list of choices. Each choice has one line under it saying what it means. Some choices also say **Turns on:** and name the tool or fields they add.

| Group | What it covers |
| --- | --- |
| **Viewpoint** | Whose eyes the story is seen through, and how close: first person, third person limited, omniscient, second person, alternating |
| **Tense** (one choice) | Past, present, or mixed |
| **Construction** | One lead, two, or an ensemble |
| **Information** | What the reader knows, and when: dramatic irony, a hidden truth, an unreliable narrator, misdirection |
| **Time** | The order of the telling: linear, nonlinear, dual timeline, frame story, flashbacks, the present calling up the past |
| **Setting** | When and where: now, recent past, historical, near or far future, an invented world, a hidden world inside ours |
| **Readership** (one choice) | Adult, New Adult, Young Adult, Middle Grade |
| **Tone** | The feeling: comic, cozy, tender, eerie, gritty and more |
| **Heat level** (one choice) | How much intimacy is on the page, from closed door to explicit |
| **Language** (one choice) | How strong the language is, from clean to strong |
| **Content to flag** | Subjects a reader may want to know about first |
| **Romance tropes** | Familiar love-story shapes, such as enemies to lovers |
| **Technique** | Devices such as running gags, foreshadowing, titled chapters |
| **Form** | The shape of the book: letters, series, novella, linked stories, a retelling |

You don't need to understand every group on your first day. Choose what you already know about your book and leave the rest. For example, choosing an invented world turns on the worldbuilding kinds of records. The last page, **What your choices switch on**, lists everything your choices have added, and each tool is taught in the chapter where you use it.

**Groups that take one answer** say *One choice.* in their description and use round buttons. Choosing a new answer replaces the old one. To clear the answer, click the chosen one again.

**Add your own** under any group lets you type a choice of your own (the box says *Your own, under (group)*). Click **Add** (or **Cancel**). It joins that group, marked *yours*, and is ticked for you.

**Example.** *The Keeper of Gull Point* is told in **Third person limited**, **Past tense**, with **A hidden truth or secret** under Information (what happened to Eli) and **Flashbacks** under Time. Its tones are **Tender** and **Eerie**.

### Novel setup: my storytelling toolkit

**What it is.** Page **5 My storytelling toolkit** lists the frameworks and methods you use to build this novel. A **framework** or **method** is one teacher's way of thinking about stories. A **beat sheet** is a framework that lays out the beats of a story in order. Each item in the list has its name in bold and a line saying the job it does, such as *Weiland (character arcs): The arc: the Lie, the Ghost, the Want and the Need, and how the inside changes with the outside.* (These are K.M. Weiland's words for a character's inner journey: the **Lie** is the false belief the character lives by, the **Ghost** is the old hurt behind it, the **Want** is what they think they want, and the **Need** is what they truly need. [The Story Brain](#the-story-brain) teaches them.) If a method adds fields, a line says **Adds:** and where, such as *Goal and motive (Ingermanson) on each character*.

The list holds:

| Kind of method | In the list | In one line |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Beat sheets | *Save the Cat (Brody for novels)* | Fifteen beats, each placed by how far into the book it falls |
| | *The Hero's Journey* | Twelve stages of departure, ordeal and return |
| | *Story Circle* | Dan Harmon's eight steps: someone goes somewhere unfamiliar, pays a price, and comes back changed |
| | *Seven-Point Structure* | Dan Wells's method: decide the ending first, then the opposite beginning, then the turns between |
| | *Three-Act Structure* | Setup, confrontation and resolution, with the turning points between them |
| | *McKee's Five Parts* | Robert McKee's design: inciting incident, progressive complications, crisis, climax and resolution |
| Character and scene methods | *McKee (Story)* | Robert McKee's tools for depth: the forces that oppose the hero, the controlling idea, and the turn in every scene |
| | *Weiland (character arcs)* | How a character changes inside as the plot changes outside |
| | *Snowflake (Ingermanson)* | Randy Ingermanson's method of growing a story from one sentence, with a goal and motive for each character |
| | *Scene and Sequel (Swain)* | Dwight Swain's rhythm: a scene of action, then a sequel of reaction and decision |
| | *The Moral Premise (Williams)* | Stanley Williams's idea that a story proves a truth: a vice leads to defeat, a virtue to success |
| | *The Writers Helping Writers thesauri (Ackerman and Puglisi)* | Reference lists for showing, not telling: wounds, traits and emotions |
| Your own | Methods you made in **My methods** | Whatever you built them to do |
| No outline | **I find my story as I write** | For writers who don't plan ahead |

The program's own one-line description under each name says the same thing in its own words. You don't need to know any of these to start.

1. Tick a method to use it. None is required, and using several together is normal.
2. When you tick your first beat sheet, a message says, for example, *Save the Cat (Brody for novels) is now the beat sheet for this novel.* The **story strip**, the thin map of your book on the purple cover above the page, fills in with acts and beats.
3. A box called **How I use it** appears under each ticked method (*your own note, yours to change*). Write what the method does for you in this book. Click away to keep it.
4. A method of your own is marked *yours*, with an **Edit** link that opens **My methods**, the page where your own methods live. When it is ticked and has questions of its own, they show under it after **Asks:**.
5. **Make a method of my own** opens **My methods**, where you can add a method from any teacher or book, with its own beats, questions and fields. Every novel can use it.

**I find my story as I write** is for writers who don't outline. Tick it with no beat sheet and the book has no beat sheet: the story strip shows your chapters instead of beats.

**The common mistake** is ticking every method because they all sound useful. Each adds fields to your record pages. Tick the ones you actually think in. You can add more any time.

**Example.** The writer of *The Keeper of Gull Point* plots with *Save the Cat* and builds characters with Weiland, so she ticks those two. In **How I use it** beside Weiland she writes, "For Marisol and Ana only. Marisol's Lie: anyone I care about will leave."

### Novel setup: the beat sheet that drives the board

When two or more beat sheets are ticked, a line appears at the bottom of the toolkit: **The beat sheet that drives the board:** with a menu. The beat sheet chosen there is the one used by the **Board** (in Narrative Zoom, where you place scene cards on beats), by the story strip, and by **I'm stuck** (the helper for when you don't know what comes next). The others stay available as other ways to look at the book.

Beside it, **Offer my placed scenes on the matching beats** (ticked at first) means: when you switch beat sheets, scene cards you had placed are offered on the matching beats of the new sheet as suggestions, waiting for you to decide. A message says so when it happens.

**Where a beat sheet is chosen.** This page is where you choose and change your beat sheets. There is one shortcut: in a novel with no beat sheet yet, Narrative Zoom offers a menu to pick one on the spot (see [Choosing a beat sheet for a novel](#choosing-a-beat-sheet-for-a-novel)). To change your beat sheet later, or add another, come back to this page.

### Novel setup: what your choices switch on

The last page, **What your choices switch on**, sums up every field and tool your current choices have added, one line each, such as *Save the Cat (Brody for novels): Save the Cat's questions for this beat (Save the Cat) on each beat*. Genres, which add questions rather than fields, are listed under the genres instead. The page appears once something is switched on; until then the row of tabs ends at **My storytelling toolkit**.

**When it helps.** When you meet a group of fields on a record page and wonder where it came from. A group named *Goal and motive (Ingermanson)* came from ticking Snowflake.

### Novel setup: what changing it does to what you wrote

The page promises: *Nothing you have written is ever hidden or removed by a change here.* In practice:

- **Turning a method off** removes its empty fields from your record pages. Any field that already holds something you wrote stays on the page, with its facts.
- **Before your first choice in Novel setup,** a brand-new novel shows Weiland's fields (the Ghost, the Lie, the Want and the Need) on its arcs (an **arc** is a record of how one character changes over the book; see [The Story Brain](#the-story-brain)), and *Save the Cat*'s questions on beats, so there is something to start from. After your first choice, only what you have chosen shows. (A novel that begins from your starting point, below, with methods already ticked, counts as set up from the start.) If empty arc fields you were using disappear, tick **Weiland (character arcs)**. This is working as designed, not a lost answer.
- **Genres and tones** never remove anything. They add questions and, for some genres, worldbuilding kinds.

## A starting point for new novels

**What it is.** A way to save this novel's working preferences as your defaults, so every new novel starts the way you like to work instead of from nothing.

**Where it is.** The last button in Novel setup, below the pages, **Make this my starting point for new novels**. The small print under it says what it keeps.

**What carries over to new novels:**

| Carries over | Stays with this novel only |
| --- | --- |
| Your name from **Settings**, and the wording of the *it's on you now* marker | Genres |
| Your storytelling toolkit, and the beat sheet that drives the board | Story types and their ingredients |
| Your **How I use it** notes | Everything under **How the story is told** |
| Your words for decisions (what each status is called) | The one sentence and the one paragraph |
| Your writing preferences: how much the collaborator speaks up unasked while you draft and while you plan (its **guidance**), the words per page used to turn word counts into page counts, and your **scene status steps** (the steps a scene moves through, such as *Idea*, *Planned* and *Drafting*) | Every record, scene and word of the book |

**How to do it:**

1. Open the novel whose setup you like, then **Novel setup** (Settings, then **Open novel setup**).
2. Scroll to the bottom, below the pages, and click **Make this my starting point for new novels**.
3. A message near the top says *Saved as your defaults. New novels start this way; your other novels are unchanged.*

From then on, every novel you start in the Library begins with those choices. You can change anything in a new novel's own setup.

**Do most writers need it?** Only once you have a way of working you repeat. It is not something to do on your first day.

**The common mistake** is expecting it to change your existing novels. It doesn't. It only affects novels you start after you click it. To change an existing novel, open its own Novel setup.

**Example.** After setting up *The Keeper of Gull Point* with Save the Cat and Weiland, the writer clicks **Make this my starting point for new novels**. Next spring she starts a second book. It opens with Save the Cat as its beat sheet and Weiland ticked, and no genres chosen, ready for her to say what this one is.

## Plant the seed: six questions for a new novel

**What it is.** A card on **Where was I?** for a brand-new novel. Its heading is **Plant the seed**, and it says: *Six questions to start a blank novel. Answer any you like; skip the rest.* It asks six short questions that give an empty book its first roots: a person who wants something, with something in the way.

**When you see it.** On a novel with no characters yet, until you plant it or click **Not now**.

**Do most writers need it?** No. But if you are staring at an empty novel and don't know where to begin, it is the gentlest start there is.

### Plant the seed: the six questions

The gray words inside each box hint at an answer.

| Question | Hint in the box | Where your answer goes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Who is this story about?** | *Their name, or a working name.* | A new character record with that name, with **Role** set to *Lead*. If you answer the other character questions but leave the name empty, the record is called *The lead*. |
| **Who are they, in a sentence?** | *Where they are in life when the story finds them.* | That character's **Who they are** |
| **What do they want?** | *Something concrete: you would know it if they got it.* | That character's **Goal** |
| **What stands in the way?** | *A person, a force, or something inside them.* | That character's **Conflict** |
| **What do they stand to lose?** | *If they fail, what is gone for good?* | That character's **What they stand to lose** |
| **How might it end?** | *A guess is fine. You can change it any time.* | The story world's **How it might end** |

**Every answer goes in as Developing**, in pencil gray. Nothing is decided until you decide it.

### Plant the seed: step by step

1. Click **Where was I?** in the list on the right.
2. Answer any of the six questions. Skip the rest.
3. Click **Plant it**. (It stays gray until at least one box has words.)
4. Story Canon opens the **Story Brain** on your list of characters, where your new lead is waiting. Click the name to open the record.

On the character's record, **Role**, **Who they are** and **What they stand to lose** are on the first page, **Who they are**. **Goal** and **Conflict** are on the page **Goals, motives and values**, under a group called **Goal and motive (Ingermanson)**. Your ending is on the story world's first page, **The heart of the book**.

**Not now** puts the card away for good in this novel. Your novel is fine without it.

**Why it helps.** An empty novel is the hardest page there is. Six small answers turn it into a person who wants something, with something in the way. That is enough to write a first scene.

**The common mistake** is waiting until every answer is perfect. These are pencil answers. Write the first thing that feels true; you can decide later.

**Looks wrong, but isn't.** The **Goal** and **Conflict** answers appear under **Goal and motive (Ingermanson)** even if you haven't ticked Snowflake in Novel setup. A group that holds your words always shows.

**Example.**

- Who is this story about? *Marisol Vega*
- Who are they, in a sentence? *The forty-one-year-old keeper of the Gull Point light, who has never left the island and has lived alone since her brother Eli drowned twelve years ago.*
- What do they want? *Never to need anyone: to keep the light alone, as she always has.*
- What stands in the way? *An empty boat on her rocks, and Cyrus Hale, the harbor master, who knows more than he says.*
- What do they stand to lose? *The light, and the last of what she believes about the night Eli died.*
- How might it end? *She lets Ana Ruiz stay.*

She clicks **Plant it**. The Story Brain opens with one character, *Marisol Vega*, and every answer in pencil.

## Where you land: Where was I?

When the first hour is over, your novel has a home. **Where was I?** is the novel's home page: the first view in the list on the right, with *your novel's home page* under its name. Every time you come back to this book, Story Canon opens it there (unless you just started it with **Start writing**, **Set it up first** or **Start from a manuscript or story bible**, which open the Writing Room, Novel setup or Import and export the first time).

Its top says so: *This is (your title)'s home page.* Below that it shows the one next thing to do, with an orange **Write** button, and then, on four numbered pages (**Today**, **Still open**, **This week** and **The shelf**), everything else you would otherwise have to remember. Whenever you lose your place, click **Where was I?**.

The whole page is taught, section by section, in the next chapter: [Where was I? Your novel's home page](#where-was-i-your-novels-home-page).
