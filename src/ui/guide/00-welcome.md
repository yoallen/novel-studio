# Welcome to Story Canon

Story Canon is a program for planning and writing a novel. It holds your manuscript, every idea you have about your story, and how sure you are about each one. It works like a notebook that never loses a page and always knows which pages are settled and which are still in pencil.

This chapter gives you the one big idea the whole program rests on, then shows you how to get around. Read it first. Everything else in the guide builds on it.

## What Story Canon is, and the idea behind it

Printed on the first page of the program, under its name, is a motto: *Story Canon remembers. The author decides.*

**Canon** is a word readers use for what is officially true in a story. If it's canon, it happened. Story Canon is named for that: it keeps track of what is canon in your book, what might become canon, and what never will.

The motto describes how the work is split between you and the program.

- **Story Canon remembers.** Every idea you write down, every chat you bring in, every change you make, with who said it and when. Nothing is quietly thrown away. Months from now you can ask "where did this come from?" and get an answer.
- **The author decides.** Only you settle anything. The program never turns a maybe into a yes for you. An idea from a friend, from ChatGPT, or from the writing partner built into Story Canon stays a suggestion until you choose it, on purpose, with a click.

**Why this matters to your book.** Most notebooks treat every line the same. Over months of planning, an idea you once said "maybe" to starts to feel decided, simply because it has been sitting there so long. Then you build three chapters on something you never actually chose. Story Canon keeps the difference visible, so a maybe always looks like a maybe.

**Example.** A writer is planning a novel about a lighthouse keeper. In March she types, "Maybe the keeper's brother drowned here?" In June she has forgotten that was only a guess. In Story Canon, that line still looks like pencil in June, with "March 3" beside it. She sees at a glance that she never decided it.

## How to use this guide and Help

You do not need to read this guide front to back. Read this chapter and the next one, *Starting a novel*. Then open the chapter for whatever screen you are on when you need it.

### Opening Help

The guide lives inside the program. There are three ways to open it:

1. Press **F1** on your keyboard, from anywhere.
2. Click the **Help** tab, the last tab on the right edge of the window.
3. Click a **Learn more** style link beside something on screen (see below). Help opens at the section that explains that exact thing.

While a sprint is running, F1 does nothing, because a sprint keeps you on the page.

### What is on the Help screen

- **Search the guide** (the box at the top left). Type two or more letters, such as *beat* or *decided*. A list of matching sections appears under the box, each with the chapter it is in and a few words around the match. Click a section name to jump there. Clear the box to see the chapter list again.
- **The chapter list** (under the search box). Click a chapter to open it. The chapter you are reading is underlined, and its sections are listed beneath it so you can jump straight to one.
- **The chapter itself** (the large area on the right). Links inside the guide stay inside Help.

To leave Help, click any other tab on the right edge, or the pink **Return to Writing** ribbon if you came from the page.

### Teaching beside the work

You do not have to come to Help to learn. The program teaches right where you are working:

- **Why it matters.** Under many blanks on a record page is a one-line explanation of what the blank is for, followed by a **Why it matters** link. Click it to fold out more: how often books need it, the common mistake, and an example. Click **Less** to fold it away.
- **Not the same as its neighbors.** When two blanks sound alike (such as *Goal* and *What they think they want*), a short note under one says how they differ.
- **About characters: what they are and how to use them** (and the same for every other kind of record). A fold-out at the top of a record page or a list. It opens the first time you visit, and stays folded once you close it. At its end, **Read the full chapter in Help** opens this guide at the matching part of [Every kind and every field](#every-kind-and-every-field).
- **How this view works.** A fold-out at the top of the checking views in Narrative Zoom, explaining what the view shows and where its information comes from.

**The common mistake** is reading all of this before you start. You don't need to. Write first, and open the teaching when a blank puzzles you.

## Records and facts: the idea every screen rests on

Two words come up on almost every screen. Learn them once and the rest of the program makes sense.

### What a record is

A **record** is one thing in your story that gets its own page. A character is a record. So is a place, a relationship, a clue, a question you have, a scene card, a song on your book's soundtrack. Think of a record as an index card with a name written across the top.

- **Where you find records:** in the **Story Brain** tab. The list on the left groups them by kind (Characters, Places, Clues and so on), with how many you have of each.
- **What a record page holds:** the name at the top, a free **Notes** box, and a set of labeled blanks grouped under headings. A character has blanks such as *Role*, *Who they are* and *What they stand to lose*. A place has different ones. The kind of record decides which blanks it offers.
- **Why it helps:** everything about one person or place sits on one page, so you stop hunting through old notebooks for her eye color.

### What a fact is

A **fact** is one thing you wrote in one blank of a record. If Kevin's *Role* blank says "Co-lead of the A story," that line is a fact.

A fact is more than its words. Each one carries three things with it, shown in the **margin** to its right:

| In the margin | What it tells you |
| --- | --- |
| The status, in small capital letters (such as **DECIDED**) | How sure you are. This is the big idea of the next section. |
| Where it came from (such as *From Story Bible v5* or *Suggested by ChatGPT*) | Who said it. Facts you type yourself have no line here, because they are yours. |
| The date and time | When it was written down. |

Below those are the buttons for what you can do with that fact, such as **Decide this**, **Set aside** and **Where did this come from?**

A blank can hold more than one fact. That is on purpose: you can keep two or three possible answers side by side while you make up your mind.

**Example.** The lighthouse keeper's record has a blank called *What they stand to lose*. It holds two facts in pencil: "The lighthouse itself" and "Her brother's memory." Both stay until she chooses one.

**When the Library says "212 records, 495 facts"** it means 212 pages and 495 entries written on them.

## The five statuses: how sure you are

Every fact has a **status**: a mark of how sure you are about it. There are five, plus one for things you have retired. Each status looks different on the page, the way ink looks different from pencil, so you can tell them apart without reading a word.

The record pages show a small key near the top, under the record's name: *Decided (inked)*, *Developing (pencil)*, *Still open (a blank)*, *Example, never canon*, *Suggestion (a slip, tucked in)*. That key is a reminder of the marks below.

| Status | Looks like | Means |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Decided** | Solid black text with a small black square | You have made up your mind. |
| **Developing** | Gray text, a short dashed mark, a dashed underline | You are still working on it. |
| **Still open** | Slanted gray words on a ruled line, a short solid dash | A question you have not answered yet. |
| **Example** | Slanted text with the ❦ flower mark | An illustration only. Never part of your story. |
| **Suggestion** | A white slip with a purple left edge, tilted slightly | Someone else's idea, waiting for your choice. |
| **Set aside** | Faded and crossed out, folded away | Retired, but kept. |

### Decided: the inked square

- **What it means:** you have made up your mind. This is canon.
- **How it looks:** solid black text with a small black square in front, like something written in pen. The margin says **DECIDED** with its own small square.
- **Why it helps:** you can build on it without second-guessing. The checking tools and the collaborator treat it as true.
- **What you can do:** **Not decided after all** turns it back into pencil (Developing). **Set aside** retires it.
- **The common mistake:** marking something decided just so the page looks finished. If you aren't sure, leave it in pencil. Pencil costs nothing.
- **Example:** "Kevin is the first to discover Francine's ledger." The author of *I Declare War* said so plainly, so it is in ink.

### Developing: the pencil dash

- **What it means:** you are still working on it.
- **How it looks:** gray text with a dashed underline and a short dashed mark in front, like a pencil note. The margin says **DEVELOPING**.
- **Why it helps:** you can keep several versions side by side and compare them. Nothing forces you to choose before you're ready.
- **What you can do:** **Decide this** makes it Decided. If the blank already had a decided answer, the old one is kept in the blank's history, never thrown away.
- **The common mistake:** thinking two pencil answers in one blank are a problem to fix. They are just a choice you haven't made yet.
- **Example:** the lighthouse keeper's two possible losses above, both in pencil, both alive.

### Still open: the blank line

- **What it means:** a question that matters, and that you have not answered yet.
- **How it looks:** slanted gray words sitting on a ruled line, like a form waiting to be filled, with a short solid dash in front. The margin says **STILL OPEN**. If nothing has been written at all, the line reads *Still open: nothing written yet* and offers **Write it**.
- **Why it helps:** "I haven't decided" is different from "I forgot." Marking a question open says *this matters, and it isn't settled*. The program lists it for you (on **Where was I**, under **Questions still open**) and never fills it in by guessing.
- **What you can do:** **Write it** opens the blank so you can answer. If the open line already has words (often the question itself, or a note about what is undecided), **Decide this** settles that wording as your answer.
- **The common mistake:** feeling you must fill in every open line before you start writing. You don't. Many answers turn up while you write.
- **Example:** "Starting a rival show: Kevin's riff is a joke, not proof that he starts one." The author hasn't settled it, so it sits on the line, open.

### Example: slanted, with the ❦ mark

- **What it means:** an illustration of what an answer *could* look like. An example is never part of your story.
- **How it looks:** slanted (italic) text in a book typeface with a small flower mark ❦ in front. The margin says **EXAMPLE**. Elsewhere in the program, a sample line may be labeled *One way it could be done*: that is an example too.
- **Why it helps:** an example teaches you the shape of an answer without sneaking in as the answer.
- **What you can do:** **Set aside**, or **Where did this come from?** There is no **Decide this** beside an example. That is not broken: an example can't become canon. If you like it, write your own version in the same blank and mark that one Decided.
- **The common mistake:** reading a vivid example as the plan. Vivid is not the same as decided.
- **Example:** "Example goal: win the war, prove Anthony is sabotaging him" on Kevin's page. It shows what a goal can sound like. It does not say what Kevin's goal is.

### Suggestion: a slip tucked in

- **What it means:** an idea that came from someone other than you: the collaborator inside Story Canon, or a chat with ChatGPT or Claude that was brought in from your planning.
- **How it looks:** a white slip of paper with a purple left edge, tilted very slightly, as if tucked between the pages rather than written on them. The margin says **SUGGESTION** and names who suggested it, such as *Suggested by ChatGPT*.
- **Why it helps:** you see the idea right where it applies, but it never counts as yours until you choose it.
- **What you can do:** **Decide this** takes it in as a decision (the margin then adds *accepted by you*). **Set aside** lets it go.
- **The common mistake:** saying "sure, sounds good" to an AI in a chat and assuming that settled it. It didn't. If you want the idea, decide it here, on purpose.
- **Example:** on Kevin's page, a long slip proposes how Kevin should be on the last page. It came from ChatGPT during planning. It waits.

You can't give one of your own facts the Suggestion status. When you add a fact yourself, the status menu offers Decided, Developing, Still open and Example. Suggestions only arrive from somewhere else.

### Set aside: retired, never deleted

- **What it means:** you have let this go, but it is kept.
- **Where it goes:** it disappears from the blank. Under that blank a link appears: **Show earlier and set-aside versions** with a count in brackets. Click it to see retired facts, faded and crossed out, along with earlier decisions that were replaced.
- **What you can do:** **Bring back** beside a set-aside fact returns it to pencil (Developing).
- **Why it matters:** nothing in Story Canon is ever silently deleted. You can let go of an idea without fear.

### Why statuses matter to your book

Statuses are not decoration. The rest of the program reads them.

- **Where was I** lists your open questions and your latest decisions, so you can see what is settled.
- **Choices ahead** in the Story Brain collects every open question, every blank with competing pencil answers, and every suggestion waiting.
- **The Board and the checking views** in Narrative Zoom count a scene card as placed only when its spot is decided, so they never report a problem based on a guess.
- **The collaborator** treats decided facts as true, treats pencil as possibilities, never fills in an open line by guessing, and never offers an example as a fact.

So the more honest your statuses, the more useful everything else becomes.

### Changing a status, step by step

To add a fact with a status:

1. On a record page, find the blank you want and click **Add** at its right.
2. Type your words. Some blanks offer clickable suggestions under the box; your own words are always welcome.
3. In the small menu under the box, choose **Decided**, **Developing**, **Still open** or **Example**.
4. Click **Save**.

To change a fact you already have, use the buttons in its margin: **Decide this**, **Not decided after all**, or **Set aside**.

### When a status looks wrong but is working as designed

- **No Decide this beside an example.** On purpose. Examples can't become canon.
- **Two or three answers in one blank, all in pencil.** On purpose. They wait until you choose.
- **Your status words don't match this guide.** You can rename every status in **Settings**, under **Words for decisions** (for example, "Inked" instead of "Decided"). Your words show everywhere, and the marks stay the same.
- **A decided answer vanished after you decided a different one.** It was replaced, not lost. Click **Show earlier and set-aside versions** under that blank.

## Who the collaborator is

**The collaborator** is a writing partner built into Story Canon, powered by Claude, an AI made by the company Anthropic. It is optional and off until you turn it on, novel by novel, in **Settings** under **The collaborator**, using your own Claude API key (a private code, like a password, that you get from Anthropic and pay for by use). Before it answers, it reads your decisions, your guardrails (rules your story must never break), your open questions and your **About you** answers, so it knows your book and how you like to be helped. It never types into your manuscript, it never decides anything, and everything it offers arrives as a suggestion slip that waits for you. Some screens and imported planning call an AI "the assistant" or name ChatGPT; those are the outside chats your planning came from. Inside Story Canon, the collaborator is the one partner.

## Getting around

Story Canon is drawn to look like an open book. The purple around the edges is the book's cloth cover. The cream area in the middle is the page. Whatever you open appears on the page.

Around the page are four things you will use all the time:

- **The tabs** down the right edge, like the cut-out tabs on the side of a dictionary.
- **The story strip** across the top, a tiny map of your whole book.
- **The header**, the line across the top of the page.
- **Keyboard keys** for the things you do most.

The words *Story Canon* in slanted type at the top right corner of the cover are just the program's name. They are not a button.

### The right-hand tabs

Each tab opens one area of the program. The tab you are on turns cream and bold, so you always know where you are. The five large tabs are the main rooms. Under the slanted word *and* are five smaller ones.

| Tab | What it is for |
| --- | --- |
| **Where was I** | Your home page for this novel. The next thing to write (with a **Write** button), the book's stage and draft, your writing days, notes you left for later, open questions, latest decisions and this week's work. A novel opens here. |
| **The Writing Room** | Where you write. Chapters and scenes on the left, the page in the middle, the scene's plan on the right. Clicking this tab always lands you on a page: the scene you had open, or your next planned scene, or a blank new scene if there is nothing else. |
| **Story Brain** | Everything you know about your story, one record at a time: characters, places, clues, questions and more. |
| **Narrative Zoom** | The shape of your plot, from the whole book down to one scene: the Zoom view, the Board of scene cards, beat placement, alternate structures, and the checking views. |
| **Conversations** | Your talks with the collaborator, and chats you brought in from ChatGPT or Claude. |
| **Search** | Look through everything at once: your pages, every record, every conversation, every document you brought in. |
| **Story Trail** | The history of every change to your story, newest first, with **Undo** beside each one, and **Deleted things**, where anything you deleted waits to be restored. |
| **Import and export** | Bringing files in (a manuscript, a planning document, a spreadsheet, an editor's changes) and sending your book out (Word, PDF, ebook and more). |
| **Settings** | Choices for this novel and for all your novels, including **Novel setup**, **About you**, the collaborator, goals and backups. |
| **Help** | This guide. |

**Pages that light up another tab.** A few pages are reached from inside another one, and the tab they belong to lights up while you are there:

- **Novel setup** and **My methods** light up **Settings**.
- **Deleted things** lights up **Story Trail**.
- **Drafts** and the **Writing Trail** light up **The Writing Room**.

**Example.** You are writing a scene and can't remember the name of the lighthouse keeper's dog. Click **Story Brain**, then her record. When you have the answer, click the pink ribbon at the top to go straight back to your sentence.

### The story strip: your book in one line

The thin line across the purple cover, above the page, is a map of your whole book from the first page (left) to the last (right). It is on every screen once a novel is open.

**What it shows depends on your book.**

- **If your novel has a beat sheet** (a list of the turning points a story of a certain shape tends to have, such as *Save the Cat*), the strip shows your acts and beats. An **act** is a large section of the story. A **beat** is one turning point.
- **If your novel has no beat sheet** (you find the story as you write), the strip shows your chapters instead, each one as wide as its share of your words.

**Every mark on the strip:**

| Mark | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| Slanted words above the line (*Act One*, *Act Two, first half*, *Act Two, second half*, *Act Three*) | The names of the acts, each over a faint band showing how much of the book it covers. In a book without a beat sheet, the chapter names show here instead (short chapters may go unlabeled). |
| A thin white tick, or a faint shaded band with a line at its left | One beat, placed where it falls in the book. Short beats are ticks. Beats that stretch over a stretch of pages are bands. |
| A small solid cream box above a beat | One scene card **placed** there: decided, this scene goes here. In a book without a beat sheet, one box stands for one scene in that chapter. |
| A small hollow box (an outline only) | One scene card that **might go** there. It is in pencil: you haven't decided its spot yet. |
| A pink pin with a round head | Where the scene you have open falls in the book. |

Up to five boxes show above any one beat. A beat with more still shows five.

**Pointing and clicking.** Hold the mouse over a tick and a small label appears with the beat's name and what is there, such as *Fun and Games, 3 might go here*. Click anywhere on the strip to open **Narrative Zoom**.

**When the strip looks broken but isn't:**

- **No pink pin.** The pin shows only when a scene is open in the Writing Room and its scene card is decided on a beat. A scene that only *might* go somewhere has no fixed spot to pin. In a book without a beat sheet, the pin shows the chapter you are in.
- **The strip says only "Chapter 1".** Your novel has no beat sheet yet. Choose one in **Novel setup** under **My storytelling toolkit**, and the acts and beats appear.
- **The strip is empty.** You are in the Library, or the novel has no chapters and no beat sheet yet.

**Example.** In *I Declare War*, three hollow boxes float above *Fun and Games*. Three scene cards might go there. None is decided yet, which is why there are no solid boxes.

### The header: the line across the top of the page

From left to right:

- **Return to Writing** (the pink bookmark ribbon). See the next section.
- **Library.** Takes you to the shelf of all your novels. See the chapter *Starting a novel*.
- **The book switcher**: your novel's title in slanted letters with a small ▾ after it. Click it for a list of every novel on your shelf, each with its stage in gray. Click another title to open that novel right where you left it, without going through the Library. Move the mouse off the list to close it.
- **The name of the page you are on**, in slanted purple, such as *Story Brain* or *Novel setup*. It is a label, not a button. It is hidden in the Writing Room, where the page itself is enough.
- **Saved** (in the Writing Room only). A small circle and a word that tell you whether your typing is safe. There is no Save button for your writing, because it saves by itself as you type.
- **Capture.** A button for catching a passing thought. See below.

**The Saved indicator, word by word:**

| You see | It means |
| --- | --- |
| A filled circle and **Saved** | Everything you typed is kept. |
| **Saving…** | It is being kept right now. Keep typing. |
| **Not saved yet** | You typed in the last moment; it will save in a second. |
| A red message | Something went wrong saving. Your typing is still on screen. Don't close the program; the message says what to do. |

### The Return to Writing ribbon

When you leave the page to look something up, a pink bookmark ribbon hangs from the top left of the page with the words **Return to Writing**. Click it, or press **Ctrl+Shift+W**, and you are back in the scene you were writing.

- **When it appears:** once you have been on the page, or once the last scene you opened has words in it. It hides while you are in the Writing Room, because you are already there.
- **Why it helps:** writing gets interrupted all the time. You check a name, glance at the plan, answer a question. The ribbon means a detour never costs you your place.
- **What it remembers:** each time you leave the page, Story Canon notes where you went, as a *detour*. Your detours are listed on **Where was I** and in the **Writing Trail**. Returning closes them.

### Capture: catching a thought without losing your place

An idea arrives mid-sentence. Don't stop to file it. Catch it.

1. Click **Capture** at the top right of the page, or press **Ctrl+Shift+N**.
2. Type the thought in the box.
3. Choose what it is. The line under the choices says where each one goes:
   - **Just a thought**: anything, unsorted. It waits among your captured thoughts until you file it as a question or an idea, or leave it be. If you are not sure, use this one.
   - **A question I have**: something you need to find out or decide. It goes to your questions, to answer later.
   - **An idea I am thinking about**: something you might use, such as a scene, a twist or a line. It goes to your ideas.
4. If you are in the Writing Room, two more choices appear. **Attach to this scene** (ticked at first) ties the thought to the scene you are writing. Then choose **Resolve later**, or **I need this to keep writing** if it is blocking you.
5. Click **Save it**, or press **Ctrl+Enter**. **Cancel** or **Esc** closes the box without saving. Saving an empty box simply closes it.

Your words are kept exactly as written. Unsorted thoughts wait on **Where was I** under **Thoughts waiting to be sorted**. Everything you capture is also in the Story Brain, under **Questions and ideas**.

**The common mistake** is stopping to sort the thought properly right then. That is how a ten-second note becomes a twenty-minute detour. Capture it and go back.

### Handy keys for the whole program

Press the keys together. **Ctrl** is the key in the bottom-left corner of your keyboard.

| Keys | What they do | Where |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **F1** | Open Help | Anywhere |
| **Ctrl+Shift+N** | Capture a thought | Anywhere in a novel, even during a sprint |
| **Ctrl+K** | Open Search | Anywhere in a novel |
| **Ctrl+Shift+W** | Return to Writing, to the scene you were in | Anywhere in a novel |
| **Ctrl+Shift+F** | Focus: hide everything but the page | The Writing Room |
| **Esc** | Leave Focus, or close a small window such as Capture | The Writing Room, any dialog |
| **Ctrl+Shift+L** | Leave a note for later, \[\[like this\]\], and keep writing | On the page |
| **Ctrl+Enter** | Save what you typed in Capture | The Capture box |
| **Ctrl+Z** | Undo your last bit of typing | On the page |
| **Ctrl+Y** | Redo it | On the page |
| **Ctrl and +**, **Ctrl and -**, **Ctrl and 0** | Make everything bigger, smaller, or back to normal size | Anywhere |

To undo a change to your story itself (a decision, a deleted record), use **Story Trail**, where every change has its own **Undo**.
