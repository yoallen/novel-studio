# Characters and arcs

A character page holds everything you know about one person in your story: their job in the book, who they are in the world, how they change, how they look and sound, what they want and what stands in the way. This chapter walks down a character page in order, explains where each part comes from and what it is for, and teaches the craft behind the tools: K.M. Weiland's character arcs, Randy Ingermanson's character questions, Robert McKee's forces of antagonism, and Angela Ackerman and Becca Puglisi's thesauri of wounds, traits and emotions.

You don't need any of that craft vocabulary to start. Every term is explained the first time it appears.

The record page itself (the title, the margin, statuses, Add, Connections) works the same for every kind of record and is explained in [The Story Brain](#the-story-brain). Every single field on a character is explained in [Characters: every field](#characters-every-field). This chapter ties them together.

## Opening and starting a character

- **To open a character:** click **Story Brain** on the right edge, then **Characters** in the left list, then the character's name.
- **To start one:** on the Characters list, type a name in the box at the bottom (*A name is enough to start*) and click **Add character**. The new page opens.
- **Plant the seed** on a brand-new novel's **Where was I** page also starts your lead character from six short questions, all in pencil.

The Characters list shows, for each person, how many facts are decided, developing and still open, and a few telling facts such as *Narrative importance: main*. A character marked *Kept for you* shows the mark in pink.

**The common mistake** is treating an empty page as a to-do list. A minor character with three good facts is complete. Depth is measured against how much the character matters to the book, never against how many fields are filled.

## A character page in order

From top to bottom, a character page has:

1. **The name, Kept for you, Ask about this, the legend, ON THIS PAGE, the About fold-out, and Notes.** These are the same on every record: see [A record page from top to bottom](#a-record-page-from-top-to-bottom).
2. **Suggestions waiting for you**, if the collaborator has suggested something for this character.
3. **Role in the story**, always first: their job in the book.
4. **Identity**, always second: who they are in the world.
5. **Arcs**: how they change, with **Add an arc** and **Read the Lies as a pair with**.
6. **Guardrails**: what must stay true about them.
7. **Across time**, once they have a birth year, a fixed age, or other selves.
8. **Fill in one line**, a small button for low-energy days, while some everyday groups still have empty fields.
9. **The other groups**: those that hold something first, then the empty ones (folded), in this order: Goal and motive, What works against them, Beyond human, Appearance, Language and voice, Physical, Personality, Environmental, Psychological, Wound and its fallout, Traits and flaws, Depth lenses, Contradiction layers, Comparisons, Writing aids (private), My fields. Groups that come from methods you have not turned on are left out.
10. **Interview**: questions you ask the character, answered in their voice.
11. **Other facts**, always last of the groups: the catch-all.
12. **Add your own field**, **Connections**, and **Delete**.

On a novel you haven't set up yet, a gray line after the groups says *More places to write come with the methods you choose in Novel setup*, with an **Open Novel setup** link.

The page can be long. Use **ON THIS PAGE** near the top to jump straight to any section.

## Role in the story: the character's job

**Role in the story** is always the first group, and the program says to fill it first. It answers: what is this person *for* in this book?

| Field | What to write | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Role** | Their structural job: lead, co-lead, B-story lead (who carries the second storyline, often the one that teaches the theme), supporting, narrator, secret keeper, or a hidden engine driving events offstage. | Tells you how much page time and inner life they need. |
| **Who they are** | A sentence or two, the way you'd describe them to a friend who will meet them tomorrow. | Your anchor in chapter twenty when you're unsure how they'd react. |
| **Narrative importance** | Main, supporting or minor. | A measure of weight, not worth. Depth is judged against it. |
| **Narrates?** | Whether they tell part of the story, and how: first person ("I") or close third ("she," from inside her head). | Settles whose voice carries which chapters. |
| **Viewpoint withheld until** | For a secret keeper: the beat before which the reader never gets inside their head. | Keeps a secret from leaking through their thoughts. |
| **What they stand to lose** | What is gone for good if they fail. | Stakes make every obstacle tense. |
| **Last page, in one line** | Who they are on the last page, in one line, even before you know the ending. | Gives every scene a direction to push them. |

**Viewpoint withheld until, explained.** A **secret keeper** is a character who knows something the reader must not learn yet. If the reader is inside their head, the secret is hard to hide honestly. Write the name of a beat here, exactly as it appears on your Board (such as *All Is Lost*). The Story review in Narrative Zoom then asks about any scene told from inside this character's head before that beat. In *I Declare War*, Miss Francine is the secret keeper: her viewpoint is withheld until the reveal.

**What they stand to lose, done well.** Stakes are the cost of losing, not the reward for winning, and they land on this one person. "She could lose everything" is vague. "If the florist loses the contest, her mother's name comes off the sign" is a stake.

**Example.** A lighthouse keeper, Ines. *Role*: Lead. *Who they are*: "A woman who keeps a light for strangers so she never has to let anyone close." *Narrative importance*: main. *Narrates?*: Close third. *What they stand to lose*: the lighthouse, which is being automated. *Last page*: "She hands the keys to someone else, and stays anyway."

## Identity: who they are in the world

**Identity** holds the facts people most often get wrong by assuming: **Gender identity**, **Pronouns**, **Sexual orientation**, **Romantic orientation, if it differs**, **Intersex or sex characteristics, if it matters**, **Race or ethnicity**, **Heritage, nationality and roots**, **Age**, **Year born**, **Age, if it never changes**, **Faith or religion**, **Culture and community**, **Disability, chronic illness or neurodivergence**, **Work**, and **Family**.

**How it works, and why.**

- **Nothing is ever guessed.** These fields say *Not yet specified* until you write something. The program never works out a character's gender from a name, or their race from a photo of an actor, or anything from another field.
- **Your words, always.** Some fields offer a row of common answers when you click **Add** (*Woman*, *Man*, *Nonbinary*… for gender; *she/her*, *they/them*… for pronouns). Click one, or type your own words. The buttons are a convenience, never a list you must choose from.
- **Timing.** Pronouns, orientation, age, disability, work and family can change over a story, so these fields offer the **Timing** dropdown (*Before the story*, *True when the story opens*, *Happens during the story*). Someone can be a nurse before the story and unemployed during it, both decided.
- **What reads these fields.** *Gender identity* (or pronouns, if there is no gender) is what the Bechdel test in the Cast view uses. *Year born* lets the program work out the character's age in every scene that has a year. Nothing else is inferred from identity.

**The common mistake** is filling every identity field for every walk-on character. If it doesn't matter to the story, leave it unspecified. That is a choice too.

**Example.** For Ines: *Gender identity*: Woman. *Pronouns*: she/her (*True when the story opens*). *Year born*: 1961. *Work*: Assistant keeper (*Before the story*); Head keeper (*True when the story opens*).

See [Characters: Identity](#characters-identity) for every field.

## Arcs: how a character changes

An **arc** is the change a character goes through inside, from who they are at the start to who they are at the end. Plots resolve; arcs are what make readers feel the ending meant something.

Story Canon builds arcs on the teaching of **K.M. Weiland**, a novelist and writing teacher whose book *Creating Character Arcs* breaks inner change into a few linked parts. Her idea is simple and strong: a character changes because they start out believing something false, and the story forces them to test that belief until they either let it go or are destroyed by it.

### Weiland's arc, one part at a time

The parts form a chain. Each one explains the next. Learn them in this order.

**The Ghost.** Something in the character's past that shaped them, often called the **wound**. It must have happened before the story starts.

- *Why it works:* it makes the false belief (the Lie) believable. Readers forgive a character's mistakes when they understand where the mistakes come from.
- *How it fails:* a Ghost chosen for drama ("her whole family died in a fire") that has nothing to do with the Lie, or one so vague it explains nothing ("something bad happened once").
- *It need not be tragic.* It needs to be personal and convincing. Being praised only when she won, every time, for twelve years, is a Ghost.

**The Lie.** The false belief the character holds about themselves or how life works, taken from the Ghost. Once, it protected them. Now it holds them back.

- *Why it works:* it is the engine of the whole arc. It explains why the character keeps making the wrong choice, and its defeat is what the ending means.
- *How it fails:* a Lie that is only a mistake about facts ("she thinks the bank closes at five"). A Lie is a belief about how life works, and it must feel true to them.
- *Write it in their voice,* as a sentence they would say if they were honest: "If I let my guard down, I could lose everything again."

**The Want.** What the Lie makes the character chase: usually an outer goal they believe will fix things.

- *Why it works:* it drives the plot and gives the reader something to root for.
- *How it fails:* a Want that would heal them if they got it. Then there is no inner conflict.

**The Need.** What would truly heal them. Usually the opposite of the Want, and tied to letting the Lie go.

- *Why it works:* it is where the arc is heading. When the character reaches for the Need at the cost of the Want, the change becomes visible on the page.
- *How it fails:* a Need written as a moral ("she needs to learn a lesson"). A Need is a change in what they reach for.

**The Truth.** The true belief that replaces the Lie: the exact answer to it.

- *Why it works:* it is the theme of the book made personal to this one person.
- *How it fails:* a Truth that is a slogan ("love conquers all") instead of the answer to this character's Lie.

**One example, all the way through.** Ines the lighthouse keeper.

- *Ghost:* at nineteen, she lent the island's only boat to her brother, and he didn't come back.
- *Lie:* "If I let anyone depend on me, I will fail them."
- *Want:* to keep the light alone, forever, owing nothing to anyone.
- *Need:* to let the new apprentice depend on her, and to depend on him.
- *Truth:* "People are kept safe by being held, not by being kept away."

Read it back as a chain: because of the Ghost, she believes the Lie; because of the Lie, she wants the Want; what she needs is the opposite; the Truth is what she finally knows. If any link doesn't lead to the next, that link is the one to rewrite.

### The three arc types

Weiland describes three shapes an arc can take. You choose one in the arc's **Arc type** field.

| Arc type | What happens | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Positive Change** | The character starts with the Lie, fights it, and ends holding the Truth. The most common arc. | Ines lets the apprentice in. |
| **Flat** | The character already holds the Truth at the start. They don't change much; they change the world around them, and are tested hard along the way. | A new pastor who already believes in mercy, in a town that doesn't. |
| **Negative Change** | The character ends worse. Weiland describes three kinds: they learn the Truth too late and it is bitter (disillusionment), they reject the Truth and cling to the Lie (a fall), or they start with the Truth and trade it for a Lie (corruption). | A florist who learns her rival is her daughter and burns the shop anyway. |

**The common mistake** is assuming every arc must be Positive. Flat arcs drive many beloved books, and Negative arcs make tragedies. Another mistake is calling an arc Flat when the character is simply unchanged *and* changes nothing; a Flat character must still push on the world.

### The arc card on the character page

Under **Arcs**, each arc appears as a card:

- **The arc's title** in slanted purple type, such as *Kevin's arc*. Click it to open the arc's own page.
- **Open and change**, a button on the right that opens the same page. The card itself is for reading; you change an arc on its page.
- **The arc type** as a chip, such as **Positive Change**, or *Arc type not set*. Then *About trust.* (what the arc is about), or *What it is about is not set.*
- **The chain**, in columns: **Ghost**, then **which leads to the Lie**, **which leads to the Want**, **which leads to the Need**. Each shows what is written, with *(Developing)* after anything in pencil. A blank part shows as an empty ruled line; click it to open the arc's page.

The Truth, the tests and the ending are on the arc's page, not on the card.

If your novel does not use Weiland's method, the card says *Open the arc to write where it starts, how it turns and who they are at the end. Weiland's Ghost, Lie, Want and Need come with Weiland in Novel setup.* Turn on *Weiland (character arcs)* under **My storytelling toolkit** in Novel setup to get the chain. (A novel you haven't set up yet shows the chain anyway.)

### Adding an arc, step by step

1. On the character page, click **Arcs** in **ON THIS PAGE**.
2. Click **Add an arc**. A box appears: **What part of life is this arc about?**
3. Type a **value**: one word or a short phrase for the quality that will change, such as *trust*, *courage* or *belonging*. You can leave it empty.
4. Click **Add the arc**. (Click **Cancel** to stop.)

The arc opens on its own page, named for the character and the value (*Rosa Delgado: trust*), or *arc 2* if you left the value empty. The value is saved in pencil. Fill in the rest there. **Back to** *the character's name* at the top of the arc's page takes you back.

**Why the value comes first.** An arc follows one value as it changes. "Trust" is something you can watch rise and fall scene by scene; "growth" is not. A character can carry more than one arc, each on a different value: Francine in *I Declare War* has an arc on loneliness and another on respect.

### The arc's own page

The arc's page has two groups.

**Arc**: the arc's shape.

- **Value this arc is about**: the quality that changes.
- **Arc type**: click **Add** and choose **Positive Change**, **Flat**, **Negative Change** or **Not decided yet** from the buttons under the box, or write your own words.
- **Key tests and turns**: the moments where the character chooses between the Lie and the Truth. One entry per test. Let some of them fail; a character who passes every test hasn't changed, they were already fine.
- **Who they are at the end**: the inner state at the end, not the plot outcome. "Lets people in, even when it is inconvenient," not "wins the contest."
- **Still unresolved**: loose ends you know about. Some stay open on purpose.

**Lie and truth** *(Weiland)*: **Ghost**, **Lie**, **Want**, **Need** and **Truth**, as taught above.

Every field holds several ideas in pencil side by side. That is the right way to work on a Lie: write three wordings, live with them, decide one.

See [Arcs: every field](#arcs-every-field) for each field's full teaching.

### Reading two Lies as a pair

Under the arc cards, **Read the Lies as a pair with** has a dropdown of your other characters. Choose one, and a box called **The two Lies, read as a pair** shows both characters' Lies, one under the other.

**Why it helps: foils and doubles.** Two characters set side by side tell the reader something about each other.

- A **foil** is a character whose contrast makes another's traits visible. Their Lies pull in opposite directions.
- A **double** shows how alike two people are. Their Lies are the same belief in different clothes.
- A **mirror** is looser: two people alike in situation but different in what they choose.

None of these is wrong, but you should know which you have.

**The name-swap test.** Tick **Read with the names swapped**. The two Lies are shown with the characters' first names exchanged. If you can swap the names and nothing changes, the two are doubles, not foils. If you meant them to be foils, push one Lie until it pulls the other way.

The swap is only a reading test. It changes nothing you wrote. It swaps first names only, so a "her" may read oddly; read past it.

**Example.** Ines's Lie: "If I let anyone depend on me, I will fail them." The apprentice Tomas's Lie: "If nobody depends on me, I don't matter." Swapped, each sentence is plainly wrong for the other person: they are foils. Their scenes will generate friction without your having to force it.

## Guardrails for one character

Under the arcs, **Guardrails** holds what must stay true about this character, whatever else changes: *The collaborator checks every suggestion against these.*

Type a rule in **Add a guardrail** and click **Add**. Each rule shows in ink with a **Remove** button. A guardrail is a hard line, not a preference: "Anthony holds no official position in the neighborhood" is a guardrail; "Anthony is charming" belongs in his fields. See [Guardrails: rules that must stay true](#guardrails-rules-that-must-stay-true).

## Across time: ages and other selves

**Across time** appears under Guardrails once the character has a **Year born**, an **Age, if it never changes**, or another record connected as **is the same person as**.

- **The same person at other ages.** When one person appears at different ages as separate characters (the eight-year-old in the flashbacks and the forty-year-old narrator), give each self its own record and connect them with **is the same person as**. Each self's page then lists the others, youngest first, with their ages.
- **Age in each scene.** With a *Year born* on the character, a *Year* on a scene card (in its **Story time** group), and the character connected to the scene with **appears in** (or set as its viewpoint), the page lists their age in every scene. Until then it says *Give scenes a year, and connect this character to them, to see their age in each.*
- **Someone who never ages.** A ghost, an immortal, a remembered younger self: use *Age, if it never changes*, and the page says *Always 8.*

**The common mistake** is leaving the scene's year blank. Without it there is nothing to count from.

**Example.** Ines, born 1961, appears in the storm scene, *Year 1987*: the page lists her at 26. Her younger self, a separate record connected as *is the same person as*, is *Always 19* in the flashback where the boat goes out.

### Goal and motive: Randy Ingermanson's character questions

**Who he is.** Randy Ingermanson is a novelist and teacher best known for the **Snowflake Method**, a way of designing a novel by growing it outward from one sentence. One of its early steps is a short sheet for each main character. Story Canon's **Goal and motive** group is that sheet.

**The fields, taught in order:**

- **Values.** What the character holds most dear. Ingermanson's prompt: finish "Nothing is more important than ___" as many ways as you can. This field is a list: keep every answer.
  - *Why it works:* the two or three values that conflict with each other are the engine of the character. Someone who values both loyalty and honesty will be torn when a friend lies.
  - *How it fails:* values that never collide. Then nothing inside the character is at stake.
- **Ambition.** The abstract hunger underneath everything: to be respected, to be safe, to matter.
- **Goal.** The concrete, reachable thing they are trying to get in this story. You should be able to picture the moment they win it.
  - *Why the two are separate:* the goal is what they chase; the ambition is why it matters. The gap between them is where the truest choices come from. A character who wins the goal and finds the ambition still hungry has a story.
- **Conflict.** What stands between them and the goal, in a line.
- **Epiphany.** What they learn by the end.

**Example.** Delphine the florist. *Values*: nothing is more important than her mother's name; than doing the work right; than never asking for help. *Ambition*: to be taken seriously by the town that pitied her mother. *Goal*: win the cathedral wedding contract. *Conflict*: Marisol, who is better connected. *Epiphany*: her mother's name was never the shop; it was how she treated people.

**How it connects.** Plant the seed's answers to "What do they want?" and "What stands in the way?" go into *Goal* and *Conflict*. Ingermanson's Goal is often the same thing as Weiland's Want seen from outside: the Want is the chase the Lie drives; the Goal is the target as the plot sees it.

See [Characters: Ingermanson](#characters-ingermanson).

### What works against them: Robert McKee's forces of antagonism

**Who he is.** Robert McKee is a screenwriting teacher whose book *Story* is one of the most widely read books on structure. One of his central ideas is the **principle of antagonism**: a protagonist and a story can only be as interesting as the forces against them.

**The idea, taught in two parts.**

**First, the value and how far it can fall.** Every story is about a **value**: something that can swing positive or negative, such as love, justice, respect or safety. McKee shows that a value doesn't just have an opposite; it has a ladder down:

1. **The positive value**: respect.
2. **The contrary**, a step down but not the opposite: disrespect.
3. **The contradictory**, the plain opposite: humiliation.
4. **The negation of the negation**, the value at its very darkest, where something is wrong twice over: contempt wearing a polite face, honor to a man's face and scorn behind his back.

McKee's point is that most stories stop at the opposite. Great ones push the character all the way down to the fourth step before the end, so the climb back means something.

**Second, the three levels of opposition.** The forces against a character come from three places:

- **Inner**: from inside them: fear, pride, a wound, the Lie.
- **Personal**: from people close to them: family, lovers, friends, rivals.
- **Extra-personal**: from the wider world: institutions, society, nature, chance.

A full story uses all three.

**The fields:**

- **The value they live by**: the value under pressure in their own story.
- **Inner force**, **Personal force**, **Extra-personal force**: what works against them at each level.
- **The worst form of their value**: the negation of the negation, set for your book's tone. A comedy's darkest point is different from a tragedy's.

**Why it helps.** It shows you whether your antagonism is strong enough. If one of the three forces is empty, or the value never reaches its worst form, the middle of the book may sag.

**The common mistake** is making the villain the only force. In *I Declare War*, the planning says "the strongest force against Kevin is Kevin": the inner force is the biggest.

**Example.** Ines. *Value*: trust. *Inner*: her belief she will fail anyone who depends on her. *Personal*: the apprentice who won't stop asking for help. *Extra-personal*: the lighthouse board automating the light. *Worst form*: trusting no one, not even herself, and calling it duty.

**Conflict versus What works against them.** Ingermanson's *Conflict* is what blocks the goal, in a line. McKee's group breaks the opposition into its three levels and follows the value to the bottom. Use either or both.

See [Characters: Forces of antagonism](#characters-forces-of-antagonism).

### Wound and its fallout, Traits and flaws: the Ackerman and Puglisi thesauri

**Who they are.** Angela Ackerman and Becca Puglisi run the Writers Helping Writers site and wrote a series of reference books called thesauri. *The Emotion Thesaurus* lists how each emotion shows in the body, the mind and behavior. *The Emotional Wound Thesaurus* traces painful past events through the beliefs and behaviors they leave behind. *The Positive Trait Thesaurus* and *The Negative Trait Thesaurus* explain where traits come from and how they show. All of them teach one thing: **show, don't tell.** Don't write "she was afraid"; write what fear does to her hands.

Turning on *The Writers Helping Writers thesauri (Ackerman and Puglisi)* in Novel setup adds three groups.

**Wound and its fallout**, on each character: one defining wound followed through its effects.

- **The wounding event**: what happened, and how old they were. (Weiland calls this the Ghost.)
- **What they came to believe because of it**: the false belief (Weiland's Lie).
- **What they fear will happen again**.
- **What they avoid or guard against now**: a list.
- **How it shows in what they do**: a list of behaviors the reader can see.
- **What sets it off**: a list of triggers.
  - *Why it works:* the last three fields are things you can put in a scene. A reader never needs to be told the wound if they watch her refuse every offered hand.
  - *How it fails:* a wound that explains nothing she does on the page.

**Traits and flaws**, on each character: **Positive traits** (with where each came from, and how it shows without being named) and **Flaws** (with what each one protects them from, and what it costs). A flaw usually protects against the wound.

**Emotion on the page**, on each scene card: the viewpoint character's main emotion in the scene, **What the body shows**, **What they feel inside**, **What their thoughts do**, and **What they try to hide, and what leaks out**. See [Scene cards: Emotion on the page](#scene-cards-emotion-on-the-page).

**Example.** Ines. *Wounding event*: lent the boat at nineteen; her brother drowned. *Came to believe*: if anyone depends on me, I'll fail them. *Avoids*: lending anything; teaching. *How it shows*: does every task herself, even when it costs her sleep; won't let Tomas hold the ladder. *What sets it off*: anyone asking "Can I borrow…?" *Flaw*: self-reliance, which protects her from failing anyone and costs her every friendship on the island.

See [Characters: Wound and its fallout](#characters-wound-and-its-fallout) and [Characters: Traits and flaws](#characters-traits-and-flaws).

### Appearance and Language and voice

**Appearance** has *The overall look*, *Skin tone*, *Hair*, *Eyes*, *Height*, *Build and body*, *Distinctive features* and *How they dress*. Use these to stay consistent across a long draft (her eyes were gray in chapter two), not to write a head-to-toe description on the page. Like identity, they say *Not yet specified* until you write them, and hair, build and dress can carry timing.

**Language and voice** has *Languages they speak* (and how well), *Accent and how they sound*, and *Sayings and verbal habits*. Rhythm and word choice carry a voice better than spelled-out dialect.

### Personality, Physical, Environmental and Psychological

These four are the everyday texture of a person:

- **Personality**: *Temperament* (their natural emotional weather), *Humor*, *Habits and props*, *Strengths*, *Weaknesses*.
- **Physical**: *Physical notes*: health, energy, how they move.
- **Environmental**: *Home and neighborhood*, and *History* (past events, one per entry).
- **Psychological**: *Fears*, *Wounds*, *Desires*, *Beliefs*: the raw material of motivation.

They are the groups **Fill in one line** draws from.

### Depth lenses

**Depth lenses** are questions that test whether a character is alive beyond their job in the plot. A **lens** here just means a way of looking at the character. Use them on anyone who feels flat.

- **What they think they want**: the want they'd admit to.
- **What drives them underneath**: the need they don't see in themselves.
- **Under pressure**: how they behave under pressure, uncertainty, success and failure.
- **Comic engine**: what reliably makes them funny on the page (the flaw, habit or contradiction that keeps producing laughs).
- **Voice**: how they sound as a whole: vocabulary, rhythm, what they notice, how they joke.
- **With different people**: how they act with a rival, an ally, family, a stranger.
- **Agency**: what they make happen, rather than react to.

**The common mistake** is a character who only reacts. If *Agency* is hard to fill, the character is being pushed around by the plot.

See [Characters: Depth lenses](#characters-depth-lenses).

### Contradiction layers

Complex characters live in the gaps between who they seem to be and who they are. This group has four fields in two pairs: **Public identity** beside **Private identity**, and **What they do** beside **Why they really do it**.

**Example.** Anthony in *I Declare War*: public, the unbothered local anchor; private, a man terrified of being forgotten. What he does: needles Kevin for sport. Why: Kevin is one of the few people who always notices him.

### Comparisons and Writing aids

- **Comparisons**: *Like…*, a real person or character whose qualities you are borrowing. Note which qualities, so it never becomes a copy.
- **Writing aids (private)**: tools just for you, marked *(private, never exported)*. **Casting tuning fork** is an actor whose voice and tempo help you hear the character while you write (a tuning fork gives a musician the right note). It is never a description of the character. **Theme music** and **Playlist** are songs that put you back inside them.

Private fields never leave the program in a manuscript export. To put a song *in the book*, add it to the **Soundtrack** and connect it with **is the theme song of** instead.

### Other facts: the catch-all

**Other facts about them** is a list for anything true about the character that has no field of its own: a birthday, a car, the name of a childhood dog. A book you bring in puts facts here that arrived without a place, each starting with a short label of its own (*Stephen King / car:*, *Catastrophizing:*).

It sits last on purpose, so the page above stays in order. When a fact here has a proper home (a fear belongs in *Fears*), write it there and **Set aside** the copy here.

## Interviewing a character

Near the bottom of the page, **Interview** *the character's name* lets you ask the character questions and answer in their own voice.

**Why it works.** A character who can answer personal questions honestly is a person. One who can't is still a plot device. Answering in the first person, as them, gets you out of your own head.

**What you see.** At first, a line: *Ask* name *questions and answer in their own voice. Answers stay in pencil.* Then a button for each ready-made set of questions, each with a line about it, and a box for your own question.

### Adding a question set, step by step

1. Click **Add the questions:** followed by the set's name.
2. The set's questions appear under its name, each with a writing box that says *As* the character's name*…*
3. Click in a box and answer as the character, in their words.
4. Click anywhere outside the box. The answer is kept in pencil, as developing. Change it any time; earlier answers are kept.

Once a set is added, its button disappears, so a set can't be added twice.

### Getting to know them

Ten questions that get under the surface: the first thing they remember, who taught them how to love, what they lie about most often, what they'd never forgive, what they're afraid someone will find out, who they miss. Answer as the character, in their words, not yours.

### The villain interview

A villain exercise taught by the writer Dara Resnik. Five questions about childhood: the happiest memory, the first friend and where they are now, the biggest fear when small, what makes them cry, who hurt them most as a child.

**How to use it.** Answer as the villain, who believes they are right, helping, or saving someone, and that the cost is worth it. Then say the answers out loud without looking. If the villain can't answer as honestly as your hero, you have written an obstacle, not a person.

**Example.** The lighthouse board chairman who wants the light automated. *What makes you cry?* "The list of keepers' names on the chapel wall. Every one of them died out there alone. I am trying to make sure the list ends." He is still the antagonist. Now he is also right about something.

### Your own questions

Type a question in **A question of your own** and click **Ask**. It joins the page under **Your own questions**, with its own answer box.

## Relationship pages

A **relationship** record holds what is between two or more characters. Open **Relationships** in the Story Brain's left list.

**Making one.** Either add it on the Relationships list (*Add relationship*) and then connect each person with **relates to**, or let the Cast view make it for you (see below), which connects the people at once.

**What's on the page, top to bottom:**

- **Between** *the people's names*, under the title, each a link to their page. Until you connect them it says *Connect the people in it under Connections (relates to), and they are named here.*
- **Where they stand**: Trust, Respect, Closeness, Resentment, Dependence, Rivalry, and Balance of power. A word or a short line is enough, such as "high, and slipping." These change over a story, so each has **Timing**: add a new entry as it changes and mark when each is true.
- **Underneath** (folded until used): what they share underneath, the opposition on the surface, where the relationship is really going, hidden influence, false assumptions, and who knows what the other doesn't.
- **Between them**: **What they call each other** (names nobody else uses; the hint's example, *Tabs and Tee*, is a pair of nicknames) and **Words only they share**.
- **Connections**, with **The people in it** at the top.

On each person's page, the relationships they are in are listed under **is in these relationships** in Connections.

**The common mistake** is describing a relationship once and never tracking how it moves. A relationship that changes over the book should have more than one *Trust* entry by the end, each with its timing.

**Example.** Delphine and Marisol. *Trust*: none (*True when the story opens*); grudging (*Happens during the story*). *Balance of power*: Marisol, through her connections. *What they share underneath*: both want their mothers' shops to survive. *What they call each other*: "Carnation" and "Hedge."

See [Relationships: every field](#relationships-every-field).

## Characters in scenes, and the cast checks

A character record on its own doesn't know which scenes the character is in. Three things tell Story Canon:

1. **Appears in.** Connect the character to a scene card with **appears in** (on the character's page or the card's). Books you bring in arrive with many of these already made, marked **inferred**.
2. **Viewpoint.** The scene's viewpoint character, set in the scene plan beside the page in the Writing Room.
3. **Their name on the page.** Story Canon notices a character's name in your written scenes.

**Where you see it:**

- **Connections** on the character page: *… appears in*, with every scene card.
- **Who is here** in the scene plan beside the page in the Writing Room, with each person's age once the years are known.
- **The Cast view** in Narrative Zoom, described next.
- **Who knows what** in Narrative Zoom, which uses who appears in a scene to say who learns a revealed truth there.

### The Cast view

In **Narrative Zoom**, the **Cast** view shows each character as a row and every scene and scene card as a numbered column, in reading order, grouped by act. A mark sits wherever the character is:

| Mark | Means |
| --- | --- |
| ● | the scene's viewpoint |
| ■ | in the scene, from your records (*appears in*) |
| ○ | named on the written page |

Shaded columns are planned scene cards not written yet, so you can see your cast before the book exists. Hover a column number for its title; click a mark to open that scene.

Above the grid, gentle notices say when a character has gone quiet for a long stretch, and a line names characters who are not in any scene or card yet. Below the grid:

- **Who is together**: click two or more names to see every scene they share. Tick *Only scenes where no one else is there* to find their private scenes.
- **Groups that keep meeting**: three or more characters who share two or more scenes.
- **Two at a time**: every pair that shares a scene, how often, and in which acts. Each pair, group and selection has **Their relationship** (if a record exists) or **Make a relationship record**, which makes one with the people already connected.
- **The Bechdel test**: does the book have at least two named women who talk to each other about something other than a man? The program finds the scenes where two or more women are together. Only you can say what they talk about: click **They do** or **Not here** for each. Gender comes only from what you wrote in each character's **Gender identity** (or **Pronouns**). Anyone with neither is listed as *No gender recorded for*.

**Why it helps.** A character who vanishes for six chapters, or two who are supposed to be close and never share a scene, is easy to miss in a draft and easy to see here.

**The common mistake** is reading an empty row as a problem. A character with no marks may simply not be connected yet. Connect them with **appears in**, and they show up.

**Example.** The florist writer opens Cast and sees that Marisol has no mark between the contract scene and the wedding: twelve scene cards without her rival. She adds a short scene of Marisol watching Delphine's shop at night, and the middle tightens.

## Character questions people ask

**The arc card has no Add buttons.** The card is for reading. Click **Open and change** (or the arc's title) to change an arc.

**My character has goal fields but I never turned on Ingermanson.** Plant the seed puts the lead's want and obstacle into *Goal* and *Conflict*. A field that holds something always shows, even when its method is off.

**The Ghost on an imported arc is full of reference numbers.** That's your planning's own wording, kept exactly, with its footnotes (such as *T L16949*). Click a number to see what it points to. See [Reference numbers in imported facts](#reference-numbers-in-imported-facts).

**Some empty groups are folded and some are open.** Groups with one or two fields, and each kind's main group, stay open. See [Field groups, and why some are folded](#field-groups-and-why-some-are-folded).

**There is no list of arcs in the left list.** Arcs live under their character. Open the character and look under **Arcs**, or open **Choices ahead** to see every arc field still undecided.
