# Catching problems: the continuity checks

**Continuity** means a story stays consistent with itself. A character isn't in two places on the same morning. A secret isn't revealed before anyone could know it. A clue that pays off in chapter 30 was actually planted somewhere. A name is spelled the same way on page 12 and page 312.

Slips like these hide in the gaps between chapters you wrote weeks apart. The continuity checks lay the whole book out at once, so they show up while they're still easy to fix.

## Questions, never errors: how the checks talk to you

Story Canon's motto is "Story Canon remembers. The author decides." The checks follow it. They never say something is wrong. They ask.

That's not politeness. A program can't know your intentions. A flashback *is* an effect before its cause. An unreliable narrator *is* supposed to contradict the facts. A character who vanishes for ten chapters might be the twist. So every check ends with "Intended?" or a question like it, and you answer.

- **Nothing a check says changes your story.** It only reads.
- **A check only knows what you've told it.** If you never marked a scene as revealing a secret, no check can see the reveal. An empty check usually means "nothing recorded yet", not "nothing wrong".
- **Your answers are kept.** When you answer a question in the **Story review**, it stays quiet for good, and your reason is saved.

**The common mistake.** Reading a question as a verdict and "fixing" something you did on purpose. If it's intentional, say so, write down why, and move on.

### Planned cards in the checks

Most checks show your whole plan, not only what you've written. A scene card that isn't written yet appears beside written scenes with a small dashed tag:

| Tag | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| (no tag) | A written scene in the Writing Room |

## Timelines: reading order and story order

**Where:** **Continuity checks** > **Timelines**. The page is titled **Two timelines**.

**What it shows.** Every scene and scene card twice:

- **In the order of the book**: the order the reader meets them.
- **In the order things happen**: the order the events take place in your story world.

The two can differ on purpose: a flashback, a prologue from years later, a dual timeline. Seeing them side by side shows whether the difference is the one you meant.

**The columns.**

| Column | What it holds | Where it comes from |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Scene** | Its number in reading order | The Board and the Writing Room |
| **Title** | The scene or card, with its planned tag | Click to open the card |
| **Beat** | The beat it sits on, or "no beat" | Its placement |
| **When** | Your words for when it happens | **When (story time)** on the card, or beside the page in the Writing Room |
| **Year, day or date** | The sortable time | **Year**, **Day in the story** or **Date**, under **Story time** on the card |
| **Time** | Time of day | **Time of day** on the card |
| **Where** | The place | **Where** on the card |
| **Season, weather** | The season, or the weather | **Season** or **Weather** on the card |

"not set" means the field is empty.

Under a title you may see small gray lines, **Calls up:** and **Called up by:**. They come from the **calls up** connection, for a scene that brings an earlier one back to mind (a memory, an echo, a callback).

### Getting scenes into story order

The second table only includes scenes with a **year**, a **day number** or a **date**. Words like "the next morning" show in the **When** column but can't be sorted. Below the table, a line counts the scenes that have no year, day or date yet.

1. Open a card (click its title).
2. Unfold **Story time**.
3. Fill in **Day in the story** (a number on your own story clock, such as 3), or **Date**, or **Year**.
4. Come back to **Timelines**. The scene now appears under **In the order things happen**.

**When to use it.** Any book where time matters: a ticking clock, a long span, flashbacks, two timelines. Also in revision, when you suspect a week has quietly become three days.

**What to look for.** A scene out of story order that you didn't mean as a flashback. Two scenes on the same day that can't both fit. A long gap with no **Time since the previous scene**.

**The common mistake.** Filling in only **When (story time)** in words and wondering why the second table is empty. Words are for you; numbers are for sorting. Use both if you like.

**Example.** A beekeeping novel told in two strands: Hazel in 1974 and her granddaughter in 2024. Every card gets a **Year**. In the order things happen, all the 1974 scenes come first. In the order of the book, they alternate. If a 1974 scene accidentally says 1947, it jumps to the top of the second table where you can see it.

## Viewpoint: whose eyes, and can we trust them

**Where:** **Continuity checks** > **Viewpoint**.

**What it shows.** Each chapter of the Writing Room, and in each one, every written scene: whose eyes we see it through, in what person and tense, how far the reader can trust the teller, and whether we also hear a second character's thoughts.

**Some terms:**

- **Viewpoint** (or point of view): the character whose eyes and thoughts the scene is told through.
- **Person:** first person ("I opened the door"), third person ("she opened the door"), or second ("you open the door").
- **Tense:** past ("she opened") or present ("she opens").
- **Reliability:** **Reliable** means they tell it straight. **Unreliable** means they may mislead, on purpose or not. **Selectively informed** means honest, but missing pieces.
- **Second viewpoint:** another character whose thoughts we also hear in the same scene. Switching heads mid-scene is often called **head-hopping**.

**Where it comes from.** Three places, in this order:

1. A scene's own viewpoint wins. It's the same setting as **Viewpoint character** beside the page in the Writing Room.
2. A scene with no viewpoint of its own takes its chapter's.
3. Person and tense fall back to the book's, from the story world or Novel setup.

### What's on the Viewpoint page

- **Book defaults:** one line with the book's person and tense, such as "Kevin narrates in first person; tense not set."
- **Story world and viewpoint rules:** a button that opens your book's story world record right here, so you can set the book's viewpoint, person, tense and **Viewpoint rule** (see [Story world: Viewpoint](#story-world-viewpoint)). Click **Close** under it when you're done.
- **Gaps in a character's viewpoint**, as quiet notes: "Anthony: no viewpoint scene from Chapter 3 to Chapter 9 (6 chapters). An observation, not a rule."
- **One section per chapter**, with a **Viewpoint** dropdown at the right for the chapter as a whole ("None set" until you choose).
- **A table of the chapter's scenes:**
  - **Scene:** click a title to open it in the Writing Room.
  - **Viewpoint:** the scene's own viewpoint. If it follows the chapter, the dropdown says so, such as "Kevin Wickley (from chapter)".
  - **Person** and **Tense:** type to override the book's. "Same as book" means it follows the book. Your change saves when you click away.
  - **Reliability:** **Not set**, **Reliable**, **Unreliable** or **Selectively informed**.
  - **Second viewpoint:** **None**, or a character.

If the Writing Room has no chapters yet, the page says so. Viewpoint is about written scenes, so planned cards don't appear here.

### Setting viewpoint the quick way

1. Pick the chapter's viewpoint in the dropdown at the right of the chapter's heading. Every scene in it follows.
2. Change a single scene only where it differs.

**The questions it can raise** (in the Story review):

| Question | Where it comes from | What to do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A scene lists more than one viewpoint | Your story world's **Viewpoint rule** says one per scene, and a scene has a **Second viewpoint** | Cut the second head, or answer "It's intentional" |
| A chapter has more than one viewpoint | Your rule says one per chapter | Split the chapter, or answer "It's intentional" |
| A scene is in a secret keeper's head too early | A character's **Viewpoint withheld until** names a beat, and a scene before that beat is in their viewpoint | Move the scene later, or change its viewpoint |
| A character has no viewpoint scene for a stretch | Simple counting | Only an observation. Decide whether the absence serves the book |

**The common mistake.** Setting the viewpoint scene by scene in a book where every chapter belongs to one character. Set it once on the chapter.

**Example.** A two-narrator romance alternates chapters between a florist and her rival. Each chapter's **Viewpoint** is set once. One chapter accidentally has a scene set to the wrong florist. The story world's **Viewpoint rule** says "One viewpoint per chapter", so the Story review asks about it.

## Who knows what: secrets, reveals and the reader

**Where:** **Continuity checks** > **Who knows what**. The page is titled **Who knows what, and when**.

**What it shows.** Three things:

1. **Each secret or fact** that a scene reveals, in its own section.
2. **The reader's view, scene by scene**, a table.
3. **Secret operations (author only)**, a log of what a schemer does behind everyone's back, if you keep one.

### Each secret, and who learns it

For each secret (an event or reveal that some scene **reveals**), you see:

- **The truth:** what really happened, from the record's own "What actually happened" field.
- **When it happens:** the event's own timing.
- **When the reader meets it:** the first scene or card that reveals it, such as "scene 9, 'Ledger discovery (two-stage)' (planned)".
- **Each character who learns it,** and where. A character counts as learning it in a scene that reveals it if they **appear in** that scene. If you've connected a character to the secret with **suspects** or **believes falsely**, that's shown too.

"when not recorded" means the program knows the character is connected but can't tell where they learn it.

**Reading it with judgment.** Everyone in the revealing scene is listed as learning it there, including a character who knew all along. In I Declare War, Miss Francine "learns" her own scheme in the ledger scene, simply because she's in it. Read her line as "is there when it comes out".

### The reader's view, scene by scene

| Column | What it holds | Where it comes from |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Scene** | Each scene and card in reading order, with its planned tag | |
| **Reader suspects** | What the reader suspects by the end of the scene | **What the reader suspects by the end of this scene** on the card |
| **Revealed here** | Secrets this scene reveals, or "nothing" | **reveals** connections |
| **Who learns** | Who learns what here | Characters who **appear in** the scene |
| **Visible or hidden story** | Whether the scene shows what the characters believe (visible) or what's really going on (hidden) | **Visible or hidden story** on the card |

### Secret operations (author only)

A **hidden move** is something a character does in secret that the others misread or blame on someone else. The log is "The only place the real story lives in order", and it's never shown in viewpoint summaries. Each schemer gets a table: **When**, **The hidden move**, **What the others see**, **Who takes the blame**.

**To log a hidden move:**

1. At the bottom of the page, under **Log a hidden move**, choose the character in **Whose?**.
2. Type **What they secretly do**.
3. Click **Add**. It says "Added to the secret operations log."

Hidden moves are also kept in the Story Brain, under Secret operations, filed under that character. Open a move there to fill in **What the others see** and **Who takes the blame**. See [Secret operations: every field](#secret-operations-every-field).

### Making a reveal show up here

1. Make a record for the secret in the Story Brain (an event works well), and fill in what actually happened.
2. Open the scene card where the reader finds out.
3. Under **Connections**, choose **reveals**, choose the secret, and click **Connect**.
4. Connect the characters who are there with **appears in**, if they aren't already.
5. For a character who suspects or wrongly believes something, open the character and connect them to the secret with **suspects** or **wrongly believes**.

**The questions it can raise.** When a move or a placement puts things out of order, the Story review asks things like "Kevin would act on 'Francine started the war' in 'The brawl' before learning it", or "'Who poisoned the hives?' would be answered in 'The fire' before the story raises it."

**The common mistake.** Expecting the check to find reveals in your prose. It can't read meaning. It knows a scene reveals something only when you connect it with **reveals**.

**Example.** A mystery at a seaside bakery. The truth: the owner's sister forged the will. The reader meets it at the Midpoint, in "The recipe box". The sister herself "learns it" there too (she's in the scene), the owner learns it at All Is Lost, and the nephew **wrongly believes** the owner forged it. One look at this page shows that the nephew never learns the truth at all. Intended?

## Setups and payoffs: every clue, start to finish

**Where:** **Continuity checks** > **Setups and payoffs**.

**Some terms:** A **setup** (or plant) is something placed early that matters later. A **payoff** is where it matters. A **clue** is a record for one setup in the Story Brain. The rule the page lives by: "If it pays off later, it has to be here first."

**What it shows.** Every clue, in a table:

| Column | What it shows | Where it comes from |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **What** | The clue (click to open it). "must pay off" under it if you marked it significant | The clue record |
| **Planted** | Where it's planted | Your words in **Where it is planted** on the clue, plus scenes connected with **plants** |
| **What it really means** | The truth behind it | **What it really means** on the clue |
| **Truth comes out** | Scenes that reveal it | **reveals** connections |
| **Pays off** | Where it pays off | Your words in **Where it pays off**, plus scenes connected with **pays off** |

**How your words show.** In **Planted** and **Pays off**, your own words come first: solid if decided, pencil if developing. If your words name a scene card or a beat, the program finds it and shows it underneath: a card link with its planned tag, or "at the beat **Setup**". "none" means nothing is recorded.

Below the table, a **Motifs** section lists each motif (a recurring image or object) and the scenes it appears in.

**The questions it can raise** (in the Story review):

| Question | What it means | What to do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "… pays off but nothing plants it first." | A payoff is recorded, and no plant is | Fill in **Where it is planted**, or connect the planting scene with **plants**. If the plant is truly missing, write it |
| "… is planted and marked significant, but nothing pays it off yet." | A clue you said must pay off has no payoff recorded | Record the payoff, or plan one. Not every detail needs a payoff, but this one, you said, does |
| "The setup … would now come after its payoff …" | A move would put the payoff first | Leave the move, or move the setup earlier too |

**The common mistake.** Marking every clue significant. Only mark the ones the reader will feel cheated without. Small textures can be planted and left.

**Example.** A lighthouse mystery: the keeper's broken barometer is planted in chapter 2 ("Where it is planted: the Setup, when she taps it and it doesn't move") and pays off in the storm ("Where it pays off: All Is Lost, when she trusts it and nearly dies"). The page shows both, with the two beats linked. If you later move the storm scene before chapter 2, the Move dialog asks about it before anything changes.

## Storylines: the threads and how they braid

**Where:** **Continuity checks** > **Storylines**.

**Some terms:** A **storyline** is one thread of plot: the main plot, a romance, a mystery, a rivalry. Save the Cat calls the main one the A story and the next the B story.

**What it shows.** A grid. Each storyline is a column; every scene and card is a row. A dot marks a scene that moves that storyline forward. Each column heading shows the storyline's letter and name (click to open it), and under it, its story type in slanted type, where it stands, and the values it carries. Above the grid: "The strongest scenes move more than one thread", with a count of the scenes that do.

**Where it comes from.** Storyline records in the Story Brain, and **advances** connections from scenes and cards to them.

**To fill it in:**

1. In the Story Brain, under Storylines, add each storyline. Give it a **Letter** (A, B, C) if you like. See [Storylines: every field](#storylines-every-field).
2. Open a scene card. Under **Connections**, choose **moves forward**, choose the storyline, and click **Connect**. (The check calls this connection "advances".)
3. Repeat for each scene that moves a thread.

Until you have a storyline, the page says "No storylines yet."

**The questions it can raise:**

- "… never shares a scene with another storyline." A thread that runs untouched beside the others. Braid it in, or answer that it's meant to stand apart.
- "… last appears in scene 12 of 40." A thread that stops early. Fine if its later scenes aren't mapped yet; if it really stops, does it end, or did you drop it?

**The common mistake.** Making a storyline for a value, like "trust" or "grief". Values ride on plot threads; they aren't threads of their own. Put them under **Values it carries** on the storyline.

**Example.** I Declare War with two storylines, "A: The feud" and "B: Francine's garden". Only "Fish-guts chain reaction" moves both, so the count says "1 scene moves more than one." That tells the author where the two threads touch, and where they could touch more.

## Cast: who is where, and the Bechdel test

**Where:** **Continuity checks** > **Cast**. The page is titled **The cast across the story**.

**What it shows.** A grid: each character is a row, every scene and card is a column in reading order, and a mark sits wherever a character is. Below the grid: who shares scenes, groups that keep meeting, every pair, and the Bechdel test.

**Where it comes from.** Three places: your records (**appears in** on a scene card), each written scene's viewpoint, and the character's name on the written page. Planned cards count too, so you can see the cast of a book before it's written.

### The top of the Cast page

- **On this page:** links that jump to **Who is together**, **Groups that keep meeting**, **Two at a time** and **The Bechdel test**.
- **The Bechdel test result**, in one line, with **Go to it**.
- **Gone-quiet notes**, in boxes: "The block-party wife is introduced in Chapter 1, 'Kevin declares war' and not seen after …, 10 scenes before the end. Intended?" A character who appears and then vanishes.
- **Not in any scene or card yet:** characters with a record who aren't placed anywhere.

### Reading the cast grid

The key above the grid:

| Mark | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **●** (filled circle) | The character is the scene's **viewpoint** |
| **■** (filled square) | **In the scene (your records)**: connected with **appears in** |
| **○** (hollow circle) | **Named on the page**: their name appears in the written scene |
| Shaded column | A **planned scene card, not written yet** |

- **The top header** names the acts across the columns.
- **The numbers** are scenes and cards in reading order. A **·** (dot) is a card with no beat, so no number. Hover a number to see its title, chapter and beat.
- **The number beside each name** counts the scenes and cards they're in.
- **Click any mark** to open that scene or card.

A ○ means only that the name was found in the text. If a mark looks odd, click it and check the page.

### Who is together, groups and pairs

**Who is together.** Click two or more names (they light up) to see every scene they share, with who else is there. Tick **Only scenes where no one else is there** to see just their scenes alone. **Clear** unpicks them.

Beside the result: **Their relationship** opens their relationship record, or **Make a relationship record** makes one, already connected to each of them. A relationship record can hold a pair, a family, a rivalry or a whole group.

**Groups that keep meeting.** Three or more characters who share two or more scenes. **Show scenes** lists them.

**Two at a time.** Every pair that shares a scene, how often, and in which acts.

### The Bechdel test

The **Bechdel test** is a well-known quick check of how women are written in a story: does it have at least two named women who talk to each other about something other than a man? It doesn't measure quality. A book can pass and be shallow, or fail for good reasons (a prison novel about men, say). It's a mirror, not a grade.

**How Story Canon runs it.** The program finds every scene and card where two or more women are together. Only you know what they talk about, so for each one it asks, and you answer:

1. Scroll to **The Bechdel test** (or click **Go to it** at the top).
2. For each scene listed, with the women in it named beside it, click **They do** if they talk about something other than a man, or **Not here** if they don't.
3. Click the same button again to clear your answer.

**Gender comes only from what you write on each character's page, never from a name.** A character with no gender recorded is listed under "No gender recorded for:", with a link to open them.

**The results you may see:**

| Result | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| "Passes, in N scenes." | At least one scene where you answered **They do** |
| "Waiting for your answers." | Scenes with two women exist, and some are unanswered |
| "Does not pass yet." | You've answered every scene, and none is **They do** |
| "No scene has two named women together yet." | No scene or card has two women in it |
| "Not enough named women recorded." | Fewer than two characters are recorded as women |

You may also see "Never in a scene with another woman:" followed by names.

**When to use Cast.** In planning, to see whether your cast is balanced before you write. In revision, to catch a character who disappears for half the book, or two characters who should clash but never meet.

**The common mistake.** Treating the gone-quiet notes as orders. A mentor who vanishes after Act One may be exactly right. Answer "Intended?" in your head and move on.

**Example.** A heist novel with five crew members. The grid shows the safecracker in every Act One scene and then nothing until the Finale. That's a long silence for someone the reader is supposed to worry about. The author adds her to two Act Two cards.

## Pacing: the shape of the book by length

**Where:** **Continuity checks** > **Pacing**.

**What it shows.** Each written scene as a bar, in reading order. The taller the bar, the longer the scene.

- **The left edge** shows the word scale, from 0 to your longest scene.
- **The dashed line**, labeled "usual", is your usual scene length (the median: half your scenes are longer, half shorter). The line above the chart says what it is, such as "Your usual scene runs about 2,400 words."
- **The numbers under the bars** are scene numbers in reading order. On a long book, only every second, fifth or tenth is labeled.
- **"Ch 1", "Ch 2"** and thin lines mark where chapters begin.

**Hover over a bar** to see its title, chapter, words, viewpoint, beat, whether it's a scene or sequel, its turn (the value at the start and end), its average sentence length and how much of it is dialogue. **Click a bar** to open that scene in the Writing Room.

**As a table**, a fold-out under the chart, lists the same scenes with **#**, **Scene**, **Chapter**, **Words**, **Viewpoint**, **Beat**, **Turn** and **Dialogue**.

**Where it comes from.** The words on the page in the Writing Room. Viewpoint, beat and turn come from each scene's card. Cards with no writing have no length, so they aren't drawn; the line under the chart counts them.

**The questions it can raise**, shown as boxes above the chart:

- "… runs 9,000 words, well over your usual scene (2,400). Intended, or two scenes in one?" (Only for a scene over 2,500 words and more than two and a half times your usual.)
- "… starts and ends on the same value (Trust +). Every scene has to turn. Intended?"

**What to look for.** A run of very long scenes (the reader may tire), a run of very short ones (the book may feel choppy), or one scene far longer than the rest.

**The common mistake.** Making every bar the same height. Rhythm comes from variety: a long scene, then a short sharp one.

**Example.** A thriller's Act Two shows six bars in a row near the dashed line, then one bar three times as tall. Hovering shows it's the interrogation, and it's two scenes: the questioning and the confession. The author splits it.

## Reader response: the tabs a reader would stick in the book

**Where:** **Continuity checks** > **Reader response**.

**What it shows.** Chapter by chapter, the colored sticky tabs a reader would put in the book to find a spot again:

| Color | Tab |
| --- | --- |
| Purple | **Profound** |
| Pink | **Funny** |
| Yellow | **Relatable** |
| Green | **Shocking** |
| Gray | Any word of your own |

A line at the top counts how many chapters have each tab. Each chapter is listed with its number, title and its tabs, or "no tab yet".

**Where it comes from.** Each scene card's **What you want the reader to feel**, under **Reader response**. A chapter shows the tabs of all its scenes. Chapters belong to the Writing Room, so a card's tab shows here once its scene is written (with **Write this scene**).

**To set a tab:**

1. Open a scene card.
2. Unfold **Reader response**, and click **Add** under **What you want the reader to feel**.
3. Click **Profound**, **Funny**, **Relatable** or **Shocking**, or type your own word.
4. Click **Save**. Add more than one if the scene earns it.

**What to look for.** A chapter with no tab may need a moment that makes the reader laugh, nod, gasp or stop. A long run of one color may want another kind.

**The common mistake.** Tagging every scene with every feeling. A tab is a promise that this is the spot a reader would mark. Be choosy.

**Example.** A cozy mystery shows pink tabs in nine chapters out of ten and green in only one. The author decides the middle needs a real shock, and moves the second body earlier.

## Soundtrack: the songs in your story

**Where:** **Continuity checks** > **Soundtrack**.

**What it shows.** The songs in your story, in the order they first play in the book: title, artist, what the song carries, the chapters and scenes it plays in, and whose theme song it is. Titles and artists only, never lyrics.

**Where it comes from.** Song records in the Story Brain, under Soundtrack (see [Soundtrack: every field](#soundtrack-every-field)), connected to scenes with **plays in**, or to a character with **is the theme song of**.

**To add a song:**

1. In the Story Brain, add a record under Soundtrack with the song's title. Fill in **Artist** and **What it carries**.
2. Under **Connections**, connect it to a scene card with **plays in**, or to a character with **is the theme song of**.

"Not placed in a scene yet" means the song has no **plays in** connection to a written scene.

**When to use it.** To hear the book's mood from start to end, or to keep a character's theme song consistent. Writers often use songs as a private key to a character, never mentioned on the page.

**The common mistake.** Quoting lyrics in your notes or manuscript. Song lyrics are usually under copyright, and quoting them in a published book often needs permission. Story Canon keeps titles only for that reason.

**Example.** In I Declare War, a song connected to Kevin with **is the theme song of** and to "Kevin declares war" with **plays in** shows as "Plays in Chapter 1, Kevin declares war. Theme song of Kevin Wickley."

## Names: every name on the page, counted

**Where:** **Continuity checks** > **Names**. The page is titled **Names in the book**.

**What it shows.** Every name your written scenes use, how often, in how many scenes, and which record it belongs to. Names that look like misspellings come first.

**How the program decides what's a name.** A capitalized word in the middle of a sentence counts as a name. At the start of a sentence every word is capitalized, so there a word counts only if it's a name from your records, such as a character, place or storyline.

**This can look broken, but it's working as designed.** If "Kevn" (a typo) only ever starts a sentence, it isn't counted, because the program can't tell a misspelled name from any other first word. Once it appears mid-sentence ("Across the street, Kevn heard every word"), it shows up.

### What's on the Names page

- **Spelled another way?** A box for each likely misspelling: "'Kevn' appears 1 time; your record says 'Kevin'. Intended?" (or "the book mostly says", when there's no record). Click a scene title in the box to open it and fix it.
- **Names with no record yet:** names used three times or more that have no record in the Story Brain, each with its count, such as "Camry ×4".
- **The table:** **Name**, **Times**, **Record** (or "none") and **Scenes**, for up to 200 names.

**What to do with it.** Fix any misspelling you didn't intend. Give a name you use often its own record, so the program can follow that person or place through the story (on the Cast check, for example).

**The common mistake.** Making a record for every capitalized word. "Camry" and "Tuesday" don't need records. Characters and places do.

**Door closed.** If you've closed the door for drafting (in Settings, so nothing judges your pages while you write a first draft), this page shows "The door is closed until …, so possible misspellings of names wait until then." Click **Look anyway** to see it.

## Story review: every question in one place

**Where:** **Continuity checks** > **Story review**.

**What it shows.** "Questions, never verdicts." Every question the program can ask about your story right now, with the answers you've already given. A gray line says what it checks and when it last looked.

**Where it comes from.** Your records. It compares causes and effects across scenes and cards, beats on your beat sheet, viewpoint rules in your story world, clues marked significant, storylines, Ghost timing on character arcs, and characters in two places at the same story time.

### What each question looks like

Each question has three lines:

1. **The question**, such as "'Fair play' pays off but nothing plants it first."
2. **What kind it is**, in gray: **A question**, **An observation** (a pattern worth noticing, not a problem) or **A fact from your records** (something certain, like a character in two places at once). After the kind comes the rule it checks, such as "payoff without a plant". Then up to three links to the records involved.
3. **Your answer:** a box, **Why it is this way (optional)**, and three buttons.

### Answering a review question

| Button | When to use it | What happens |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **It's intentional** | You did it on purpose | The question goes quiet for good. Your reason is kept under **Your answers** |
| **Not now** | You'll deal with it later | It moves to **Set aside for later**, still visible, out of your way |
| **I've dealt with it** | You fixed it | The question goes quiet for good, under **Your answers** |

1. If you want to keep a reason, type it in **Why it is this way (optional)** first. "Flashback, on purpose" is enough.
2. Click the button.

**Your answers** lists every question you've answered, with "Intentional" or "Dealt with" and your reason. **Ask again** brings one back as an open question, for when you change your mind or change the book.

Questions under **Set aside for later** offer **It's intentional** and **I've dealt with it**, but not **Not now** (they're already set aside).

**The common mistake.** Clicking **I've dealt with it** without changing anything, to clear the list. The question will stay quiet even if the problem is still there. If it's on purpose, say **It's intentional** and write why. Future you will want the reason.

### No open questions

When nothing is open, the page says "No open questions." and explains: "Every check ran and none found anything to ask in what is recorded so far."

**This can look like the check didn't run. It did.** Questions appear as your records give it something to compare: connections between scenes (causes, plants, reveals), cards decided on beats, story times and places, viewpoint rules. A fully planned book with no connections has little to compare.

### The questions it can ask

| Rule (shown in gray) | What it asks about | Where to fix it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **cause before effect** | A scene that **causes** another would come after it | The Board, or answer "flashback" |
| **dependency** | A scene that **depends on** another would come before it | The Board |
| **foreshadowing** | A scene that **foreshadows** another no longer comes first | The Board |
| **setup before payoff** | A plant would come after its payoff | Setups and payoffs, the Board |
| **raised before answered** | A question would be answered before the story raises it | The Board |
| **acting before knowing** | A character acts on something before learning it | Who knows what |
| **beat order** | A scene on an earlier beat comes after one on a later beat | The Board, or the Writing Room |
| **beat sheet** | A beat starts later than your sheet advises; a "win" at a disaster beat; Midpoint and All Is Lost both false victories (or both false defeats) | Where beats fall, the beat pages |
| **payoff without a plant** | A clue pays off with nothing planted | Setups and payoffs |
| **unpaid promise** | A significant clue never pays off | Setups and payoffs |
| **storyline weave** | A storyline never shares a scene with another | Storylines |
| **storyline continues** | A storyline stops early (an observation) | Storylines |
| **one viewpoint per scene** / **per chapter** | A head-hop against your viewpoint rule | Viewpoint |
| **withheld viewpoint** | A secret keeper's head too early | Viewpoint |
| **viewpoint balance** | A character has no viewpoint scene for a stretch (an observation) | Viewpoint |
| **two places at once** | A character in two scenes at the same story time in different places (a fact) | Timelines, the cards' story time |
| **Ghost timing** | A Ghost (a character's past wound) placed during the story instead of before it | The character's arc |

**Door closed.** Like Names, the Story review waits while the door is closed for drafting. Click **Look anyway** to see it.

**Example.** The I Declare War review asks: "Break into Two starts at 42.6% of the manuscript. Your sheet says: 'Before you get one-quarter of the way through your novel.' Intended?" Only two scenes are written, so the percentage means nothing yet. The author types "Only two scenes written; this will settle as the draft grows" and clicks **It's intentional**. It moves to **Your answers**, reason and all.
