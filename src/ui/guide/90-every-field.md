# Every kind and every field

This chapter explains every kind of record in the Story Brain and every field on it: what it is, why it matters, whether most books need it, the common mistake, and an example. It is the same teaching you see when you click **Why it matters** under a field, gathered in one place so you can read it straight through or look one up.

**Every field is optional.** A field is a place to put what you know when you know it. A blank field is never a mistake, and no book needs all of them. The labels "Most books need this", "Useful for many books" and "Only for some books" are there to help you choose where to spend your time.

**Kinds that only some books use** (such as Magic and powers, or Languages) appear when you switch on worldbuilding for a book in Novel setup.

## Characters: every field

A Character record holds everything you know about one person (or being) in your story: their role, their wants, their wounds, how they look and sound, and how they change. Readers fall in love with people, not plots. A record lets you see a character whole, keep details consistent across a long draft, and connect them to their arcs, relationships and scenes. When you forget whether her eyes are gray or what she fears, the record remembers.

**When to use it.** Start a record the moment a character shows up, even with one line. Add depth as you plan, and return during drafting and revision when you need to check a detail or test a choice.

**The common mistake.** Filling every field before writing a word, or treating a full record as a finished character. Depth is judged against the character's importance, and a minor character with three good fields is complete.

**Example.** Rosa, a baker who feeds the whole street and never lets anyone feed her. Her goal is to win the county pie contest and keep the shop open. Her wound is watching the bank turn her mother away. By the last page she lets her neighbors carry the ovens in.

### Characters: Role in the story

*The basics of this character's job in the book: who they are, how central they are, whether they narrate, and what they stand to lose. Fill these first.*

**Role.** The job this character does in the book's structure: lead, co-lead, B-story lead (who carries the second storyline, often teaching the theme), supporting, narrator, secret-keeper, or a hidden engine driving events offstage. Most books need this. The role tells you how much page time and inner life this person needs. Without it, a minor character can crowd the lead, or a key player stays thin.

The common mistake: Confusing role with personality. "The funny one" is a trait. "B-story lead who shows the hero a gentler way to grieve" is a role.

Example: Lands: Hattie, the night nurse, is the B-story lead; through her the hero learns to ask for help. Fails: Hattie is "supporting" and has nothing she must do.

**Who they are.** A sentence or two that says who this person is at heart, the way you would describe them to a friend who will meet them tomorrow. Most books need this. It is your anchor. Deep in chapter twenty, unsure how they would react, this line answers. Without it, a character drifts and readers meet a different person each time.

The common mistake: Listing facts instead of a person. Age, job and hair color are not an essence. Aim for the contradiction or hunger that makes them who they are.

Example: Lands: "A baker who feeds the whole street and never lets anyone feed her." Fails: "Rosa, 42, a baker with brown hair who lives in Ohio."

**Narrative importance.** How central this person is to the book: main, supporting or minor. It is a measure of weight, not of worth. Most books need this. It sets how deep you need to go. A main character needs an inner life, a wound and an arc; a minor one may need only a name, a want and a voice. Story Canon judges depth against this, never against how many fields are filled.

The common mistake: Feeling you must fill every field for everyone. A walk-on with a backstory the book never uses wastes your time and the reader's.

Example: Lands: the ferry captain is minor, with a gruff voice and a wish to retire. Fails: he gets four pages of childhood and the reader waits for him to matter.

**Narrates?.** Whether this character tells part of the story, and how: first person ("I") or close third ("she," from inside her head). Viewpoint means the character whose eyes and thoughts the reader is inside. Most books need this. Viewpoint controls what the reader can know and feel. A narrator's blind spots become the reader's. Without tracking it, voices blur and a narrator thinks something they couldn't know.

The common mistake: Giving viewpoint to everyone who has something interesting to think. Each viewpoint you add thins the reader's bond with the others.

Example: Lands: only the lighthouse keeper narrates, in first person, so his late wife stays a mystery. Fails: the wife gets one chapter halfway in, just to explain a clue.

**Viewpoint withheld until.** For a character who holds a secret: the beat or moment before which the reader never gets inside their head. A beat is one turning point in the story's structure. Only for some books. Keeping the reader out of a secret-keeper's mind is fair play. Dipping into their thoughts and then hiding the secret feels like cheating. Marking the line keeps you honest.

The common mistake: Only for books with a secret-keeper, twist or mystery. The common slip is one stray early thought that later reads as a lie.

Example: Lands: we never hear the sister's thoughts until the Midpoint, when she admits she sold the farm. Fails: in chapter two she thinks, "I miss the old place."

**What they stand to lose.** What this character loses for good if they fail. Stakes are the cost of losing, not the reward for winning. Most books need this. Readers worry only when something real can be lost. Clear stakes turn every obstacle into tension. Without them, the character can lose a scene and the reader just shrugs.

The common mistake: Making stakes vague or too big to feel ("the world will end") or forgetting the personal layer. The best stakes are specific and land on this one person.

Example: Lands: if the florist loses the contest, she loses the shop her mother built, and her mother's name comes off the sign. Fails: "She could lose everything."

**Last page, in one line.** One line that says who this character is on the last page of the book. Write it even if you don't know the ending yet; you can change it. Most books need this. Knowing the destination shapes every step. It tells you what the character must learn, lose or refuse on the way. Without it, arcs wander and endings feel tacked on.

The common mistake: Writing the plot outcome instead of the person. "She wins the contest" is an event. "She can finally hand someone else the scissors" is who she has become.

Example: Lands: "A man who keeps the light burning for strangers, not for a ghost." Fails: "He fixes the lighthouse."

### Characters: Ingermanson

*Randy Ingermanson's character questions: goal, ambition, values, conflict and epiphany. Together they give a character a direction, a reason and a change.*

**Goal.** From Randy Ingermanson: the concrete, reachable thing the character is trying to get in this story. You should be able to picture the moment they win it. Most books need this. A goal gives the plot a spine. It tells the reader what to root for and gives each scene a direction. A character with no goal waits for things to happen to them.

How it differs: Goal is the concrete thing they chase in this plot. What they think they want is the wish they would admit to; Desires are longings of every size; the arc's Want is the chase the Lie drives.

The common mistake: Stating a feeling instead of a target. "To be happy" is not a goal; nobody can see it happen.

Example: Lands: "Win the county pie contest and keep the bakery open past spring." Fails: "To feel successful."

**Ambition.** From Randy Ingermanson: the abstract hunger underneath the goal. The goal is what they chase; the ambition is why it matters to them. Most books need this. The gap between goal and ambition is where the truest choices come from. When winning the goal would betray the ambition, the character has to choose, and that choice reveals them. Without an ambition, a win is just a trophy.

The common mistake: Making the ambition the same as the goal, only bigger. "Win the contest" and "win every contest" are both goals.

Example: Lands: goal, win the pie contest; ambition, to be respected by the town that laughed at her father. Fails: goal, win the contest; ambition, be the best baker.

**Values.** From Randy Ingermanson: the things this character holds most dear. Finish "Nothing is more important than ___" as many ways as you can. Most books need this. Values are the engine of hard choices. When two collide, the character can't have both, and the reader leans in to see which wins.

The common mistake: Listing nice virtues that never compete. Honesty, family and kindness only matter on the page when the story makes them fight.

Example: Lands: "Nothing is more important than loyalty to my brother" and "Nothing is more important than the truth," then her brother lies to the police. Fails: a list of five values that never meet.

**Conflict.** From Randy Ingermanson: what stands between the character and the goal. It can be a person, a force, a circumstance or something inside them. Most books need this. Conflict is what makes a goal hard, and hard is what makes it interesting. It creates scenes, because every attempt meets resistance. Without it, the character gets what they want on page ten.

How it differs: What blocks the goal, in a line. What works against them (McKee) breaks the opposition into inner, personal and wider forces.

The common mistake: Choosing conflict that can be solved with one phone call. If one honest conversation would end the problem, the reader gets impatient.

Example: Lands: the contest judge is the rival florist's husband, and the florist owes him money. Fails: the contest is hard because the florist is nervous.

**Epiphany.** From Randy Ingermanson: what the character learns by the end, the realization that changes how they see themselves or the world. Most books need this. The epiphany gives the story its meaning. It is the reason the events mattered. Without one, the plot resolves but the character is untouched, and the ending feels hollow.

The common mistake: Stating it as a moral the character announces. An epiphany is shown through a new choice the old self would never make.

Example: Lands: the keeper hands the light's keys to his apprentice and walks to town for the first time in years. Fails: the keeper says, "I've learned that life goes on."

### Characters: Forces of antagonism

*From Robert McKee: the value at stake in the character's story and the forces that push on it, from inside, from people close to them, and from the wider world, down to the value's darkest form.*

**The value they live by.** From Robert McKee's forces of antagonism: the value at stake in this character's own story, such as justice, love, freedom or truth. It is the quality the story puts under pressure. Useful for many books. Naming the value lets you test it from every side. Each force against the character should push on this same value. Without it, the pressures feel random rather than pointed.

The common mistake: Picking a value that is too broad to test. "Goodness" is hard to push on. "Loyalty" or "self-respect" can be attacked in specific ways.

Example: Lands: the baker's value is independence; the bank, her own pride and her sister all push on it. Fails: the value is "happiness" and nothing in particular threatens it.

**Inner force.** From Robert McKee: what inside the character works against them. A fear, a habit, a flaw, a belief. Most books need this. Inner forces make a character complicit in their own trouble, which makes them human. Readers forgive outer obstacles but stay up late for a person fighting herself. Without an inner force, the conflict is all weather and no climate.

How it differs: One of three layers of opposition. Conflict, under Goal and motive, is the single obstacle in a line.

The common mistake: Naming an inner force that never actually costs them a scene. It has to make them do the wrong thing at least once.

Example: Lands: the florist's pride stops her from asking her rival for the wholesaler's number, and she loses the order. Fails: "She has self-doubt," and nothing happens because of it.

**Personal force.** From Robert McKee: someone close to the character who works against them, a friend, partner, parent or child. Not always a villain; often someone who loves them. Most books need this. Opposition from someone close cuts deeper than opposition from a stranger, because the character cannot simply walk away. It brings conflict into kitchens and bedrooms where readers live.

The common mistake: Making the close opponent a cartoon. The best personal force has a good reason for standing in the way.

Example: Lands: the keeper's daughter wants him to sell the lighthouse because she is afraid he will die alone out there. Fails: the daughter wants the money and is simply greedy.

**Extra-personal force.** From Robert McKee: what in the wider world works against the character. Institutions, laws, nature, money, society, a war, a storm. Useful for many books. This force gives the story scale and lets the pressure come from somewhere the character can't charm or argue with. It also roots the story in a real world with rules.

The common mistake: Leaving the wider world as backdrop. If the institution never acts, it is scenery, not a force.

Example: Lands: the county decides to automate the lighthouse, with a date on the letter. Fails: "Times are changing," with no letter, no date and no one doing the changing.

**The worst form of their value.** From Robert McKee: the worst form of the character's value, darker than its simple opposite. If the value is love, the opposite is hate, and the negation of the negation is hate disguised as love. Useful for many books. It shows how far down the story can go. The lie that looks like their value gives you the darkest, most honest moments. Set its depth to fit this book's tone.

The common mistake: Stopping at the simple opposite. Love against hate is a fight; love against a smothering control that calls itself love is a tragedy.

Example: Lands: the baker's independence twists into cutting off everyone who ever helped her, and calling it strength. Fails: she becomes dependent, which is only the opposite.

### Characters: Identity

*Who this character is in the world: gender, pronouns, orientation, race, heritage, age, faith, culture, disability, work and family. Fill what matters to this person and this book; leaving something unsaid is a choice too.*

**Gender identity.** This character's gender identity, in your own words. Useful for many books. It shapes how the world treats them and how they move through it. Story Canon uses it for checks like the Bechdel test (do two women talk to each other about something other than a man?), so the program never guesses from a name.

The common mistake: Leaving it out and letting the program, or a reader, assume. Or treating it as a plot twist rather than part of a whole person.

Example: Lands: "Woman." "Nonbinary, out to friends, not at work." Fails: blank, and the program counts a character named Sam as whatever it guesses.

**Pronouns.** The pronouns this character uses (she, he, they, or others), and any that change by setting or over time. Most books need this. Pronouns are on nearly every page. Recording them keeps the prose consistent, especially in long drafts and for characters whose pronouns shift with the story.

The common mistake: Not recording a change in time. If a character's pronouns change partway through, note when, so earlier and later chapters stay true to their moment.

Example: Lands: "They/them. Their grandmother still says she, and they let it go." That one line gives you a scene. Fails: switching between he and they in drafts with no record of which is right.

**Sexual orientation.** Who this character is sexually attracted to, if it matters to who they are or to the story. Only for some books. When it shapes their desires, risks or community, recording it keeps the character consistent and specific. Leaving it unsaid is a choice too, and a fine one when it has no bearing on the book.

The common mistake: Matters most in romance, coming-of-age and stories about community. The mistake is mentioning it once as a reveal and never letting it touch their life again.

Example: Lands: "Bisexual; her ex-husband knows, her church doesn't," and that shapes the wedding chapter. Fails: a single line in chapter forty that changes nothing.

**Romantic orientation, if it differs.** Who this character is romantically drawn to, if it differs from their sexual orientation. Some people feel attraction one way and fall in love another way, or not at all. Only for some books. For some characters this difference is the heart of their story. Naming it keeps their relationships true and avoids flattening them into a single label.

The common mistake: Only needed when it differs and matters, often in romance or literary fiction. The mistake is filling it in by default when it simply matches.

Example: Lands: "Asexual, but very much falls in love," and the romance is built around it. Fails: repeating the orientation field word for word.

**Intersex or sex characteristics, if it matters.** Intersex traits or sex characteristics, where they matter to the character or the story. Intersex means being born with sex traits that don't fit typical definitions of male or female. Only for some books. When it shapes the character's body, history, medical life or identity, writing it down keeps those details true across the book.

The common mistake: Only when it matters to this person's story. The mistake is using it as a shock reveal rather than as part of a whole life.

Example: Lands: "Intersex; learned at fourteen from a doctor's chart left open, and has distrusted doctors since." Fails: a body detail dropped in only to surprise the reader.

**Race or ethnicity.** This character's race or ethnicity, in your own words. Useful for many books. It shapes how others see them, what they carry from family and history, and how they move through their world. Writing it down keeps their appearance and experience consistent, and helps you see the cast as a whole.

The common mistake: Assuming a default, so only some characters get an ethnicity named. Readers notice when one group is marked and another is treated as normal.

Example: Lands: "Black American, Mississippi roots, raised in Chicago." "Irish American, second generation." Fails: only the character of color has the field filled.

**Heritage, nationality and roots.** Where this character's people come from, where they were raised, and what they call home. Nationality, roots, and the places that shaped them. Useful for many books. Heritage gives a character a past that existed before page one: food, holidays, expressions, family stories. It is a quiet source of texture and conflict, especially when home and roots are not the same place.

The common mistake: Listing a country and stopping. The rich part is how they feel about it.

Example: Lands: "Grandparents from Oaxaca, raised in Tucson, calls neither place home and both places home." Fails: "Mexican."

**Age.** How old this character is when the story starts, or in a given part. Most books need this. Age shapes what they can do, what they know, how others treat them, and what is at stake. A wrong age in a later chapter breaks a reader's trust.

The common mistake: Entering an age once and forgetting it changes. If the story spans years, use Year born instead and let the program work it out.

Example: Lands: "Sixty-eight; old enough to remember the last keeper." Fails: she is sixty-eight in chapter one and remembers a war she would have been too young to see.

**Year born.** The year this character was born. Useful for many books. If each scene has a year, Story Canon works out the character's age in every scene. That matters for books that span decades, timelines that jump, or families with many generations.

The common mistake: Doing the math in your head and getting it wrong in one chapter. Or entering a birth year that clashes with an event they remember.

Example: Lands: born 1951, so the program shows she is seventeen in the 1968 chapter and seventy-two in the frame story. Fails: she says she was ten when the mill closed, but the program shows she was four.

**Age, if it never changes.** An age that never changes: for a ghost, an immortal, a remembered younger self, or anyone outside of time. Only for some books. It tells the program not to age this character from their birth year. It also reminds you, as the writer, that this person is frozen, which is often the point of their story.

The common mistake: Only for characters outside normal time, often in fantasy, ghost stories and memory-driven literary fiction. The mistake is forgetting, so a ghost child ages in the timeline.

Example: Lands: the drowned brother stays eight in every chapter, while his sister grows old. Fails: the program shows him as forty-one in the present-day scenes.

**Faith or religion.** This character's faith or religion, including no faith, lapsed faith, or a complicated one. Useful for many books. Faith shapes values, rituals, community, guilt and hope. It gives scenes texture (a prayer before meals, a holiday) and can drive conflict when belief and want collide.

The common mistake: Naming a religion and never letting it touch a choice. Or treating every believer of a faith as the same.

Example: Lands: "Raised Baptist, stopped going after her mother's funeral, still hums the hymns when she bakes." Fails: "Christian."

**Culture and community.** The culture and community this character belongs to: the people whose customs, food, jokes and expectations they carry. It can link to a Culture record in your world. Useful for many books. Community gives a character a place to belong or push against. It shapes what is normal to them and what shames them. It gives you scenes: church suppers, festivals, family gatherings.

The common mistake: Treating culture as costume, a few foods and holidays, with no effect on how the character thinks or what they risk.

Example: Lands: "A fishing town where you never ask for help and always give it," which is why the keeper can't accept the casserole. Fails: "Small-town," with no rules.

**Disability, chronic illness or neurodivergence.** Any disability, chronic illness or neurodivergence this character lives with, and how it shapes their days, in their terms. Neurodivergence means a brain that works differently from the typical, such as autism or ADHD. Useful for many books. It makes a character specific and true. Energy, access and other people's assumptions shape their days. Recording it keeps details consistent.

The common mistake: Using it only as a plot device: a tragedy to overcome, a cure at the end, a twist. Default to a whole person who has this, not a story about it.

Example: Lands: "Type 1 diabetic; checks her sugar before every delivery and keeps juice boxes in the glove box." Fails: an illness that only appears when the plot needs a hospital scene.

**Work.** What this character does for work, paid or not: a job, a trade, caregiving, school. Most books need this. Work fills most of a person's days. It gives them skills, a vocabulary, coworkers, and a way of seeing problems. A baker sees the world in timing and heat; a lawyer in evidence.

The common mistake: Giving them a job they never seem to do. If she is a nurse, she should think like one sometimes.

Example: Lands: "Tow-truck driver; reads people by how they act when they are stranded." Fails: "Works in marketing," and nothing in the book shows it.

**Family.** Who this character's family is: parents, siblings, partners, children, chosen family, and where things stand with each. Most books need this. Family is where most wounds and loyalties start. It gives you ready-made conflict, history and stakes. Writing it down also keeps names and ages straight.

The common mistake: Listing names without the relationships. Who calls whom? Who isn't speaking?

Example: Lands: "Two brothers; she talks to the younger every Sunday and hasn't spoken to the older since the will." Fails: "Two brothers."

### Characters: Beyond human

*For characters who are not simply human. Link them to their kind's record for what their people share, and note their own powers and limits here.*

**Kind of being.** What kind of being this character is, if not simply human: an elf, a ghost, an android, a selkie. Connect them to that kind's own record with an "is a" link. Only for some books. The kind's record holds what their people share, written once. This field holds only what is special about this one, which keeps your world consistent.

The common mistake: Only for fantasy, science fiction, paranormal and similar books. The mistake is repeating the whole kind's rules on every character, then changing them in one place and not the other.

Example: Lands: Wren "is a" selkie; the selkie record says they cannot stay ashore past a year. Fails: Wren's own record says six months, a different selkie's says a year.

**Their own powers or abilities.** This character's own abilities, beyond what their kind shares. A gift, a skill, a magic they alone have. Only for some books. Powers shape what the character can do and what the plot must work around. Listing them keeps you from forgetting one in a crisis, when the reader will ask why she didn't just use it.

The common mistake: Only for books with special abilities. The mistake is adding a power when the plot needs it. Readers enjoy a solution only when they knew about the power before it.

Example: Lands: "Can hear lies as a ringing in her teeth," shown in chapter two, used in chapter thirty. Fails: in the climax she suddenly can stop time.

**Their own limits and weaknesses.** What this character can't do, and what weakens them. Their personal cost or limit, beyond their kind's. Only for some books. Limits make better stories than powers. A limit forces creative choices and creates real danger. A character with powers and no limits has no tension.

The common mistake: Only for characters with powers. The mistake is a limit that never actually bites.

Example: Lands: "Hearing lies gives her a migraine that lasts a day; she rations it." Fails: "Using her power makes her tired," and she is never tired.

### Characters: Appearance

*How this character looks: the first impression, then the details. Use these to stay consistent, not to write a head-to-toe inventory on the page.*

**The overall look.** The overall impression this character makes: what someone notices first, from across a room. Most books need this. Readers build a picture fast and keep it. A strong first impression, given early, saves you from listing features later. It also shows how others read this person, which may not be who they are.

The common mistake: Starting with a head-to-toe inventory. A first impression is one or two things, not a list.

Example: Lands: "She looks like she is about to leave, even sitting down." Fails: "Medium height, brown eyes, brown hair, jeans."

**Skin tone.** This character's skin tone, in your own words: its depth, its undertone (the warm, cool or olive cast beneath the surface), and how light finds it. Useful for many books. Specific, respectful description helps readers see the character and keeps the description consistent. It also lets you describe every character's skin, not only some.

The common mistake: Using food words (coffee, chocolate, caramel) for darker skin. It is a tired habit that many readers find objectifying. Or describing only non-white characters' skin.

Example: Lands: "Deep brown with a red undertone that glows copper in lamp light." Fails: "Mocha skin."

**Hair.** Color, texture, length and how they wear it. Hair can change over the story, so note when. Useful for many books. Hair is one of the quickest ways to show time passing, mood, culture and care. A cut, a style or a wig can be a scene. Tracking it prevents a braid from turning into a bob without explanation.

The common mistake: Forgetting that hair changes. If she cuts it in chapter twelve, mark the version so later chapters stay right.

Example: Lands: "4C, kept in twists; she cuts it short after the funeral," with the change dated. Fails: long in chapter twenty, after she cut it in chapter twelve.

**Eyes.** Eye color, shape, and anything about how they use their eyes: a steady stare, a squint, glasses. Useful for many books. Eyes are where many readers look first. What the eyes do (avoid, hold, narrow) often matters more than color.

The common mistake: Over-describing. Eye color matters to the reader less than writers think. One clear note is enough.

Example: Lands: "Gray, and he never looks at you while he is lying." Fails: "Piercing emerald orbs."

**Height.** How tall this character is, plainly or compared to others. Useful for many books. Height affects how a character moves through rooms, how they fight, how others treat them, and how scenes are staged. It is also a common continuity slip.

The common mistake: Not recording it, then having a short character reach the top shelf easily.

Example: Lands: "Six-four; ducks under every doorway in the old house." Fails: he is short in chapter three and towers over his brother in chapter nine.

**Build and body.** The shape and strength of this character's body: how they carry their weight, their size, their fitness. Useful for many books. Build shapes action and how others react. It tells the reader how this person fills a room or a doorway.

The common mistake: Using build as shorthand for character (the heavy one is lazy, the thin one is nervous). Readers notice the stereotype.

Example: Lands: "Broad, strong shoulders from hauling flour sacks; soft in the middle and comfortable in it." Fails: "Fat," as if it were a personality.

**Distinctive features.** Distinctive marks that set this character apart: scars, tattoos, birthmarks, glasses, a gap in the teeth. Useful for many books. One distinct feature lets readers find a character in a crowded scene. A feature with a story behind it can also reveal history.

The common mistake: Giving a feature with no story or using a scar as shorthand for villainy.

Example: Lands: "A burn scar on her left wrist from her first oven; she rubs it when she lies." Fails: "A scar," with nothing behind it.

**How they dress.** How this character dresses: style, care, what they wear for work and for themselves. Useful for many books. Clothes show class, taste, mood, money and how they want to be seen. A change in dress can mark a change in the character.

The common mistake: Describing every outfit. Choose the clothes that say something.

Example: Lands: "Her father's old flannel shirts over everything, even to church." Fails: a paragraph on brand names that tells us nothing about her.

### Characters: Language and voice

*What languages they speak, how they sound, and what they say again and again. These keep dialogue recognizable.*

**Languages they speak.** The languages this character speaks and how well: native, fluent, conversational, a few phrases. Useful for many books. Language decides who a character can talk to, overhear, or misunderstand. It can be a source of humor, secrecy, intimacy and conflict.

The common mistake: Letting a character suddenly understand a language they couldn't before, or making every bilingual line a translated echo.

Example: Lands: "Spanish at home, English at school, and her grandmother's Zapotec only in songs." Fails: she understands a radio broadcast in Russian with no explanation.

**Accent and how they sound.** How this character sounds: accent, rhythm, pitch, and the words they choose. Useful for many books. Rhythm and word choice carry a voice better than spelled-out dialect. Readers hear the character without tripping over apostrophes.

How it differs: The sound of their speech. Voice, under Depth lenses, is the whole of how they talk and think.

The common mistake: Writing dialect phonetically ("Ah'm gwine"). It's hard to read and often turns into caricature.

Example: Lands: "Slow and low, drops the ends of questions so they sound like statements." Fails: dropping every g with an apostrophe.

**Sayings and verbal habits.** Sayings, phrases and verbal habits this character repeats: a pet phrase, a way of swearing, a habit of answering with a question. Useful for many books. A small verbal habit makes a character instantly recognizable in dialogue, even without a tag. Used well, a repeated phrase can grow in meaning.

The common mistake: Overusing it until it becomes a tic. Or giving two characters the same saying by accident.

Example: Lands: "Says "we'll see" when she means no." Readers learn it, then feel it when she says it to her son. Fails: three characters all say "for real though."

### Characters: Physical

*Health, energy, and how they move. Bodily realities that ground a character in scenes.*

**Physical notes.** Physical notes: health, energy, how they move, any bodily realities that shape their day. Useful for many books. How a character moves and feels in their body grounds them in scenes. It shows age, mood and health without telling.

The common mistake: Forgetting to carry a physical condition through. If her knee hurts in chapter one, it hurts on the stairs later.

Example: Lands: "Bad left knee; takes stairs one at a time, and hates when people notice." Fails: a bad knee that disappears the moment she needs to run.

### Characters: Personality

*Temperament, humor, habits, strengths and weaknesses: the everyday texture of who they are.*

**Temperament.** This character's natural emotional weather: calm, quick to anger, anxious, sunny, steady. The way they tend to react before they think. Useful for many books. Temperament makes reactions believable and lets you plan contrast in a scene. Two calm people talk; a calm person and a hothead create sparks.

The common mistake: Confusing temperament with a single mood. A steady person can still break, and that break matters because it is rare.

Example: Lands: "Slow to anger, slow to forgive." Fails: "Moody," which tells you nothing about which way they go.

**Humor.** What makes this character funny, and what they find funny. Useful for many books. Humor is one of the fastest ways to make readers like someone. What a character laughs at also shows their values and wounds.

The common mistake: Giving every character the same humor, usually the author's. Each funny person should be funny in their own way.

Example: Lands: "Dry, deadpan, laughs only at her own disasters." Fails: "Funny."

**Habits and props.** Small repeated actions and the objects that go with them: cracking knuckles, a lucky pen, always carrying a thermos. Useful for many books. Habits and props make a character visible and give their hands something to do in a scene. A habit can also show a feeling the character won't say.

The common mistake: Giving the same habit to two characters by accident. Make sure each habit belongs to the right person.

Example: Lands: the keeper winds his dead wife's watch every night. Fails: two characters both twist their rings when nervous.

**Strengths.** What this character is good at, inside and out. Useful for many books. Strengths let a character do things and earn the reader's respect. They also set up the moment when a strength fails them.

The common mistake: Listing strengths that never show. If she is brave, there should be a scene where she is brave and it costs her.

Example: Lands: "Can calm a crying child or a drunk customer in under a minute." Fails: "Kind, smart, brave."

**Weaknesses.** What this character is bad at or struggles with: skills, temptations, blind spots. Useful for many books. Weaknesses create obstacles and make success harder, which makes it sweeter. They also make a character relatable.

The common mistake: Choosing cute weaknesses (clumsy, too caring) that never cost anything.

Example: Lands: "Can't keep money; gives it away the day she gets it." That weakness can end the bakery. Fails: "Too much of a perfectionist."

### Characters: Environmental

*Where they live and what happened before the story began.*

**Home and neighborhood.** Where this character lives: the house, the street, the neighborhood, and what it says about them. Useful for many books. Home gives scenes a place to happen and shows class, taste, care and history. A home can comfort or trap a character.

The common mistake: Describing the home but never showing how the character lives in it.

Example: Lands: "A rented upstairs apartment over the laundromat; always smells of dryer sheets, never quiet." Fails: "A nice apartment downtown."

**History.** Key events in this character's past, one per entry: what happened before the story begins. Useful for many books. History explains why a character is who they are now. It gives you material for memories, secrets and reveals. Tracking it keeps your backstory consistent.

How it differs: Past events of any kind, hurtful or not. Wounds are the hurts; the arc's Ghost is the one behind the Lie.

The common mistake: Putting all of it on the page. History is for you; the reader needs only the pieces that change a scene.

Example: Lands: "At nineteen, left nursing school when her father got sick." Fails: three pages of her history before the story starts.

### Characters: Psychological

*The inner landscape: fears, wounds, desires and beliefs. The raw material of motivation and change.*

**Fears.** What this character is afraid of, from small dreads to the big fear underneath. Useful for many books. Fear drives avoidance, and avoidance drives plot. A character who fears something will go out of their way not to face it, until the story makes them.

The common mistake: Giving a fear the story never tests.

Example: Lands: "Afraid of being needed and failing," and the story puts a sick child in her care. Fails: "Afraid of spiders," and there are no spiders.

**Wounds.** Old hurts this character still carries: losses, betrayals, humiliations. For the single defining one, use the Wound and its fallout group. Useful for many books. Wounds explain behavior that would otherwise seem odd. They make readers patient with a character's flaws. Angela Ackerman and Becca Puglisi's Emotional Wound Thesaurus is a strong guide to how wounds shape a person.

How it differs: Every old hurt they carry. The arc's Ghost is the single event behind one arc's Lie.

The common mistake: Making every wound tragic, or adding wounds that never shape a choice.

Example: Lands: "Her best friend took credit for her design in college." She now signs every sketch. Fails: a list of five tragedies that never come up.

**Desires.** What this character longs for: small cravings and big dreams, spoken and secret. Useful for many books. Desire is what moves a character forward. Knowing what someone wants, beyond the plot goal, makes them feel alive and gives scenes pull.

How it differs: Longings of every size, spoken or secret. Goal is the one concrete target in this plot.

The common mistake: Listing desires that are all for the plot. People also want naps, peace and a good tomato.

Example: Lands: "Wants one day with no one asking her for anything." Fails: "Wants to win."

**Beliefs.** What this character holds true about the world, people, God, love or themselves. Useful for many books. Beliefs are the lens a character sees through. Beliefs that are wrong, or that the story challenges, become the material of change.

The common mistake: Listing beliefs that never meet a challenge.

Example: Lands: "Believes family is the only people you can trust," and her brother betrays her. Fails: "Believes in hard work."

### Characters: Wound and its fallout

*One defining wound traced through its effects: the event (K.M. Weiland's Ghost), what they came to believe, what they fear, how they guard against it, and what sets it off. Angela Ackerman and Becca Puglisi's Emotional Wound Thesaurus is a strong companion here.*

**The wounding event.** The wounding event: what happened, and how old they were. K.M. Weiland calls this the Ghost, the past event that haunts the character. Most books need this. The wound is the root of the false belief that drives the arc. A specific event makes the character's later choices make sense. Angela Ackerman and Becca Puglisi's Emotional Wound Thesaurus explores how wounds shape people.

How it differs: Weiland calls this the Ghost. If it is already on the arc, there is no need to write it here too.

The common mistake: Making it vague or too big. It doesn't have to be tragic, but it must be personal and convincing.

Example: Lands: "At nine, she watched her mother beg the bank for an extension and get turned away." Fails: "She had a hard childhood."

**What they came to believe because of it.** What this character came to believe because of the wound. Usually a false belief that once felt protective. Most books need this. This belief is the bridge between the past and the present. It explains why the character acts against their own good. In K.M. Weiland's terms, this is close to the Lie.

The common mistake: Writing a belief that is plainly false and silly. It must have felt true and kept them safe.

Example: Lands: "If you ask for help, people will see you as weak and leave." Fails: "She believes nothing good ever happens."

**What they fear will happen again.** What this character fears will happen again because of the wound. Most books need this. This fear explains their guarded behavior and their choices under pressure. It gives you the scene where the fear comes true, or seems to.

The common mistake: Choosing a fear that doesn't link to the wound.

Example: Lands: "That she will lose everything again because she depended on someone." Fails: "Afraid of heights."

**What they avoid or guard against now.** What this character avoids or guards against now, to make sure the wound doesn't happen again. Useful for many books. Guards are visible in behavior. They show the reader the wound without telling. They are also what the story must strip away.

The common mistake: Listing guards the character never shows in a scene.

Example: Lands: "Pays cash for everything; never signs a loan." Fails: "She protects herself."

**How it shows in what they do.** How the wound shows in what this character does: habits, reactions, avoidances. Useful for many books. These behaviors are the wound made visible. They let readers feel the hurt without being told, and set up the moment when behavior changes.

The common mistake: Telling instead of showing. A behavior is something a camera could see.

Example: Lands: "Rereads every contract three times; leaves when someone offers to pay." Fails: "She is distrustful."

**What sets it off.** What sets the wound off: situations, words or people that make the old pain rise. Useful for many books. Triggers give you scenes where the past surges into the present. They make a character's sudden reactions understandable.

The common mistake: Using triggers only for drama, without consequences.

Example: Lands: "A man in a suit saying "I'm sorry, there's nothing I can do."" Fails: "Banks," which is too broad to stage.

### Characters: Traits and flaws

*Good qualities and flaws, each tied to where it came from and what it costs, so they can be shown rather than named.*

**Positive traits.** This character's good qualities, each with where it came from and how it shows without being named. Angela Ackerman and Becca Puglisi explore traits this way in their thesaurus books. Useful for many books. Good traits make readers like and root for a character. Knowing where each came from makes it believable and connects it to the backstory.

The common mistake: Naming the trait on the page ("She was generous") instead of showing it.

Example: Lands: "Generous: grew up with nothing and swore no one at her table would go hungry. Shows in the extra loaf in every bag." Fails: "Generous."

**Flaws.** This character's flaws, each with what it protects them from and what it costs. Angela Ackerman and Becca Puglisi explore flaws this way in their thesaurus books. Most books need this. Flaws make characters human and create trouble. Knowing what a flaw protects shows you why they keep it, and what the story must take away.

The common mistake: Choosing flaws that are actually strengths in disguise, or that cost nothing.

Example: Lands: "Stubborn: protects her from being talked out of what she knows. Costs her the bank loan and her sister's trust." Fails: "Stubborn," with no cost.

### Characters: Depth lenses

*Questions that test whether a character is alive beyond their plot function: their wants, their behavior under pressure, their voice, and whether they make things happen.*

**What they think they want.** What this character thinks they want, beyond their plot function. The want they would tell you about. Useful for many books. It gives the character a life outside the plot. It also sets up the gap between what they think they want and what drives them underneath.

How it differs: The wish they would admit to. Goal is the concrete target in this plot; the arc's Want is the chase the Lie drives.

The common mistake: Repeating the plot goal. Ask what they want as a person, not as a piece of the story.

Example: Lands: "Wants her mother to finally say she's proud." Fails: "Wants to solve the case."

**What drives them underneath.** What drives this character underneath, the need they don't see in themselves. Useful for many books. Hidden motives create surprising but believable behavior. Readers often spot it before the character does, which builds tension and sympathy.

The common mistake: Making the hidden motive too obvious, or announcing it in narration.

Example: Lands: she thinks she wants her mother's pride, but she wants to stop needing it. Fails: "Deep down she just wants love."

**Under pressure.** How this character behaves under pressure, uncertainty, success and failure. Useful for many books. Pressure reveals true character. Knowing how they react lets you build scenes that test them and shows the reader who they really are.

The common mistake: Giving the same reaction in every situation. People act differently when winning than when losing.

Example: Lands: "Under pressure she gets quiet and efficient; after a win she gets reckless." Fails: "She handles it well."

**Comic engine.** What reliably makes this character funny on the page: the flaw, habit or contradiction that keeps producing laughs. Only for some books. A comic engine is a source, not a single joke. It gives you comedy in any scene. Without it, humor comes and goes at random.

The common mistake: Most useful in comedies and romantic comedies, or for a comic-relief role. The mistake is relying on jokes rather than a trait that creates them.

Example: Lands: "Treats every small problem like a military campaign." Fails: "She makes witty remarks."

**Voice.** How this character sounds in thought and speech: vocabulary, rhythm, what they notice, how they joke. Most books need this. Voice makes a character recognizable without a dialogue tag. It also shows their education, mood and mind.

How it differs: How they sound as a whole. Language and voice holds the pieces: the languages they speak, their accent, and their sayings.

The common mistake: Using dialect as a shortcut. Voice lives in word choice, sentence length and attention, not spelling.

Example: Lands: "Short sentences. Notices exits and prices. Jokes only about herself." Fails: "Southern."

**With different people.** How this character acts with different people: a rival, an ally, family, a stranger. Useful for many books. People change with their company. Showing this makes a character feel real and helps you plan how scenes will play.

The common mistake: Making them act the same with everyone.

Example: Lands: "With her sister: bossy. With her rival: overly polite. With strangers: warm." Fails: "She's nice to everyone."

**Agency.** What this character makes happen, rather than reacts to. Agency means the power to act and cause change. Most books need this. Readers follow characters who drive the story. A character with agency makes choices that cause events. Without it, things just happen to them, and readers lose interest.

The common mistake: Mistaking activity for agency. Running from danger is a reaction; deciding to confront it is agency.

Example: Lands: "She calls the newspaper herself and exposes the bank." Fails: "A reporter happens to find the documents."

### Characters: Contradiction layers

*The gaps between public and private self, and between what they do and why. Complex characters live in these gaps.*

**Public identity.** Who this character is in public: the face they show the world. Useful for many books. Every person shows the world a version of themselves. The gap between public and private self is where drama lives.

The common mistake: Making public and private the same, which wastes the layer.

Example: Lands: "The cheerful baker who knows everyone's name." Fails: a public face no one ever looks behind.

**Private identity.** Who this character is in private: when no one is watching. Useful for many books. The private self is the truth the public face hides. Showing it creates intimacy with the reader.

The common mistake: Making the private self only darker. It might be softer or more frightened.

Example: Lands: "Cries in the walk-in freezer where no one can hear." Fails: "She's secretly evil."

**What they do.** What this character actually does, especially when it seems to contradict who they claim to be. Useful for many books. Readers judge by actions. Showing what a character does, especially when it surprises, makes them complex.

The common mistake: Listing behavior that matches what they say, which wastes the contrast.

Example: Lands: "Says she hates her rival, but sends her customers when her own shop is full." Fails: "She's mean to her rival."

**Why they really do it.** Why this character really does what they do, especially when the behavior seems odd. Useful for many books. The reason behind a contradiction makes it believable. It turns inconsistency into depth.

The common mistake: Leaving it unexplained to yourself. You don't have to tell the reader, but you should know.

Example: Lands: "She sends customers to her rival because they apprenticed together and she still loves her." Fails: "No reason."

### Characters: Comparisons

*Real people or other characters whose qualities you are borrowing, with a note of which qualities.*

**Like….** A real person or character whose qualities you are borrowing. Note which qualities so it never becomes a copy. Only for some books. Comparisons give you a quick picture and a tone to aim for. Naming the specific quality keeps you from copying the whole person.

The common mistake: Useful when it helps you hold a character. The mistake is borrowing too much, until the character is a copy.

Example: Lands: "Like my aunt Dee: the way she laughs at funerals." Fails: "Like Sherlock Holmes," and the character becomes a detective with a violin.

### Characters: Writing aids (private)

*Tools just for you: an actor's voice, a song or a playlist that puts you back inside this character. Never shown as part of the book.*

**Casting tuning fork.** An actor whose voice and tempo help you write this character. It is private, for you only, and never a description of the character. Only for some books. Hearing a voice in your head makes dialogue easier and more consistent. It's a tool, like a tuning fork for an instrument.

The common mistake: Helpful for writers who hear dialogue. The mistake is describing the actor's face as the character's.

Example: Lands: "The pace of a weary newscaster." Fails: the character ends up with the actor's famous face.

**Theme music.** A song or piece of music that captures this character's mood or essence for you. Private, for you only. Only for some books. Music can put you back in a character's head fast, especially after a break. It holds a feeling better than notes can.

How it differs: A private mood note for you. To put a song in the book, add it to the Soundtrack and connect it with is the theme song of.

The common mistake: Helpful if music moves you. The mistake is quoting lyrics in the book; keep it to titles only.

Example: Lands: an old blues song you play before every chapter from her point of view. Fails: printing the lyrics in the epigraph.

**Playlist.** A list of songs for this character: one for each mood, phase or relationship. Private, for you only. Only for some books. A playlist tracks a character's changes and helps you write scenes in the right mood.

The common mistake: Helpful if music moves you. The mistake is spending more time on the playlist than on the pages.

Example: Lands: three songs, one for before, during and after her loss. Fails: sixty songs and no chapters.

### Characters: Other facts

*Facts that came in from your planning without a place of their own, and anything that fits nowhere else. Kept here, last, so the page above stays in order.*

**Other facts about them.** A catch-all list for anything true about this character that has no field of its own: a birthday, a car, an allergy, the name of a childhood dog. Useful for many books. Small facts are where continuity errors hide. Write them once and the dog stays named Biscuit in chapter three and chapter thirty.

How it differs: A catch-all. A fact that has its own place on this page is easier to find there.

The common mistake: Letting it become the whole character. If a fact shapes who they are, such as a fear or a wound, give it its proper field so the program can connect it.

Example: Lands: "Drives a 1994 pickup with no working radio. Allergic to shellfish. Left-handed." Fails: "Afraid of water since her brother drowned," which belongs in Wounds.

## Arcs: every field

An Arc record tracks one way a character changes over the story: the value at stake, the false belief they start with, the truth they reach, and the tests in between. Arcs are what make readers care about the ending. A plot can resolve, but if the character doesn't change (or refuses to), the book feels empty. K.M. Weiland's framework of Lie, Want, Need, Ghost and Truth gives you a clear way to build that change step by step.

**When to use it.** Sketch the Lie and Truth early, when you know your character's goal. Add tests as you outline, and check them in revision to make sure the change happens in visible steps.

**The common mistake.** Treating the arc as a moral at the end. An arc is built in scenes where the character chooses between the Lie and the Truth, sometimes wrongly.

**Example.** The lighthouse keeper's Ghost is losing his wife to the sea. His Lie is that if he loves no one, he loses no one. He wants to keep the light alone. He needs to let his apprentice in. By the end he gives her the keys.

### Arcs: Arc

*The arc's shape: the value it is about, its type, the tests along the way, and where it ends.*

**Value this arc is about.** The value this arc is about: the quality that changes in this character over the story, such as trust, courage or self-worth. One character can carry more than one arc, each on a different value. Most books need this. Naming the value keeps the arc focused. Every test and turn should push on it. Without it, the character changes in a general way and the reader can't say how.

The common mistake: Choosing a value too broad to track. "Growth" is not a value; "trust in others" is.

Example: Lands: the keeper's arc is about letting others in. Fails: the keeper's arc is about "becoming better."

**Arc type.** K.M. Weiland's arc types. Positive Change: the character overcomes a false belief and grows. Flat: they already hold the truth and change the world around them. Negative: they fall, clinging to the false belief or trading it for a worse one. Most books need this. The arc type sets the shape of the story and the tone of the ending. Knowing it early points every turn the right way.

The common mistake: Assuming every arc must be Positive. Flat arcs drive many great stories, and Negative arcs make tragedies.

Example: Lands: Positive, the baker learns to accept help. Flat, the new pastor already believes in mercy and changes a hard town. Fails: a Flat arc where the character is just unchanged and also changes nothing.

**Key tests and turns.** The moments that test and turn this arc: where the character chooses between the Lie and the Truth, fails, and tries again. One entry per test. Most books need this. Change has to happen in steps the reader can see. Each test shows how far the character has come and how far they still have to go. Without them, the change at the end feels sudden.

The common mistake: Making every test a success. A character should fail some tests, or the change feels easy.

Example: Lands: she refuses help from her sister (fails), then accepts a loan from her rival (passes), then backslides when the oven breaks. Fails: one scene where she suddenly understands.

**Who they are at the end.** Who this character is at the end of this arc. Most books need this. It names the destination. Every test should point toward it, or away from it if the arc is Negative.

The common mistake: Writing the plot outcome instead of the inner state.

Example: Lands: "Lets people in, even when it is inconvenient." Fails: "She wins the contest."

**Still unresolved.** What is still unresolved in this arc: open questions, threads you haven't tied, or things the character never fully fixes. Useful for many books. Tracking loose ends keeps you from forgetting them. Some should be tied off; others may stay open on purpose, which can make an ending feel true.

The common mistake: Leaving loose ends by accident. Decide which ones you mean.

Example: Lands: "She still hasn't forgiven her mother. That stays open on purpose." Fails: a subplot about her mother that simply disappears.

### Arcs: Weiland Arc

*K.M. Weiland's arc framework: the Ghost (the wound), the Lie it created, the Want the Lie chases, the Need that would heal it, and the Truth that replaces the Lie.*

**Ghost.** K.M. Weiland's Ghost: the formative experience that explains the Lie. It need not be tragic, but it must be personal and convincing. Also called the wound. Most books need this. The Ghost makes the Lie believable. Readers forgive a character's mistakes when they understand where the mistakes come from. Without it, the Lie seems arbitrary.

How it differs: The Ghost is the one past event behind this arc's Lie. Wounds, on the character page, hold every old hurt; History holds past events of any kind.

The common mistake: Making it melodramatic for its own sake, or leaving it so vague that it explains nothing.

Example: Lands: "At twelve, she trusted a friend with her diary and found it read aloud at school." Fails: "Something bad happened to her once."

**Lie.** K.M. Weiland's Lie: the false belief the character holds about themselves or the world. It once felt protective, and it is what the arc must overcome. Most books need this. The Lie is the engine of a change arc. It explains why the character keeps making the wrong choice, and its defeat is what the ending means. Without a Lie, a Positive Change arc has nothing to change.

The common mistake: Writing a Lie that is just a mistake about facts. It must be a belief about how life works that feels true to them.

Example: Lands: "If people really knew me, they would leave." Fails: "She thinks the bank is closed on Tuesdays."

**Want.** K.M. Weiland's Want: what the Lie makes the character chase. Usually an outer goal they believe will fix things. Most books need this. The Want drives the plot and gives the reader something to root for. Its gap from the Need is what makes the story mean something.

How it differs: The Want belongs to this arc and is driven by the Lie. The character's Goal is the concrete target in the plot, often the same thing seen from outside.

The common mistake: Making the Want and Need the same. If getting what they want also heals them, there is no inner conflict.

Example: Lands: she wants to win the contest alone, proving she needs no one. Fails: she wants to make friends, and she needs to make friends.

**Need.** K.M. Weiland's Need: what would truly heal the character. Usually the opposite of the Want, and tied to the Truth. Most books need this. The Need is the arc's destination. When the character reaches for it at the cost of the Want, the change becomes visible.

The common mistake: Stating the Need as a lesson. A Need is a change in what they reach for, not a moral.

Example: Lands: she needs to let her neighbors help, even if it costs her the win. Fails: "She needs to learn a lesson."

**Truth.** K.M. Weiland's Truth: the true belief that replaces the Lie. It is what the character finally understands. Most books need this. The Truth is the theme made personal. It gives the story its meaning and the ending its weight.

The common mistake: Making the Truth a slogan. It should be the exact answer to this character's Lie.

Example: Lands: Lie, "If people knew me, they would leave." Truth, "The people who stay are the ones who know me." Fails: "Love conquers all."

## Relationships: every field

A Relationship record tracks what is between two characters: where they stand, what is underneath, and what is private to them. Many books are really about relationships: love stories, family sagas, rivalries. Even in plot-driven books, readers care about how people treat each other. A record lets you track how trust, power and closeness change, and keeps their private language consistent.

**When to use it.** Create one for every relationship that changes over the book, or that carries a key part of the story. Return in revision to check that changes happen on the page.

**The common mistake.** Describing a relationship once and never tracking how it moves.

**Example.** Two rival florists, June and Marisol, both want the cathedral contract. Underneath, they both want their mothers' shops to survive. June secretly sends Marisol customers. By the end they share a storefront.

### Relationships: Where they stand

*Gauges of the relationship right now: trust, respect, closeness, resentment, dependence, rivalry and power. A word or a short line is enough, such as "high, and slipping." Add a new entry as it changes, and mark when each is true.*

**Trust.** How much these two believe each other and count on each other right now. A measure that can rise and fall over the book. Most books need this. Trust decides what they will tell each other, what they will risk, and how hard a betrayal lands. Tracking it lets you plan the moments it breaks or grows. Without it, trust can jump without a reason.

The common mistake: Letting trust change offstage. Every shift should happen in a scene the reader sees.

Example: Lands: "Low. She checks every receipt he hands her." Then a scene where he covers for her and it rises. Fails: they distrust each other in chapter five and share secrets in chapter six with nothing between.

**Respect.** How highly each regards the other: their skill, judgment or character. It can be one-sided. Useful for many books. Respect and liking are different. Two rivals can respect each other deeply, and two friends can quietly look down on each other. That gap gives scenes texture.

The common mistake: Assuming respect matches affection.

Example: Lands: "The rival florists can't stand each other, but each secretly studies the other's window." Fails: "They respect each other," with no sign of it.

**Closeness.** How close these two are: how much they share, how well they know each other, how they behave when alone. Not only romantic. Useful for many books. Closeness sets what is natural between them, from silences to touches to inside jokes. Tracking it lets you show growing or fading intimacy in steps.

The common mistake: Equating closeness with romance, or skipping the steps.

Example: Lands: "Close enough to sit in silence in the truck for an hour." Fails: strangers in chapter one who finish each other's sentences in chapter three.

**Resentment.** Old or growing grudges between them: what one holds against the other. Useful for many books. Resentment is buried fuel. It explains sharp words and sudden coldness, and it can explode at the worst moment.

The common mistake: Naming resentment that never leaks into a scene.

Example: Lands: "She resents that he got their father's watch." He wears it to the funeral. Fails: "Some resentment," with no source.

**Dependence.** How much one needs the other, for money, care, safety, love, or skill. It can be mutual or lopsided. Useful for many books. Dependence creates tension: a person who needs another can't simply leave. It also shapes power.

The common mistake: Forgetting it cuts both ways. The person depended on may need to be needed.

Example: Lands: "He needs her to drive him to dialysis; she needs a reason to leave her own house." Fails: "He depends on her."

**Rivalry.** Competition between them: for a prize, a person, attention, or being right. Useful for many books. Rivalry drives scenes and can turn into respect, love or hate. It gives each a reason to watch the other.

The common mistake: Rivalry with no stakes. What does winning cost?

Example: Lands: "Both florists want the cathedral contract; the winner gets the wedding season." Fails: "They compete."

**Balance of power.** Who holds more power in this relationship, and what kind: money, status, knowledge, love, age, secrets. Most books need this. Power shapes what each can say and do. When power shifts, the relationship changes, and that shift is often a turning point.

The common mistake: Treating power as fixed. The best relationship stories move it.

Example: Lands: "The landlord has the lease; the tenant knows about his second family." Fails: "He's in charge."

### Relationships: Underneath

*The hidden layer: what they share, what divides them, where it is going, and what each doesn't know.*

**What they share underneath.** What these two share underneath any surface conflict: a common need, wound or hope. Most books need this. A shared need is the hidden bond that keeps two people tied together and makes reconciliation possible. In romance, it is often why they belong together. Without it, the relationship feels like only conflict.

The common mistake: Making it too obvious, or never letting the characters see it.

Example: Lands: "Both rival florists want their mothers' shops to outlive them." Fails: "They both want to be happy."

**Opposition on the surface.** What sets these two against each other on the surface: the visible conflict, the thing they argue about. Most books need this. Surface conflict gives scenes energy. Paired with the shared need, it creates the push and pull of a strong relationship.

The common mistake: Making it so trivial it could be solved in one honest talk.

Example: Lands: "Both bid on the same storefront." Fails: "They don't get along."

**Where the relationship is really going.** Where this relationship is really going: toward love, rupture, friendship, revenge, or something else. The emotional destination, not the plot. Most books need this. Knowing the destination keeps each scene between them moving. In romance, readers expect the trajectory; your job is to make the steps surprising.

The common mistake: Losing track of it, so the relationship circles without moving.

Example: Lands: "From rivals to business partners who never admit they're friends." Fails: "It develops."

**Hidden influence.** How one shapes the other without the other knowing: a secret kindness, a manipulation, a quiet push. Only for some books. Hidden influence gives the relationship a layer the reader may discover before the characters do. It sets up reveals.

The common mistake: Useful in mysteries, thrillers, family sagas and slow romances. The mistake is revealing it too soon.

Example: Lands: "He has been paying her shop's rent through the landlord for a year." Fails: a secret that never comes out.

**False assumptions.** What each wrongly believes about the other. One entry per assumption. Useful for many books. False assumptions create misunderstanding, which creates scenes. Correcting them can be a turning point.

The common mistake: Assumptions so flimsy that one question would clear them up.

Example: Lands: "She thinks he left town for money; he left to care for his dying father." Fails: "She thinks he doesn't like cake."

**Who knows what the other does not.** Who knows something the other doesn't. One entry per secret or gap. Useful for many books. Unequal information creates dramatic irony (the reader knows more than a character) and suspense. It shapes every scene between them.

The common mistake: Losing track of who knows what, so a character reacts to news they never heard.

Example: Lands: "She knows the bakery is closing; her son doesn't." Fails: he mentions the closing in a scene before anyone tells him.

### Relationships: Between them

*The private texture of the bond: names and words only they use.*

**What they call each other.** The names these two call each other that nobody else uses: nicknames, pet names, shortened names. Useful for many books. Private names show intimacy instantly. A change in what they call each other can signal a change in the relationship.

The common mistake: Using the nickname inconsistently, or letting other characters use it.

Example: Lands: "She calls him Captain only when she's mad." Fails: three characters call him Captain.

**Words only they share.** Words or phrases only these two share, and what they mean to them. Only for some books. A private language shows a deep, lived-in bond. Readers feel included when they learn it.

The common mistake: Most useful for close relationships: siblings, longtime couples, best friends. The mistake is explaining it every time.

Example: Lands: "Biscuit means 'I'm not okay but don't ask.'" Fails: a private word explained in narration every time it appears.

## Interviews: every field

An Interview record holds one question you ask a character and their answer in their own voice. Interviewing a character helps you find their voice and discover things you didn't plan. Speaking their answer aloud tests whether you truly know them.

**When to use it.** Use it when you are getting to know a character, or when a character feels flat or quiet during drafting.

**The common mistake.** Answering as the author instead of the character.

**Example.** You ask the lighthouse keeper, "What do you miss most?" He answers, "The sound of someone else breathing in the house."

### Interviews: Answer

*The character's answer in their own voice, and whether it held up when you said it aloud.*

**Their answer, in their own voice.** The character's answer to this interview question, written in their own voice, as if they were speaking to you. Most books need this. Answering as the character tests whether you know their voice. It often turns up surprises: things they would say that you didn't plan. Without it, you know facts about them but not how they talk.

The common mistake: Answering as the author, explaining the character instead of speaking as them.

Example: Lands: "What do you fear? Being the one who stays when everyone else leaves. Don't tell my sister." Fails: "She fears abandonment because of her childhood."

**Said out loud without looking?.** Whether you said the answer out loud, without looking, and whether it held up. Useful for many books. Speaking a character's answer from memory tests whether their voice is truly in your head. If it falls apart, you know where to work.

The common mistake: Skipping the test. Many writers find their character's voice only when they say it aloud.

Example: Lands: "Yes, it held," and you heard her clearly. Fails: "It fell apart," which tells you to spend more time in her voice before drafting.

## Beats: every field

A beat is one of the turning points in a story structure, such as the Save the Cat beats Blake Snyder named and Jessica Brody adapted for novels: Catalyst, Break into Two, Midpoint, All Is Lost, Finale and the rest. This record holds what that turn must do in your book and your plan for it. Beats give the book a skeleton. Each marks a place where the story must change direction, so the middle doesn't sag and the ending has something to pay off.

**When to use it.** Early, while you outline, and again in revision when a stretch feels flat. You can decide what a beat must do long before you know what happens in it.

**The common mistake.** Treating a beat as a box to fill with any event. A beat is a turn. If nothing is different for the hero afterward, the beat isn't delivered, no matter how much happens on the page.

**Example.** A lighthouse keeper's Catalyst: an empty boat washes onto her rocks. Lands: from that morning she can't go back to her quiet routine. Fails: she reports the boat, and her life goes on as before. The event happened. The turn didn't.

### Beats: In this book

*What this beat does in your novel and how you plan to do it: its job, your event, the options you're weighing, and three checks (disaster, discovery or win; caused by the hero; how much page space it takes).*

**What this beat does in this book.** The turn this beat must deliver in your book: what is different for the hero, or the story, once it's over. Think of it as the beat's job description. Most books need this. Deciding the job first lets you leave the event open while knowing exactly what any event here must accomplish. Without it, you pick scenes because they're exciting, not because they do the work, and the structure quietly falls apart.

The common mistake: Writing the generic definition ("the hero commits to the new world") instead of what this beat does in this book.

Example: Lands: "The baker loses her lease and has to choose between her mother's recipes and the chain's offer." Fails: "Catalyst happens here."

**What it is (your plan).** Your plan: what actually happens on the page at this beat in this novel. Most books need this. This is where the abstract job becomes a scene a reader can see. Keeping it separate from the function means you can swap the event later without losing what it has to do.

The common mistake: Filling in an event and never checking it against the function. An exciting event that doesn't deliver the turn is still a missing beat.

Example: Function: the florist sees her rival isn't the enemy. Lands: in a blackout he lends her his generator, and she sees his books are as broke as hers. Fails: a pleasant chat at the market.

**How it could happen.** Possible ways the event could happen, kept side by side until you choose. Execution means the staging: who is there, where it happens, and how the turn arrives. Useful for many books. Most beats have several workable executions. Writing a few out lets you compare them instead of settling for the first idea, which is usually the most familiar one.

The common mistake: Inking one too soon, or letting options pile up forever. When one clearly wins, mark it decided and let the others go.

Example: Beat: the keeper learns the missing sailor is alive. Options: a letter in his hand, his voice on the marine radio, or he walks into the harbor cafe. Each puts different pressure on her.

**One way it could be done.** An illustration of how a beat like this could be done, from another story or a made-up case. It is never your decision; the program keeps it marked as never canon. Useful for many books. Seeing the beat done once makes the job concrete. Keeping examples apart means a borrowed idea can't slip into your book by accident.

The common mistake: Copying the example's event instead of learning its move. Ask why it turns, then find your own version.

Example: For All Is Lost: in a heist, the safe is empty and the crew turns on each other. The move: plan and team break at once. Your version might be a bakery fire that ends a partnership.

**Disaster, discovery or win.** Disaster: a reversal that leaves the hero worse off against her goal. Discovery: she learns something that changes the picture. Win: she gets what she was after, for now. Useful for many books. Stories move by alternating these. Labels show patterns, like five wins in a row, that drain tension. Finding is not winning: knowing where the treasure is isn't having it.

The common mistake: Calling a setback "a win in disguise." If she's worse off when the beat ends, it's a disaster, even if it helps her later.

Example: Lands: the beekeeper finds the poisoned hives (discovery), then the co-op votes her out (disaster). Fails: labeling the vote a win because it frees up her time.

**Caused by the hero's own efforts?.** A check: does this turn happen because of what the hero chose and did? A passive hero is one things happen to: luck, a rescue or another character drives the turn while she waits. Most books need this. Blake Snyder and Jessica Brody insist her own choices cause the big turns, especially Break into Two, the Finale and the win. If she isn't the reason anything changes, the reader stops caring and the ending feels unearned.

The common mistake: Treating it as a rule for every beat. The Catalyst often happens to the hero. The check matters most where she has to act.

Example: Lands: the ferry captain takes her own boat into the storm to reach the island. Fails: the coast guard saves everyone while she waits on the dock.

**Scene type.** How much page space the beat takes. Single Scene or Single Chapter: one contained moment. Single Beat: a quick turn inside a larger scene, sometimes one line. Multi Scene or Multi Chapters: a long stretch, like Fun and Games. Useful for many books. Size drives pacing and placement. A stretch can't be rushed into a paragraph, and a quick turn padded into a chapter sags.

The common mistake: Giving every beat its own chapter. Theme Stated is often one line, while Fun and Games can fill a quarter of the book.

Example: Rival florists: the Catalyst is one scene (a wedding books them both). Fun and Games runs many chapters of sabotage. Theme Stated is a single beat: her aunt's remark at the counter.

### Beats: On the board

*Extra fields that only certain Save the Cat beats carry, such as the theme line for Theme Stated, home, work and play for the Setup, or the whiff of death for All Is Lost. They come from Blake Snyder and Jessica Brody, and they appear only on the beats they belong to.*

**The line that states the theme.** For the Theme Stated beat: the line, usually spoken by someone other than the hero, that hints at what she needs to learn. The theme is what the story is really about under the plot. Useful for many books. Snyder and Brody place it early so the reader hears the truth before the hero is ready for it. The rest of the book proves it. Without it, her change at the end can seem to come from nowhere.

The common mistake: Making it a speech or a moral. It should almost slip past, and the hero should brush it off.

Example: Lands: the old keeper says, "A light's no use if no boat comes close," and she laughs. Fails: a page-long lecture on loneliness.

**Home.** For the Setup beat: what the hero's home life looks like before the story changes it, and what is broken or missing there. Useful for many books. Snyder and Brody ask you to show the hero at home, at work and at play, and to plant the "six things that need fixing": problems the story will later address. Home is often where her wound shows.

The common mistake: Describing the house instead of the life. The reader needs the problem, not the floor plan.

Example: Lands: the baker eats dinner standing at the counter, and her daughter has stopped asking her to sit down. Fails: "She lives in a two-bedroom flat above the shop."

**Work.** For the Setup beat: what the hero's working life is like, and what isn't working in it. Useful for many books. Work shows what she does all day, how good she is at it and what she puts up with. Those problems become things the story can fix or break, part of Snyder and Brody's six things that need fixing.

The common mistake: Using work only as a backdrop. If nothing at work needs fixing, it takes page space without doing a job.

Example: Lands: the florist designs every arrangement while her boss signs the cards and takes the credit. Fails: she works at a flower shop and likes flowers.

**Play.** For the Setup beat: what the hero does for joy or release, or the fact that she has stopped. Useful for many books. Play shows who she is when no one is asking anything of her. Losing it is often the clearest sign of what needs fixing, and getting it back can be a quiet sign of change at the end.

The common mistake: Skipping it because it seems minor. A hero with no play often reads as a job title instead of a person.

Example: Lands: the keeper used to sing on the gallery at night, and now she sleeps with the radio on so she won't hear the quiet. Fails: "She enjoys reading."

**The Debate question.** For the Debate beat: the question the hero wrestles with after the Catalyst and before she commits, such as "Should I go?" or "Can I really do this?" Useful for many books. The Debate shows the reader she has a real choice, which makes Break into Two a decision instead of an accident. It also lets her fear show before the plunge.

The common mistake: A question with an obvious answer, or a debate that runs so long the story stalls.

Example: Lands: "If I take the chain's money, am I saving my mother's shop or selling it?" Fails: "Will she go to the meeting?"

**The hero's WANT.** For Break into Two: the hero's Want, the outward goal she consciously chases into Act Two. K.M. Weiland notes the Want is usually shaped by the Lie the character believes about herself or the world. Most books need this. The Want drives the plot. Snyder and Brody pair it with the Need: she chases what she wants and only later learns what she needs. Without a clear Want, the middle has no engine.

The common mistake: Making it vague ("to be happy") or the same as the Need. The gap between them is the arc.

Example: Lands: the florist wants to win the town's wedding contract and put her rival out of business. Fails: she wants things to work out.

**The hero's NEED.** For Break into Three: the hero's Need, the inner change she must make to truly succeed. In K.M. Weiland's terms it is usually the Truth that answers her Lie. Snyder and Brody place her grasp of it at Break into Three. Most books need this. The Need gives the ending its meaning: she solves the problem because she has changed. Without it, the Finale is only a plot win.

The common mistake: Writing it as another goal ("she needs to win the contract") instead of an inner truth.

Example: Lands: she needs to stop treating every person near her as competition. Fails: she needs more customers.

**False victory or false defeat.** For the Midpoint and All Is Lost: whether the Midpoint is a false victory or a false defeat. False doesn't mean fake. It means it isn't the end, and stakes are about to rise. Useful for many books. Snyder notes the two beats mirror each other: a false victory at the Midpoint sets up a real low at All Is Lost, and a false defeat sets up the reverse. Choosing one shapes the whole second half.

The common mistake: Making both beats point the same way, so the second half has no swing.

Example: Lands: the baker wins the city fair (false victory); the chain notices, and at All Is Lost she loses the lease. Fails: she wins, then wins again.

**Whiff of death.** For All Is Lost: something dies or ends, literally or symbolically: a mentor, a dream, a relationship, an old version of the hero. Useful for many books. Snyder calls this the whiff of death. It marks the bottom and clears the way for the old self to die so a new one can rise. Without it, the low point reads like a bad day instead of a bottom.

The common mistake: Killing a character only for shock, with no link to what the hero must let go of.

Example: Lands: her father's logbook, the reason she stayed on the rock, is lost in the flood. Fails: a neighbor dies offstage and is never mentioned again.

**Hero is changed, or rejects change.** For the Final Image: whether the hero is changed, shown by a picture that mirrors the Opening Image, or rejects change, as in a tragedy or cautionary tale where she refuses her Need. Useful for many books. The Final Image proves the story mattered. Set beside the opening, it shows the whole arc in one picture. Naming which ending you're writing keeps that image honest.

The common mistake: Ending on plot resolution with no picture of change, or showing a change the story never earned.

Example: Lands: the book opens with the baker eating alone at the counter and closes with three generations at one table. Fails: the last page is the lease signing.

## Scene cards: every field

A scene card is your plan for one scene: what it must do, whose goal drives it, what stands in the way, and how it turns. It can also hold the scene's time, place, atmosphere, emotion and what the reader knows. The scene is the unit of change in a novel. Robert McKee teaches that every scene turns a value from positive to negative or the reverse. A card lets you check that a scene earns its place before you write it, and spot the one that only marks time.

**When to use it.** While outlining a chapter, or just before you draft a scene. Come back to it in revision when a scene feels flat.

**The common mistake.** Filling every group on every card. Most scenes need The scene group and little else. The other groups are for scenes where timing, atmosphere, viewpoint tricks, timelines or heat matter.

**Example.** Two rival florists set up for the same wedding. Her goal: finish the arch before he arrives. He arrives early, charms the planner and takes the best corner. Value: confident (+) to humiliated (-). Every line of the scene serves that turn.

### Scene cards: The scene

*The core of the card: the scene's job, the viewpoint character's goal, the conflict, the outcome, and the value charge at start and end. It draws on Dwight Swain's scene structure and Robert McKee's idea that a scene turns a value.*

**What this scene must do.** The job this scene does for the book: what must be different, known or set up by the time it ends. Most books need this. A scene with a clear job can be trimmed to what serves it. Without one, scenes drift into talk and description that feel pleasant but move nothing.

The common mistake: Writing what happens instead of what it must do. "They have lunch" is an event. "She learns he's been lying about the lease" is a purpose.

Example: Lands: "The keeper must realize the stranger knew her father." Fails: "The keeper and the stranger talk."

**The viewpoint character's objective.** What the viewpoint character wants in this scene, specifically and right now. The viewpoint character is the one whose eyes and thoughts we're in. Dwight Swain calls this the scene goal. Most books need this. A goal gives the reader something to root for and a way to keep score. Every line either helps or blocks it, and that is where tension comes from.

The common mistake: Giving a life wish ("to be loved") instead of something she can win or lose in this scene.

Example: Lands: the baker wants the health inspector gone before he opens the back fridge. Fails: the baker wants her shop to succeed.

**The conflict.** The opposition: who the players are, what each wants, what's in the way, and who wins. The >< mark is shorthand for two forces pushing against each other. Most books need this. Conflict is what turns a goal into a scene. If nothing resists the goal, the character simply gets it, and the reader has no reason to lean in.

The common mistake: Thinking conflict means arguing. It can be quiet: a locked door, a polite refusal, the character's own fear.

Example: Lands: she wants the arch done first >< he wants the same corner and has the planner's ear; he wins. Fails: they bicker about ribbon and nothing is at stake.

**Intended outcome or change.** How the scene ends: what changes, and whether she got what she wanted. In Swain's scene the ending is usually a disaster: no, yes but, or no and worse. Most books need this. The outcome pays off the goal and the conflict. It decides what she feels next and what she has to do. Without one, the scene ends on a shrug.

The common mistake: Letting her get the goal cleanly with nothing complicated. A clean yes stops momentum unless it's a deliberate win.

Example: Lands: the inspector leaves without opening the fridge, but he's written down her supplier's name. Fails: he leaves and everything is fine.

**Value at the start.** The charge of the scene's key value at the start: positive (+) or negative (-). A value is something the character cares about that can flip, such as trust and betrayal, safety and danger, hope and despair. Most books need this. Robert McKee teaches that a scene works when it turns a value. Marking the starting charge makes the turn easy to see, and easy to miss if it isn't there.

The common mistake: Naming a mood with no opposite. Pick a value that can actually flip.

Example: Lands: trust between two sisters (+), planning the reunion together. Fails: "a nice afternoon."

**Value at the end.** The charge of the same value at the end. If it matches the start, the scene hasn't turned. Most books need this. McKee's test: a scene that ends where it began is filler, and readers feel it even if they can't name it. A string of turns, scene after scene, is what builds an arc.

The common mistake: Turning the value by announcing it ("now she felt hopeless") instead of through something that happens.

Example: Lands: trust (-), when one sister finds the other's letter to a lawyer. Fails: they finish planning, as close as when they started.

### Scene cards: Around it

*How this scene connects to its neighbors: what led here, what it forces next, when and where it happens, and whether it lands as a disaster, discovery or win.*

**How we got here.** What happened before this scene that makes it necessary: the cause, decision or pressure that brings the character into it. Useful for many books. Scenes linked by cause feel inevitable. Scenes linked by "and then" feel random. This field shows the chain.

The common mistake: Summarizing the previous chapter instead of naming the specific cause.

Example: Lands: because she found the poisoned hive, she has come to the co-op meeting to accuse someone. Fails: "After chapter 7."

**What must follow.** What this scene forces next: a reaction, a decision or a consequence the story now owes. Useful for many books. If a scene changes things, the story has to answer. Noting the debt keeps you from dropping consequences, which readers always notice.

The common mistake: Leaving it blank on a big scene, then forgetting the fallout.

Example: Lands: after the public accusation, her neighbor must either threaten to sue or confess. Fails: the accusation is never mentioned again.

**When (story time).** When the scene happens in the story world, in whatever terms you track. Useful for many books. Placing scenes in time catches errors: two events on the same morning, a broken wrist that heals in three days.

The common mistake: Treating chapter order as time order. Flashbacks and parallel strands break that.

Example: "Evening of the second storm, three days after the boat washed up."

**Where.** Where the scene happens. Linking it to a Place record keeps details consistent. Useful for many books. Place shapes what characters can do, who might overhear, and how the moment feels. The right place can make a scene harder for the character, which makes it better.

The common mistake: Defaulting to the kitchen or the car for every hard conversation.

Example: Lands: she tries to apologize in the bakery during the lunch rush, with customers listening. Fails: her apartment, again.

**Disaster, discovery or win.** Whether the scene lands as a disaster (she ends up worse off), a discovery (she learns something that changes the picture) or a win (she gets what she wanted, for now). Useful for many books. Seeing the pattern across scenes shows pacing problems, like a run of easy wins or nothing but blows.

The common mistake: Calling a discovery a win. Learning who poisoned the hives isn't stopping them.

Example: Lands: a discovery, then a disaster in the next scene. Fails: four wins in a row before the Midpoint.

### Scene cards: Story time

*Your story's internal clock for this scene: day, date, year, time of day and the gap since the last scene. It keeps ages, seasons and travel times consistent.*

**Day in the story.** A number on your own story clock, such as day 3 of the feud. Only for some books. A day count tracks pace and deadlines. Situational: most useful for books with a short span or a ticking clock, like thrillers or a two-week romance.

The common mistake: Forgetting to update the count after a time skip.

Example: "Day 6 of 14 before the auction."

**Date.** The calendar date of the scene. Only for some books. Dates matter when holidays, real events or the day of the week touch the story. Situational: historical fiction and books set around real dates or seasons.

The common mistake: Picking a date without checking what day of the week it was, or what was happening then.

Example: Lands: December 23, so the post office line is out the door and the shop is open late. Fails: a quiet Sunday that was really a Tuesday.

**Year.** The year the scene happens. With birth years on character records, the program can work out everyone's age here. Only for some books. Ages drift in books that span years or generations. Situational: family sagas, long spans, historical fiction and flashbacks.

The common mistake: Leaving years off flashback scenes, where age mistakes are most likely.

Example: Lands: 1987, so the keeper is nine and her father is still alive. Fails: no year, and she remembers a talk from before she was born.

**Time of day.** Morning, dusk, 3 a.m., or whatever fits. Useful for many books. Time of day sets the light, the energy and who's around. It also keeps continuity straight.

The common mistake: Leaving every scene at an undefined "then."

Example: Lands: a confession at 4 a.m. in the bakery, when no one else is awake to interrupt. Fails: the same talk "at some point."

**Time since the previous scene.** How much time has passed since the previous scene. Useful for many books. Gaps change what characters feel and know. A fight two hours ago is raw. Two weeks later it has hardened into silence.

The common mistake: Skipping time without telling the reader, or letting feelings stay frozen across a long gap.

Example: Lands: "Two weeks later," and she has stopped calling. Fails: a month has passed and she's still crying in the same way.

### Scene cards: Atmosphere

*The physical and emotional climate of the scene, and how the setting itself acts on the plot.*

**Season.** The time of year in the scene. Useful for many books. Season shapes light, weather, work, holidays and mood. In some stories it limits what characters can do.

The common mistake: A story that covers months but never changes season.

Example: Lands: late autumn, the bees are clustered, so she can't open the hives to check them, and the delay matters. Fails: "It was fall."

**Weather.** The weather during the scene. Useful for many books. Weather can press on the plot as well as the mood. Used well, it's another obstacle or a quiet contrast.

The common mistake: Using weather only to match a feeling, like rain whenever someone is sad.

Example: Lands: fog grounds the ferry, so she's stuck on the island with the man she accused. Fails: it rains because she's sad.

**Physical atmosphere.** The physical feel of the space: light, sound, temperature, crowding, smell. Useful for many books. It gives the reader a body in the room. A reader who can feel the cold will believe the scene.

The common mistake: Listing objects instead of how the place feels to be in.

Example: Lands: the cold room hums, her breath fogs, the door seal sticks when she pulls. Fails: "There was a fridge, a table and a chair."

**Emotional atmosphere.** The mood of the scene as a whole: tense, tender, festive, dreadful. It isn't the same as the viewpoint character's own emotion. Useful for many books. Mood tells the reader how the room feels. A gap between the room's mood and the character's feeling can be powerful.

The common mistake: Blending it with the character's emotion, so you lose the chance for contrast.

Example: Lands: a cheerful reception where only she knows the groom's secret. Fails: a sad scene where everything is also sad.

**Sensory details.** Specific details of sight, sound, smell, taste and touch you want on the page. Useful for many books. Concrete detail makes a reader believe in a place. One sharp detail does more than five general ones.

The common mistake: Leaning only on sight. Smell and touch often carry memory better.

Example: Lands: burnt sugar on the oven floor, flour caught in the cuffs of her sleeves. Fails: "The bakery smelled nice."

**What the setting does to the plot.** How the setting changes what can happen: mud after rain, heat that sharpens a smell, darkness that limits who can see. Useful for many books. When the setting acts on the plot, it stops being backdrop and becomes a force the characters must deal with.

The common mistake: Describing a setting at length that never affects anything.

Example: Lands: the power is out, the walk-in is warming, and she has four hours to save the order. Fails: a storm described for a page while everyone stays dry.

### Scene cards: What the reader knows

*What the reader suspects by the end, and whether this scene shows the visible story or the hidden one. Most useful in mysteries, thrillers and any book with a secret.*

**What the reader suspects by the end of this scene.** What you want the reader to suspect or believe by the end of the scene, true or false. Useful for many books. Managing suspicion is how you build suspense and set up surprise. Tracking it scene by scene shows whether a reveal will shock, satisfy, or arrive too late. It matters most in mysteries and thrillers.

The common mistake: Assuming readers know only what characters say. Readers infer from everything on the page.

Example: Lands: the reader suspects the neighbor because he knew about the hives. Fails: you meant them to, but nothing in the scene points his way.

**Visible or hidden story.** Whether this scene shows the visible story (what the viewpoint characters believe is happening) or the hidden story (what is actually happening). Only for some books. A book with a secret runs two stories at once. Labeling scenes shows whether the hidden story keeps moving. Situational: mysteries, thrillers and books built on a secret or twist.

The common mistake: Letting the hidden story stall for long stretches, so the reveal feels bolted on.

Example: Visible: the sisters plan the reunion at the family house. Hidden: the older sister has already sold it.

### Scene cards: Scene and sequel

*Dwight Swain's pattern: a scene (goal, conflict, disaster) is followed by a sequel (reaction, dilemma, decision), and the decision becomes the next scene's goal.*

**Scene or sequel.** Dwight Swain splits story units into scenes and sequels. A scene is action: goal, conflict, disaster. A sequel is aftermath: reaction, dilemma, decision. The decision becomes the next scene's goal. Useful for many books. Alternating them gives the book a rhythm of action and reflection, and ties each scene to the next by cause. All scenes can feel breathless. All sequels can stall.

The common mistake: Thinking a sequel is slow summary. It's where the character absorbs the blow and chooses, which can be tense.

Example: Scene: she confronts the neighbor and he slams the door. Sequel: she shakes in her car, weighs the police against the co-op, and decides on the co-op.

**Reaction.** In a sequel, the character's immediate emotional response to the disaster that just happened. Useful for many books. Swain starts the sequel with feeling, because the reader needs to see the blow land before the character can think. Skip it and she seems unhurt, so the reader isn't either.

The common mistake: Jumping straight to planning, as if nothing hurt.

Example: Lands: she sits on the curb and can't make her hands stop shaking. Fails: "Well," she thought, "time for plan B."

**Dilemma.** The choice she faces once the first feeling passes: the options in front of her, none of them good. Useful for many books. The dilemma lets the reader weigh the options with her, which raises the stakes of whatever she decides.

The common mistake: Giving one obvious option. A real dilemma costs something either way.

Example: Lands: tell the co-op and lose her oldest neighbor, or keep quiet and risk more hives. Fails: whether to go home or stay out.

**Decision.** What she chooses to do. It becomes the goal of the next scene. Useful for many books. The decision links one scene to the next, so the plot runs on choices instead of coincidence. It also keeps the hero active.

The common mistake: A decision the next scene ignores.

Example: Lands: she'll go to the co-op tonight, so the next scene is the meeting. Fails: she decides, and the next chapter follows another thread with no follow-up.

### Scene cards: Emotion on the page

*How the viewpoint character's emotion shows: in the body, inside, in her thoughts, and in what she tries to hide. It follows the categories Angela Ackerman and Becca Puglisi use in their Emotion Thesaurus.*

**The viewpoint character's main emotion here.** The main emotion the viewpoint character feels in this scene, named as precisely as you can. Useful for many books. A precise name helps you show it consistently and pick signals that fit. Angela Ackerman and Becca Puglisi organize their Emotion Thesaurus by specific emotions for this reason.

The common mistake: Settling for a broad word like "upset" when you mean humiliation, dread or jealousy.

Example: Lands: shame, not anger, because she knows he's partly right. Fails: "bad."

**What the body shows.** The visible signs of the emotion: gestures, posture, voice, actions. Ackerman and Puglisi call these physical signals. Useful for many books. Showing emotion through the body lets the reader feel it instead of being told. Other characters can also notice these, which creates plot.

The common mistake: Using the same few signals for everything, like sighs and clenched jaws.

Example: Lands: she straightens the cake boxes that are already straight. Fails: "She sighed angrily."

**What they feel inside.** What she feels inside her body that others can't see: a tight chest, heat in the face, a hollow stomach. Ackerman and Puglisi call these internal sensations. Useful for many books. These give the reader access only the viewpoint can offer, and they make the emotion physical.

The common mistake: Overusing the pounding heart until the reader stops noticing it.

Example: Lands: her ears ring and the floor seems a little too far away. Fails: her heart pounded, for the fifth time this chapter.

**What their thoughts do.** What the emotion does to her thinking: racing thoughts, fixation, denial, sudden clarity. Ackerman and Puglisi call these mental responses. Useful for many books. Emotion bends thought. Showing the bend is how readers come to know a character from inside.

The common mistake: Writing calm, logical thoughts while her body is in a panic.

Example: Lands: she keeps recounting the trays, as if the number might change. Fails: she calmly reviewed her options.

**What they try to hide, and what leaks out.** The emotion she is trying to hide, and the signs that leak out anyway. Ackerman and Puglisi treat suppression as its own set of signals. Useful for many books. Hidden feeling creates subtext: the reader sees what other characters miss. It's one of the strongest tools for tension in a quiet scene.

The common mistake: Hiding it so well the reader misses it too, or so poorly it isn't hidden.

Example: Lands: she laughs at his joke a beat too loud, and her hand stays clamped on the counter. Fails: "She hid her jealousy perfectly," and the reader never sees a crack.

### Scene cards: Narrator and truth

*For narrators who are wrong, lying or blind to something: what the narrator believes here, set against what is actually true.*

**What the narrator believes here.** What the narrator thinks is true in this scene. An unreliable narrator is one whose account differs from the truth, through a lie, a blind spot or a misunderstanding. Only for some books. Recording the belief scene by scene keeps the gap consistent and fair. Situational: books with unreliable or mistaken narrators, mysteries and twists.

The common mistake: Letting the narrator's belief shift between scenes for no reason.

Example: "She believes her brother left town because of the gambling debt."

**What is actually true.** What is actually true here, whatever the narrator believes. The gap between the two is what the reader may or may not catch. Only for some books. Writing the truth beside the belief lets you plant fair hints a careful reader can spot, especially on a second read.

The common mistake: Hiding the truth with no clues at all, so the twist feels like a cheat.

Example: Lands: he left to protect her from the man he owed, and the scene shows him checking the window twice. Fails: no sign of it until the last chapter.

### Scene cards: Who tells it

*For scenes where the voice we seem to hear isn't the real teller, and where the reader finds out.*

**Who seems to be telling this.** Who seems to be telling this scene. Only for some books. In some books the voice we think we hear isn't the real teller. Situational: frame stories, letters, memory chapters and twist structures.

The common mistake: Writing the apparent and true narrators in identical voices, so the later reveal has nothing to stand on.

Example: The chapter seems to be the keeper remembering her father.

**Who is really telling it.** Who is really telling it, for example a chapter told through someone else's memory of a person. The reader learns the difference when you decide. Only for some books. A hidden teller adds a layer that makes the reader reread with new eyes.

The common mistake: Choosing a true narrator who couldn't have known what's in the chapter.

Example: Lands: it's really the sailor's account of her father, which explains details she could never have known. Fails: the true narrator was never in the room.

**Where the reader finds out.** Where in the book the reader finds out who is really telling. Only for some books. Timing decides whether it lands as a shock, a slow dawning or a final turn.

The common mistake: Revealing so early it does nothing, or never revealing it, so the setup is wasted.

Example: Lands: at All Is Lost, when the reader most needs to know whom to trust. Fails: in an epilogue, after every question is settled.

### Scene cards: Timeline strand

*For books with more than one timeline: which strand this scene belongs to.*

**Which timeline.** Which timeline the scene belongs to: then or now, the frame or the tale, or names of your own. Only for some books. Tracking strands helps you balance them and see how turns in one echo the other. Situational: dual timelines, frame stories and books with flashback strands.

The common mistake: Letting one strand run long without a turn, so readers skim to get back to the other.

Example: "1962: the lighthouse" and "Now: the sale." Each strand turns on the same secret in the logbook.

### Scene cards: Callbacks

*Running gags and callbacks that return in this scene, so each return can twist a little further.*

**Callbacks and running gags here.** Running gags and callbacks in this scene. A callback returns to something set up earlier. A running gag is a callback that keeps coming back. Only for some books. Each return builds warmth or laughter, but only if it twists a little further. Situational: comedy, romantic comedy and warm ensemble books.

The common mistake: Repeating the gag exactly, so it wears out.

Example: Lands: his shop sign falls; next time he nails it up; at the wedding it falls on him. Fails: the sign falls the same way four times.

### Scene cards: Reader response

*The feeling you want the reader to come away with from this scene.*

**What you want the reader to feel.** The reaction you want: Profound, Funny, Relatable, Shocking, or your own. Think of it as the sticky tab a reader would put on this page. Useful for many books. Naming it helps you find chapters that give a reader nothing to remember. A chapter with no tab may need more.

The common mistake: Aiming for every response at once, or for none.

Example: Lands: Funny for the collapsing cake, Shocking for the letter at the end. Fails: a chapter that only moves people between rooms.

### Scene cards: Heat

*For romance and any book with intimate scenes: how far this scene goes on the page and what it changes between the characters.*

**What is on the page.** How far an intimate scene goes on the page: closed door, fade out, partial or fully explicit. Only for some books. Readers expect a consistent heat level, and the book makes that promise early. Situational: romance and any book with intimate scenes.

The common mistake: Changing the level without meaning to, which breaks the promise.

Example: Lands: every intimate scene closes the door after the first kiss. Fails: two closed-door scenes, then one fully explicit in chapter 20.

**What the intimacy changes between them.** What the intimacy changes between the two characters: more trust, a new fear, a shift in power. Only for some books. Every intimate scene should move the relationship, not only the temperature. If nothing changes, it reads as an interruption.

The common mistake: Writing the scene as a reward with no consequence.

Example: Lands: after their night she shows him the ledger she has hidden from everyone. Fails: the next morning they're exactly where they were.

## Events: every field

An event is something that happens in the story world, on the page or offstage, now or in the backstory. The record separates what the event must accomplish from how it happens, and keeps track of what really happened and who knows it. Separating the job from the staging lets you commit to what the story needs while the details stay open. Tracking truth and knowledge keeps secrets straight across the whole book: who saw what, who is lying, and what the reader has been told.

**When to use it.** As soon as you know something has to happen, even before you know how. Also for backstory events that still press on the present.

**The common mistake.** Recording what happens but never what it's for. Events without a job pile up and bloat the plot.

**Example.** The bakery fire. Job: force the baker to accept her sister's help. Possible executions: a faulty oven, or arson. Truth: the sister's husband set it. Who knows: only him, until late in the book. Reader: suspects the chain.

### Events: What it does

*The event's dramatic job: what it has to change in the story.*

**What it must accomplish.** The dramatic job: what this event must accomplish for the story. You can decide it while how it happens stays open. Most books need this. Knowing the job keeps you from locking in a staging before you know what it serves, and makes it easy to cut events that serve nothing.

The common mistake: Describing the event instead of its job.

Example: Lands: "Separates the sisters just when they most need each other." Fails: "A fire."

### Events: How it happens

*The possible stagings, kept side by side until you choose.*

**How it could happen.** Possible ways the event could happen, side by side as options until you choose one. Useful for many books. The first idea is usually the most familiar. Laying out a few lets you pick the one that best serves the job and puts the most pressure on the characters.

The common mistake: Choosing too early, or never choosing.

Example: Job: the keeper is cut off from the mainland. Options: the radio dies, the causeway floods, or the supply boat stops coming. The flood also threatens the logbook, so it does two jobs.

### Events: When

*Where the event sits on your story clock, which can differ from when the reader learns of it.*

**When it happens in the story.** When the event happens in the story world. That can be very different from when the reader learns about it. Useful for many books. Pinning the time keeps cause and effect straight, especially for backstory and offstage events.

The common mistake: Leaving backstory events undated, then contradicting yourself about ages or order.

Example: "Spring 1987, two months before the father's death."

### Events: Truth and knowledge

*What really happened, who knows it and when, what the reader knows, and whether the event belongs to the visible story or the hidden one.*

**What actually happened.** What actually happened, plainly, whatever anyone in the story says or believes. Useful for many books. The truth is your reference point. Every lie, rumor and mistake is measured against it. It matters most in mysteries and books with secrets.

The common mistake: Leaving the truth vague and planning to work it out later, so the clues you plant don't fit.

Example: Lands: "Her husband set the fire to collect the insurance and pay his debt." Fails: "Something suspicious happened."

**Who knows, and when.** Each character who knows the truth, and when each learns it. Useful for many books. This stops characters from acting on knowledge they don't have yet, one of the most common continuity slips. It also shows who could reveal the secret, and when.

The common mistake: Forgetting that people talk. If two characters know, consider who else they'd tell.

Example: Lands: "Her husband: from the start. The insurance agent: chapter 12. The baker: the Finale." Fails: the baker hints at it in chapter 5, before she could know.

**What the reader knows or suspects.** What the reader knows or suspects about the event, and from when. Useful for many books. When the reader knows more than the characters, you get suspense (dramatic irony: the reader sees danger the character can't). When the reader knows less, you get mystery. Deciding which you want shapes every scene around it.

The common mistake: Not deciding, so the reader is half-told and neither tense nor curious.

Example: Lands: the reader sees the husband buy the fuel, so every kind word he says to the baker is unbearable. Fails: a hint so faint that no reader catches it.

**Visible or hidden story.** Whether the event belongs to the visible story (what the viewpoint characters believe is happening) or the hidden story (what is actually happening). Only for some books. Labeling layers shows whether the hidden story keeps moving underneath. Situational: mysteries, thrillers and books built on a secret.

The common mistake: Hidden events that never leave a trace in the visible story, so the reveal feels unearned.

Example: Visible: an accidental fire. Hidden: arson. The visible story shows a gas can missing from the shed.

## Reveals: every field

A reveal is the moment a hidden truth comes out, to a character, to the reader, or to both. The record holds what the reveal must do, how it could happen, when, and who learns what. A strong reveal makes the reader look backward: earlier scenes take on a new meaning. It should feel surprising and, looking back, inevitable. That only works if the truth was planted fairly before.

**When to use it.** When you know a secret will come out, and again while placing clues, so every clue lands before the reveal that needs it.

**The common mistake.** Revealing a truth the reader had no way to see coming. Surprise without setup reads as a cheat, not a twist.

**Example.** The baker learns her sister's husband set the fire. Lands: the reader remembers his singed sleeve in chapter 4 and his talk of debt, and everything clicks. Fails: he confesses out of nowhere, and nothing earlier supports it.

### Reveals: What it does

*The reveal's dramatic job: what it changes in the story when the truth comes out.*

**What it must accomplish.** What the reveal must accomplish: recast earlier scenes, flip a relationship, force a decision, or raise the stakes. You can decide this while how it comes out stays open. Most books need this. A reveal without a job is just information. With one, it becomes a turn the whole book has been building toward.

The common mistake: Treating the reveal as the payoff itself, with nothing changing after it.

Example: Lands: "Turns the baker against the one person she was learning to trust." Fails: "We find out who started the fire."

### Reveals: How it happens

*The possible ways the truth could surface, kept side by side until you choose.*

**How it could happen.** How the truth could come out: a confession, a document, something overheard, a slip, a deduction. Options sit side by side until you choose. Useful for many books. The method shapes the emotion. A confession is intimate. A found letter is lonely. A public accusation is explosive.

The common mistake: Defaulting to someone explaining everything in a long speech.

Example: Options: the husband confesses; she finds the receipt for the fuel; their child repeats something he said. The child's slip hurts the most and costs him the most.

### Reveals: When

*Where the reveal sits in the story, which is usually much later than the event it reveals.*

**When it happens in the story.** When the reveal happens in the story, which is separate from when the original event happened. Useful for many books. Placement decides the reveal's force. The same truth lands as a Midpoint turn, an All Is Lost blow or a Finale key, depending on where you put it.

The common mistake: Holding it so long the reader has guessed it and grown impatient.

Example: "Day 40, the night before the reopening."

### Reveals: Truth and knowledge

*The exact truth revealed, who learns it here and who already knew, what the reader knew going in, and whether the hidden story now surfaces.*

**What actually happened.** The exact truth that comes out, stated plainly. Most books need this. Writing it precisely tells you which clues you owe the reader and keeps the reveal consistent with the event record.

The common mistake: Revealing a vaguer or different truth than the one you planted clues for.

Example: Lands: "He set the fire, and her sister knew within a week." Fails: "He was involved somehow."

**Who knows, and when.** Who learns the truth at this reveal, and who already knew. Useful for many books. A reveal often lands hardest on the character who learns that someone close already knew. Tracking this also shows what each character can do next.

The common mistake: Forgetting the characters who knew all along, who now have to react too.

Example: Lands: the baker learns it here, and also learns her sister knew. Fails: only the baker is listed, and the sister's guilt never comes up.

**What the reader knows or suspects.** What the reader knew or suspected before this reveal. Useful for many books. If the reader already knew, the reveal is about the character finding out, and the payoff is her reaction. If the reader didn't, it's about surprise, and the clues must be fair.

The common mistake: Not knowing which kind of reveal you're writing, so it's neither a shock nor a satisfying confirmation.

Example: Lands: the reader has watched him for twenty chapters, so the scene is about her face. Fails: the scene plays as a surprise to a reader who knew all along.

**Visible or hidden story.** Whether this reveal brings the hidden story up into the visible one. Only for some books. Reveals are where the two layers meet. Marking them shows where the hidden story surfaces and what stays buried. Situational: books with a secret or twist.

The common mistake: Surfacing the whole hidden story at once, leaving nothing for later turns.

Example: This reveal surfaces the arson. The insurance fraud stays hidden until the Finale.

## Clues: every field

A clue is a detail you plant that points toward a hidden truth. The record holds what it really means, where it's planted, where it pays off, what readers may mistake it for, and whether it's a false trail on purpose. Clues are what make a reveal feel fair. A careful reader should be able to look back and see the truth was there. In a mystery that fairness is the contract; in any book it's what makes a twist satisfying instead of arbitrary.

**When to use it.** When you know a reveal is coming. Plant first in your outline, then check in revision that every significant clue is placed before the reveal that needs it.

**The common mistake.** Planting a clue so plainly every reader solves it at once, or so faintly no one could. A good clue is visible but easy to read another way.

**Example.** The husband's singed sleeve in chapter 4. Lands: the baker assumes he burned it at the grill, and so does the reader. Fails: the narrator says, "Strange, a burn, the same week as the fire."

### Clues: Clue

*The clue itself: what it really means, whether it must pay off, where it's planted and where it pays off, and what it's mistaken for. This is the craft of setups and payoffs, and the fair-play tradition of mystery writing.*

**What it really means.** What the clue really means: the truth it points to. Most books need this. Writing the true meaning keeps every planting consistent and tells you which reveal it serves.

The common mistake: Planting a striking detail without deciding what it means, then forcing a meaning later.

Example: Lands: "The singed sleeve means he was near the oven the night of the fire." Fails: "Something about his sleeve."

**Significant enough that it must pay off?.** Whether this detail matters enough that it must pay off. Not every detail needs to. Useful for many books. Readers notice details the story lingers on and expect them to matter. Marking the ones that must pay off lets you check none are dropped.

The common mistake: Lingering on a detail that never pays off, which feels like a broken promise.

Example: Lands: the missing gas can is marked significant and pays off at the reveal. Fails: the camera lingers on a locked drawer that's never opened.

**Where it is planted.** Each place the clue appears before the reveal. A clue planted more than once is easier for a reader to recall later. Most books need this. Tracking plantings lets you confirm each one comes before the reveal and that the clue gets enough weight.

How it differs: This is a note in your words. The Setups and payoffs check reads Connections: connect the clue to a scene card with is planted in.

The common mistake: Planting it only once, in a busy scene, so no reader remembers it.

Example: Lands: the sleeve in chapter 4, the smell of fuel on his jacket in chapter 9. Fails: one mention, buried in a crowd scene.

**Where it pays off.** Where the clue pays off: where its meaning becomes clear. Most books need this. A clue that never pays off frustrates readers. Pairing plant and payoff makes gaps easy to find.

How it differs: This is a note in your words. The Setups and payoffs check reads Connections: connect the clue to a scene card with pays off in.

The common mistake: Paying it off in narration nobody notices, so the reader never feels the click.

Example: Lands: at the reveal the baker remembers the sleeve, and the reader does too. Fails: the reveal never mentions the sleeve, so the planting was wasted.

**What it is mistaken for.** What characters and readers are likely to take the clue for at first. Useful for many books. A clue works when it has an innocent reading. That cover story is what lets you plant it in plain sight.

The common mistake: Giving no cover, so the clue shouts its meaning.

Example: Lands: everyone assumes he burned the sleeve at the barbecue. Fails: there's no other reason a sleeve would be burned.

### Clues: Red herring

*For clues that are false trails on purpose: whether this one is, and what it draws attention away from.*

**Is this a red herring?.** Whether this is a red herring: a false trail planted on purpose to lead the reader toward the wrong answer. Only for some books. Red herrings keep a mystery from being solved too early. Fair ones have their own explanation once the truth comes out. Situational: mysteries, thrillers and suspense.

The common mistake: A false trail that's never explained, which feels like the author lied.

Example: Lands: the chain's manager is seen near the bakery that night, and later we learn he was trying to make an offer. Fails: he's there, and no one ever says why.

**What it draws attention away from.** What the red herring draws attention away from. Only for some books. A red herring works best when it pulls the eye from the real clue at the same moment. Naming the target keeps the misdirection purposeful.

The common mistake: A herring that distracts from nothing in particular, so it only adds noise.

Example: Lands: the manager's visit happens in the same scene as the husband's singed sleeve, so the reader watches the manager. Fails: the herring and the real clue are fifty pages apart.

## Payoffs: every field

A payoff is the later moment that rewards something set up earlier: a skill, an object, a line, a promise. Setup and payoff work as a pair; the idea is often called Chekhov's gun, after the playwright's advice that a gun shown early should be fired later. Payoffs make a story feel designed. When an early detail returns with new force, the reader feels the book was going somewhere all along. Unpaid setups feel like broken promises.

**When to use it.** When you plant something you intend to use, and in revision to check every significant setup has its payoff and every payoff its setup.

**The common mistake.** A payoff with no setup, like a skill the hero never had until she needs it. It feels convenient instead of earned.

**Example.** Chapter 2: the keeper's father teaches her to read the light's signal code. Lands: at the Finale she signals the stranded ferry with it. Fails: she suddenly knows the code in the Finale with no mention before.

### Payoffs: Clue

*The setup and its payoff: what the setup really means, whether it must pay off, where it's planted, where it pays off, and what readers first take it for.*

**What it really means.** What the setup really means once it pays off: why it was there. Most books need this. Naming the true purpose makes sure the setup carries the right weight and returns at the right moment.

The common mistake: Planting something for flavor, then trying to make it matter at the end.

Example: Lands: "The signal code is how she saves the ferry and how she finally honors her father." Fails: "The code is a nice detail."

**Significant enough that it must pay off?.** Whether this setup matters enough that it must pay off. Not every detail does. Useful for many books. Marking the ones that must pay off lets you check none are forgotten as the draft grows.

The common mistake: Treating every detail as a setup, which clutters the book and confuses readers about what matters.

Example: Lands: the code is marked significant. The father's pipe is just texture. Fails: the pipe gets three loving paragraphs and never returns.

**Where it is planted.** Each place the setup appears before the payoff. Most books need this. One mention is easy to forget. Two or three, each light, keep it alive in the reader's mind without giving away its use.

The common mistake: Planting it once, long before the payoff, so it feels new when it returns.

Example: Lands: the lesson in chapter 2, a scene where she taps the code idly in chapter 10. Fails: one line in chapter 2, then nothing until the end.

**Where it pays off.** Where the setup pays off. Most books need this. The payoff lands hardest where it does the most work, often at a turning point like the Finale.

The common mistake: Paying it off in a minor scene, wasting its force.

Example: Lands: she signals the ferry during the Finale storm. Fails: she uses the code once to order supplies.

**What it is mistaken for.** What readers first take the setup to be, before its real purpose is clear. Useful for many books. A setup that reads as character or texture slips past unnoticed, so the payoff surprises and satisfies at once.

The common mistake: A setup that announces itself, so the reader sees the payoff coming a hundred pages away.

Example: Lands: the lesson reads as a tender memory of her father. Fails: "Someday this code might save a life," he says.

### Payoffs: Red herring

*For setups that are false on purpose, built to make the reader expect a payoff that doesn't come, and what that misdirection hides.*

**Is this a red herring?.** Whether this setup is false on purpose: built to make the reader expect a payoff that doesn't come, or comes differently. Only for some books. A false setup can steer readers away from the real payoff. Situational: mysteries, suspense and books that play with expectation.

The common mistake: A false setup the story never acknowledges, which reads as a dropped thread, not a trick.

Example: Lands: everyone expects the flare gun to save the ferry, it's wet and useless, and the code does it. Fails: the flare gun is shown, then simply forgotten.

**What it draws attention away from.** What the false setup draws attention away from. Only for some books. Misdirection works when the reader's eye is busy elsewhere while the real setup is planted.

The common mistake: Misdirection that points nowhere useful, so it only adds noise.

Example: Lands: the flare gun gets the attention while she taps the code in the background. Fails: the flare gun and the code never appear near each other.

## Storylines: every field

A storyline is one thread of plot running through the book. Blake Snyder calls the main plot the A story and the second thread, often the relationship that carries the theme, the B story. A book can have more. Threads give a book texture and let one plot comment on another. Tracking each one shows where it stands, so threads don't vanish for a hundred pages or end without resolution.

**When to use it.** Once you know your book has more than one thread, and throughout revision to check each thread's state and promises.

**The common mistake.** Letting a thread go dormant without meaning to. Readers remember open threads, and a forgotten one feels like a hole.

**Example.** A story: the baker fights to keep the shop. B story: she and her sister learn to trust each other. Lands: the sister's help is what saves the shop, so the B story resolves the A story. Fails: the sister vanishes after the midpoint.

### Storylines: Storyline

*The thread's role, Blake Snyder story type, current state, how it resolves, the values it carries and the promises it makes to the reader.*

**Letter (A, B, C…).** An optional letter, A, B, C and so on, that marks this thread's role. The letter is its role, not its identity. Useful for many books. Letters show which thread carries the main plot and which support it. Snyder uses A for the main plot and B for the thread that carries the theme.

The common mistake: Treating the letter as a permanent name. A thread can change role in revision.

Example: Lands: the shop is A, the sisters are B, the rival chain is C. Fails: lettering every minor subplot until you reach H.

**Story type.** Blake Snyder's story type for this thread: Monster in the House, Golden Fleece, Out of the Bottle, Dude with a Problem, Rites of Passage, Buddy Love, Whydunit, The Fool Triumphant, Institutionalized or Superhero. A book can carry a different one in each storyline. Useful for many books. Each type has parts readers expect. Naming the type tells you what the thread owes them.

The common mistake: Picking a type by mood instead of by its parts.

Example: Lands: the sisters' thread is Buddy Love, so they need something that keeps them apart and a reason they're incomplete without each other. Fails: calling it Buddy Love with no barrier between them.

**Where it stands.** Where the thread stands now: Introduced, Developing, Escalating, Temporarily resolved, Dormant, Reopened, Resolved, Intentionally unresolved, or your own. Useful for many books. States show at a glance which threads are moving and which have stalled. A thread dormant too long fades from the reader's mind.

The common mistake: Leaving it at Introduced long after the thread has moved.

Example: Lands: the rival chain thread goes Dormant after the Midpoint, then is Reopened at All Is Lost. Fails: it goes quiet in chapter 8 and is never marked.

**Resolves now, or held open on purpose.** Whether the thread resolves within this book or is held open on purpose, as in a series or an ambiguous ending. Useful for many books. Deciding makes an open ending a choice instead of an oversight. Readers forgive an open thread if the story tells them it's meant to be.

The common mistake: Leaving a thread open by accident and calling it ambiguity.

Example: Lands: the shop is saved, but whether the sisters forgive the husband stays open, and the last scene shows her not answering his call. Fails: the rival chain is never mentioned again.

**Values it carries.** The values the thread carries, such as loyalty, independence or forgiveness. Values ride the plot lines; they are not threads of their own. Useful for many books. Naming values shows what each thread is really about and how threads echo each other's themes.

The common mistake: Making a value its own storyline with no plot attached, like "the forgiveness thread."

Example: Lands: the A story carries independence, the B story carries trust, and the climax forces them to collide. Fails: a "trust storyline" with no events in it.

**Promises made to the reader.** What this thread promises the reader: questions it raises, tensions it sets up, payoffs it implies. Most books need this. Every thread makes promises early, and readers keep track. Listing them lets you check each one is kept, broken on purpose, or held for a sequel.

The common mistake: Raising a question with a strong hook and never answering it.

Example: Lands: "Will the sisters ever bake together again?" answered in the Final Image. Fails: the first chapter hints at a family recipe that is never revealed.

## Set pieces: every field

A set piece is a big, memorable sequence, the scene readers describe to friends: the storm rescue, the wedding disaster, the chase across the market. Set pieces are where the premise pays off on the page. They draw several threads together and give the book its peaks. A book with none can feel flat even if every scene works.

**When to use it.** While outlining, often early, since a striking set piece can be a seed for the whole book. Return to it to make sure it does a story job, not just a show.

**The common mistake.** Building a spectacular sequence that changes nothing. If the story would be the same without it, it's decoration.

**Example.** The wedding where both rival florists' arrangements collapse during the vows. Lands: they have to work together in front of everyone, which turns the B story. Fails: it's funny, and the next chapter they're exactly as they were.

### Set pieces: Set piece

*What the sequence does for the story, which storylines it moves, and whether it lives on one card or across a sequence of scenes.*

**The story job it performs.** The story job the set piece performs: the turn, reveal or change it delivers. Most books need this. A set piece earns its space by doing work. Naming the job keeps the spectacle tied to the story.

The common mistake: Describing what the reader sees instead of what changes.

Example: Lands: "Forces the rivals to rescue the wedding together, the first time they cooperate." Fails: "A big, funny wedding disaster."

**Storylines it moves.** The storylines this set piece moves forward. Useful for many books. The best set pieces move several threads at once, which is part of why they feel big.

The common mistake: A set piece that moves only one thread while the others stand still.

Example: Lands: the wedding moves the A story (the contract) and the B story (the rivals' truce) at once. Fails: it moves only the contract, and the romance just watches.

**One card or a sequence.** Whether the set piece is one card (a single scene) or a sequence of cards across several scenes or chapters. Useful for many books. Knowing the shape helps you plan pacing and whether to break the sequence with other threads.

The common mistake: Cramming a sequence into one scene so it feels rushed, or stretching a single moment across chapters.

Example: Lands: the storm rescue is a sequence: the warning, the launch, the search, the return. Fails: all four in two pages.

## Secret operations: every field

A hidden move is something a character does in secret, offstage, that drives the plot while others don't see it. Together these make up the secret operations: the hidden story under the visible one. When a character is secretly acting, the visible story has causes the reader can feel but not see. Tracking the moves keeps the hidden story logical, so the eventual reveal holds up when readers look back.

**When to use it.** When your book has an antagonist, ally or secret keeper acting out of sight. Plan the moves alongside the visible beats.

**The common mistake.** Inventing the hidden moves after the reveal to explain it. Readers can tell when the secret plan doesn't match what they saw.

**Example.** The sister's husband secretly takes out a loan against the shop. Others see him working late and think he's helping. Lands: at the reveal, every late night makes sense. Fails: the loan appears only in the confession.

### Secret operations: The move

*When the move happens, what it is, what the others see and how they misread it, and who takes the blame.*

**When (beat or rough point).** When the hidden move happens, by beat or rough point in the story. Useful for many books. Placing it against the visible beats shows how the secret story pushes the visible one.

The common mistake: Leaving it unplaced, so the visible story's effects show up before their hidden cause.

Example: "Just before the Midpoint, the week of the city fair."

**The hidden move.** What the character actually does in secret. Most books need this. Writing it plainly lets you check that it's possible, motivated and consistent with what others later learn.

The common mistake: Keeping it vague, so you can't plant fair signs of it.

Example: Lands: "He forges her signature on the loan papers." Fails: "He's up to something."

**What the others see, and how they misread it.** What the other characters see of the move, and how they misread it. Most books need this. This is the visible trace of the hidden story: the part you can put on the page. It's also where fair clues come from.

The common mistake: Leaving no visible trace, so the reveal has nothing to stand on.

Example: Lands: they see him at the bank and assume he's paying the mortgage. Fails: no one ever sees anything.

**Who takes the blame.** Who takes the blame for the move's effects, if anyone, until the truth comes out. Useful for many books. Misplaced blame creates conflict in the visible story and raises the cost of the reveal.

The common mistake: Blaming a character with no reason to be suspected, so the misdirection feels forced.

Example: Lands: the baker blames the chain for the debt and refuses their help. Fails: she blames a stranger introduced that chapter.

## Kept lines: every field

A kept line is a line of dialogue or prose you want to use somewhere in the book but haven't placed yet. Good lines come at odd times: in the shower, while drafting another chapter. Keeping them in one place means they aren't lost, and you can choose the moment they'll land hardest.

**When to use it.** Any time a line arrives before its scene does. Return to them while drafting and revising to place them.

**The common mistake.** Forcing a kept line into a scene where it doesn't fit, because you love it. A line that fits nowhere can wait for another book.

**Example.** "You can't fix a recipe by shouting at the oven." Lands: the baker's mother says it in a flashback, and the baker says it to her daughter in the last chapter. Fails: it's dropped into an argument where it reads as a joke.

### Kept lines: The line

*Who says it and how it's delivered.*

**Said by (in the story).** Who says the line in the story. Useful for many books. The same words land differently from different mouths. Choosing a speaker tests whether the line fits a voice.

The common mistake: Giving a line to whoever is nearby, when it only sounds right from one character.

Example: Lands: the gruff old keeper says the tender line, which surprises the reader. Fails: a character who never talks that way says it.

**How it is delivered.** How the line is delivered: emphasis, timing, physical business. Business means the small action a speaker does while talking. Useful for many books. Delivery can make a plain line powerful or a clever line fall flat. Noting it keeps the effect you heard when you wrote it.

The common mistake: Stacking adverbs ("she said bitterly") instead of showing delivery through action and timing.

Example: Lands: she says it without looking up, still wiping the counter. Fails: "she said, sadly and angrily."

### Kept lines: Where it goes

*Possible places the line could go. Keeping a line does not place it.*

**Where it might go.** Where the line might go. Candidates can sit side by side until you choose. Useful for many books. A line's power depends on placement. The same words can open a chapter, end one or return as a callback.

How it differs: Notes on where it might go. To tie the line to a scene, connect it under Connections with appears in.

The common mistake: Placing it at the first chance instead of the best one.

Example: Candidates: the opening scene, the All Is Lost, the Final Image. Lands: saved for the Final Image, where it echoes. Fails: used in chapter 1, then nothing to echo.

## Motifs: every field

A motif is a recurring image, object, sound or phrase that gathers meaning each time it returns: a lighthouse beam, a cracked bowl, a song on the radio. Motifs carry meaning without stating it. When an image returns changed, the reader feels the story's change in one quick picture. That's especially valuable in literary and upmarket fiction.

**When to use it.** Often you discover a motif while drafting, then strengthen it in revision. Sometimes you choose one early as a thread for the whole book.

**The common mistake.** Repeating the image without letting its meaning change. Then it's decoration, and readers start to notice the repetition instead of the meaning.

**Example.** The baker's mother's cracked mixing bowl. Lands: at first a reminder of loss, then something she's afraid to use, and finally the bowl her daughter mixes in. Fails: it sits on the shelf in five chapters and means the same thing each time.

### Motifs: Motif

*What the motif means, and how that meaning changes across the book.*

**What it means.** What the motif means at each point in the book. Add a version for each stage as its meaning shifts. Most books need this. Tracking the shift shows whether the motif grows with the story. A motif that changes meaning is how an image becomes part of the arc.

The common mistake: Writing one fixed meaning ("the bowl means grief") and never letting it move.

Example: Lands: the beam means duty at the start, a trap in the middle and a welcome at the end. Fails: the beam means hope on every page.

## Themes: every field

A Theme record holds an idea your story explores: what it is about underneath what happens. Theme is what the reader carries away. When you name it, you can test scenes against it and give your characters opposing answers to the same question, which is where theme becomes drama instead of a lecture.

**When to use it.** Name a theme when you notice what keeps coming up as you plan or draft. Many writers only see their theme after a first draft; that's normal.

**The common mistake.** Stating the theme as a moral and making characters preach it. A theme is a question the story argues with itself, not an answer it announces.

**Example.** Lands: "Can you protect people without controlling them?" Each character answers differently. Fails: "Friendship is important," said aloud in the last chapter.

**Description.** The theme in a sentence or a question, and how the story explores it. So you can check scenes against it and keep the argument alive on both sides.

## Values: every field

A Value record names something a character or the story treats as precious: freedom, loyalty, safety, truth. Robert McKee's idea is that every scene turns a value from positive to negative or the reverse. Naming the values lets you see what is at stake in each scene and whether it changed.

**When to use it.** Name values when you plan scenes or when a scene feels flat and you can't see why.

**The common mistake.** Picking values too general to measure. "Love" is hard to track; "trust between two sisters" can go up and down scene by scene.

**Example.** A ferry captain values safety above all. A scene where she refuses to sail turns safety positive and her income negative. Both can be tracked.

**Description.** The value in a few words and how it is tested in the story. So you can see what each scene puts at risk.

## Questions I have: every field

A question is something about your story you haven't answered yet: why a character would do that, how a plot point could work, what the ending should be. Open questions are part of writing. Keeping them as records means they're not lost in a notebook, and you can work through them with possible answers and the assumptions behind them, without committing too soon.

**When to use it.** Any time you hit a gap or a doubt. Come back when you're ready to decide.

**The common mistake.** Answering a question just to close it, with the first idea, when the story needs a better one.

**Example.** "Why doesn't the keeper just leave the island?" Possible answers: duty, fear, a promise to her father. Assumption: that leaving is easy. Lands: questioning that, you realize the causeway floods, so leaving is really a choice to risk drowning.

### Questions I have: Thinking it through

*Possible answers, the assumptions hiding behind the question, and the answer once you decide it.*

**Possible answers.** Possible answers to the question, side by side until you choose. Useful for many books. Writing several answers helps you avoid the first, most familiar one, and shows which answer creates the most story.

The common mistake: Writing only one candidate, which isn't really choosing.

Example: Question: why does the rival lend her the generator? Candidates: guilt, attraction, a plan to sabotage her. Lands: the third opens a later reveal. Fails: "Because he's nice."

**Assumptions behind it.** The assumptions hidden in the question: things you're taking for granted that might not be true. Useful for many books. Many stuck questions are stuck because of an assumption. Naming it often shows a way out.

The common mistake: Treating the assumption as a fact of the story.

Example: Question: how does she pay the rent? Assumption: she has to pay it. Lands: what if her mother owned the building, and the lease was always a lie?

**Answer.** The answer, once you know it. When you mark it Decided, the question moves to Answered. Useful for many books. A decided answer becomes something the rest of the story can rely on. Mark it decided when you're sure, and update the records it affects.

The common mistake: Answering here and forgetting to carry the answer into the records it changes.

Example: Lands: "She stays because her father's ashes are in the lamp room," then updated on the keeper's character record. Fails: answered here, and her record still says she stays out of duty.

## Things and ideas I'm thinking about: every field

An idea is a possibility you're thinking about but haven't committed to: a twist, a character, a setting, a scene, a line of inquiry. Ideas need somewhere to wait. Keeping them as records means you can weigh what each would change and where it might fit before letting it into the canon.

**When to use it.** Any time an idea arrives. Return to them when you're stuck or revising.

**The common mistake.** Folding an idea straight into the story without checking what it would break. A great idea in the wrong place can cost you three chapters.

**Example.** Idea: the stranger is the keeper's half brother. Lands: you note it would change her father's arc and the stranger's motive, and might fit at the Midpoint. Fails: you add it to chapter 12 and later find it contradicts chapter 3.

### Things and ideas I'm thinking about: Thinking it through

*What the idea might change if you used it, and where in the story it might fit.*

**What it might change.** What would change in the story if you used this idea: characters, events, reveals, the ending. Useful for many books. Listing the ripples shows whether the idea strengthens the book or quietly breaks it.

The common mistake: Seeing only the exciting part and missing what it would undo.

Example: Lands: "If they're siblings, the romantic subplot has to go, and the father becomes a liar." Fails: "It would be a great twist."

**Where it might fit.** Where the idea might go: which beat, storyline or scene. Candidates can sit side by side. Useful for many books. The right placement can make a good idea great. Noting a few options lets you choose with the whole book in view.

The common mistake: Fitting it wherever you happen to be drafting.

Example: Candidates: the Midpoint, All Is Lost, the Final Image. Lands: at All Is Lost it doubles the blow. Fails: in chapter 2, before the reader cares.

## Captured thoughts: every field

A Captured thought is anything you saved with Capture: an idea, a line, a worry, a question. Ideas arrive at bad times. Capture keeps them exactly as you said or typed them, so you can keep writing and sort them later.

**When to use it.** Use Capture (Ctrl+Shift+N) any time, especially mid-scene. Sort captured thoughts when you plan: file each as a question or an idea, or connect it to a record.

**The common mistake.** Letting captured thoughts pile up unread. Look through them once a week.

**Example.** Driving home you think: "What if the lighthouse keeper's brother is the one who cut the cable?" Capture it in ten seconds and file it as an idea on Sunday.

**Description.** The thought, exactly as captured. So nothing you thought of is lost or reworded.

## Notes: every field

A Note is a free-form record for anything that doesn't fit another kind. Not everything is a character or a beat. Notes keep the leftovers inside the novel, where search can find them.

**When to use it.** Use a note when no other kind fits.

**The common mistake.** Using notes for everything. A note can't be checked or placed the way a character or a scene card can.

**Example.** A list of names you might use for the town's bakeries.

**Description.** Anything you want to keep. So it's with the novel and searchable.

## Objects: every field

An Object record holds one thing in your story that matters: a ring, a letter, a car, a recipe card. Objects carry meaning the reader can see and touch. A thing that keeps coming back (and changes hands, breaks, or is finally used) can show a change in a character without a word of explanation.

**When to use it.** Make one when a thing shows up twice, or when you know it will matter later.

**The common mistake.** Making a record for every prop. Only objects that do work in the story need one.

**Example.** A baker keeps her late mother's chipped measuring cup. In chapter one she won't let anyone touch it. In the last chapter she hands it to her apprentice. The cup says what the baker has learned.

**Description.** What the object is, what it looks like, who has it, and what it means. So it stays consistent, and so you can track where it is and what it means each time it appears.

## Places: every field

A Place record holds one location in your story: a kitchen, a harbor town, a whole city, or a world on the far side of a door. It keeps what the place is like and what it does to the people in it. Readers live in your places for hundreds of pages. A place you know well gives you details that stay consistent, moods that shift on purpose, and pressure on your characters (a small town where everyone talks, a city too big to be found in). A place you only half know turns into a gray stage where people stand and talk.

**When to use it.** Start a Place when a scene first lands somewhere that matters. Add to it as you draft, and check it whenever you return to that place so the doors, the smells and the neighbors stay the same.

**The common mistake.** Building a place like a travel brochure: lots of description, no effect on the story. A place earns its pages when it helps or blocks what someone wants.

**Example.** A lighthouse keeper's tower is ninety-one steps up, with one window that faces the reef. That matters: when the storm comes, she can see the boat going down but cannot reach it in time. The stairs are not decoration. They are the conflict.

### Places: Place

*The basics of any location, large or small: what it is like, the mood it gives off, the season you see it in, and who you usually find there, details to reuse, and what it means to the story. Fill these for every place that hosts a scene.*

**What it is like.** The physical facts of the place: size, layout, materials, light, what you see when you walk in. Most books need this. This is your reference so the back door stays on the left in chapter three and chapter thirty. Without it, readers catch the contradictions and stop trusting the page.

The common mistake: Listing everything at once. Write the details you need to remember, then reveal them in the story only as characters notice them.

Example: Lands: "A bakery in a former bank. The vault is the walk-in cooler, and the steel door still sticks." Fails: "A nice cozy bakery with tables and a counter."

**Atmosphere.** The mood the place gives off: the feeling a person has when they are there. Atmosphere is emotion attached to a location. Useful for many books. Atmosphere tells the reader how to feel before anyone speaks. It also lets you create contrast, such as a cheerful place where something terrible happens.

The common mistake: Naming the mood with adjectives ("eerie," "cozy") instead of the details that cause it. Write what produces the feeling.

Example: Lands: "The flower shop is too quiet since the rival opened across the street. The bell over the door has not rung all morning." Fails: "It feels sad."

**Season.** The time of year we usually see this place, or the season it is in during the story. Useful for many books. Season changes light, clothing, travel, food and mood. A harbor in January is a different place from the same harbor in July, and the reader feels that.

The common mistake: Setting a season and then forgetting it, so characters stroll in shirtsleeves in a scene that should be freezing.

Example: Lands: "Late October. The ferry stops running after the fifteenth, so anyone still on the island is staying all winter." The season creates a trap.

**Who is usually here.** The people, or kinds of people, you usually find in this place. Add one entry per person or group. Useful for many books. A place is partly its regulars. Knowing who is here tells you who might overhear, who might help, and who would notice a stranger.

The common mistake: Leaving places empty except for the main characters, which makes the world feel staged.

Example: "The diner at 6 a.m.: two retired cops at the counter, the night nurse in the corner booth, and the owner's son pretending to do homework."

**Details to reuse.** Small, concrete things about this place you can bring back: a cracked step, a smell, a sound at night. Useful for many books. A detail that returns makes a place feel lived in, and a detail that changes shows time passing without a word of explanation.

Example: The porch light that buzzes before it comes on. By the last chapter someone has fixed it.

**What it means to the story.** Why the story happens here and not somewhere else: what this place stands for, to the people in it and to the book. Useful for many books. A place that means something does work in every scene set there. It can stand for safety, shame, or the life someone wants.

Example: The garden is the one thing the widow next door still controls. Every scene there is about control.

### Places: Settlement

*For a town, city, village or camp: how it is governed, fed, defended and divided. These come from classic worldbuilding practice, which asks how a community actually survives. Use them when your story spends real time in one community.*

**Who governs, and how.** Who holds authority in this settlement, and how they hold it: a mayor, a council, a lord, a gang, a church, or no one at all. Only for some books. Rules and the people who enforce them create obstacles. If your hero needs permission, protection or a blind eye, this field tells you who to ask and what it costs.

The common mistake: Naming a ruler but never showing how their power touches ordinary life. Power should show up in curfews, fees, fear or favors.

Example: Lands: "The harbor master answers to no one on land. Every ship pays him, and he decides who docks first." Fails: "There is a king."

**How people earn a living.** How people here earn a living: the main trades, who buys, who sells, and what the town depends on. Only for some books. Money shapes daily life and pressure. A town that depends on one mill or one mine has a shared fear, and that fear can drive a plot.

The common mistake: Inventing an economy that could not feed anyone, like a remote village that seems to live on selling crafts to no one.

Example: "Everyone here works for the cannery or sells to the people who do. When the cannery talks about closing, the whole town holds its breath."

**Water and food.** Where the settlement gets its water and food, and how secure that supply is. Only for some books. Water and food are the first things a real community solves. Knowing them gives you natural stakes, such as drought, a poisoned well or a blockade.

The common mistake: Ignoring supply entirely in a story where siege, travel or survival matters. Readers notice when a besieged city never gets hungry.

Example: "One spring feeds the whole village, and it sits on land owned by the family everyone hates." The supply becomes a source of conflict.

**Roads, travel and power.** How people and goods move in and out, and what keeps the lights or fires burning: roads, rivers, rail, horses, power lines, firewood. Only for some books. Travel time controls pacing and plot. If the only road washes out, nobody leaves. If the power fails, the story changes.

The common mistake: Letting characters cross great distances instantly when the world says it should take days.

Example: Lands: "One bridge connects the town to the highway. It closes at night for repairs." Fails: travel that takes as long as the plot needs it to, with no rule.

**Defenses.** How the settlement protects itself: walls, guards, police, a militia, the sea, or reputation. Only for some books. Defenses tell you how hard it is to get in, get out, or cause trouble. They matter whenever your story has a threat from outside or a crime inside.

The common mistake: Making defenses strong when the plot needs them strong and weak when it needs them weak. Decide them once and let the characters work around them.

Example: "The town has one deputy, and he is also the school bus driver. Between seven and nine in the morning, there is no law here."

**Neighborhoods and districts.** The neighborhoods or sections of the settlement, each with its own character. Add one entry per district. Only for some books. Districts let one city hold many moods and classes. Moving a character from one to another can signal danger, ambition or exile.

The common mistake: Naming districts that all feel the same. Each should differ in who lives there, what it sounds like, or what happens there.

Example: "The Flats: the old mill houses by the river, cheap rent, flooded every spring. The Hill: where the mill owners lived, now lawyers and dentists."

**Who is rich and who is poor, and where each lives.** Who has money and who does not, and where each group lives. Only for some books. Class is a source of friction, envy and secrets. Knowing the line between rich and poor tells you who looks down on whom, and who is trying to cross.

The common mistake: Making wealth purely decoration. Money should change what characters can do, what they fear, and how others treat them.

Example: "The rival florist rents a storefront on Main Street. Our heroine sells from a van parked at the edge of the market, and everyone knows the difference."

**Who lives here, and how many.** Who lives here and roughly how many: size, mix of peoples, ages, newcomers and old families. Only for some books. Size controls how private life can be. In a village of three hundred, secrets do not last. In a city of millions, anyone can vanish.

The common mistake: Choosing a size that contradicts the story, like a 'tiny town' where nobody knows anyone.

Example: "About four hundred people, most of them related. A new face at church is news by lunch."

**What it sounds and smells like.** What the settlement sounds and smells like: the noises and odors a newcomer would notice and a local would stop hearing. Useful for many books. Sound and smell make a place real faster than sight does, and they trigger memory. One good smell detail can do the work of a paragraph.

The common mistake: Using only sight. Readers already picture what things look like. Sound and smell surprise them.

Example: Lands: "Low tide smells like rot and salt. The church bell is always four minutes late." Fails: "It smells nice and is pretty quiet."

### Places: Between worlds

*For stories with more than one world, such as portal fantasy or a hidden magical world inside ours. These fields track which world a place belongs to, where the doors are, and what crossing costs.*

**Which world this place is in.** Which world this place belongs to: our ordinary world, a hidden world inside ours, or a separate world of its own. Only for some books. In stories with more than one world, readers need to know where they are standing and which rules apply. This field keeps you from mixing them up.

The common mistake: Letting the worlds blur so that the rules of one leak into the other without a reason.

Example: "The bookshop is in our world. The reading room behind the third shelf is in the hidden one, and the clocks there run backward."

**Doorways and crossing points.** The doors, gates, mirrors or thin places where someone can pass from one world to another. Add one entry per crossing. Only for some books. Crossings are where plot happens: chases, escapes, arrivals, things that should not come through. Knowing them lets you plan who can reach whom.

The common mistake: Adding a new crossing whenever a character is stuck. Too many doors make every danger escapable.

Example: "The old well behind the chapel. It only opens on nights with no moon, and you must climb down, never jump."

**The rules for crossing, and what it costs.** The rules for passing between worlds and what crossing costs: who can cross, when, and what they lose or risk. Only for some books. A crossing with no cost has no tension. A cost (a memory, a year of life, a promise) makes every trip a real choice.

The common mistake: Inventing the cost and then never charging it. If crossing costs a memory, show a character forgetting something they loved.

Example: Lands: "Each crossing takes one name from your memory. She has already forgotten her sister's." Fails: "You can cross whenever you want."

## Story world: every field

The Story world record holds the facts that cover the whole book: when it happens, how long it lasts, how it is told, and what it is about at its core. There is one of these per project. These are the decisions every scene leans on. Viewpoint and tense shape every sentence. The one-sentence and one-paragraph summaries, the moral premise and the controlling idea keep the book pointed at one meaning. When these are vague, chapters drift, and the ending feels like it belongs to a different book.

**When to use it.** Fill what you can at the very start, even as guesses, and revise as the draft teaches you. Return here when a scene feels aimless or when you need to explain your book to someone in one breath.

**The common mistake.** Treating this as paperwork to finish once. These answers change as you write. Keep older versions as Developing, and mark one Decided only when you are sure.

**Example.** A writer starts a novel about two rival florists with only a hunch: "pride ruins them, generosity saves them." That hunch becomes the moral premise, and it tells her the ending: one florist gives the other her best customer.

### Story world: The core

*The heart of the book in compressed form. The one sentence and one paragraph come from Randy Ingermanson's Snowflake Method, which grows a novel outward from a single sentence.*

**The one sentence.** One sentence of about twenty-five words that captures the whole book. Randy Ingermanson's Snowflake Method starts here. Blake Snyder adds that a strong logline carries irony: a built-in contradiction that makes the premise intriguing. Most books need this. If you can say the book in one sentence, you know what it is about, and the draft stays on course.

The common mistake: Cramming in every subplot and name. Stick to the central situation.

Example: Lands: "A lighthouse keeper who has never saved anyone must keep a shipwreck survivor alive through a winter storm, though he may be the man who drowned her brother." Fails: "A story about grief and hope on an island."

**The one paragraph.** Five sentences: the setup, three disasters, and the ending. In Ingermanson's Snowflake Method this is the second step, growing the one sentence into the shape of the book. A disaster here means a turning point that makes things much worse and forces a new choice. Most books need this. Three disasters give the book a spine, so each act knows what it builds toward.

The common mistake: Disasters that are just bad luck. Each should force a harder choice.

Example: Setup: a baker opens on the same street as her old boss. Disaster one: he steals her recipe. Two: she copies his to retaliate and gets caught. Three: the street votes to close one shop. Ending: she wins by confessing.

**How it might end.** Your best current guess at how the story ends: who wins, who loses, and what is true at the end that was not true at the start. Most books need this. An ending found early gives the middle somewhere to go. Every scene can then build toward it, and the setup can plant what the ending needs.

The common mistake: Refusing to guess until the draft is finished, which often produces a sagging middle that wanders.

Example: "She keeps the lighthouse but loses the man." Even as a guess (marked Developing), this tells you the book needs to make both the lighthouse and the man precious.

### Story world: Moral premise

*From Stanley D. Williams's moral premise: the theme stated as an equation, where a vice leads to defeat and a virtue leads to success. It helps every subplot argue the same truth.*

**The vice.** In Stanley D. Williams's moral premise, the vice is the wrong value or behavior that leads characters to defeat. It is the first half of the equation "[vice] leads to defeat, but [virtue] leads to success." Useful for many books. Naming the vice gives you a way to test every subplot: the characters who cling to it should suffer for it. It turns theme from a slogan into consequences.

The common mistake: Choosing a vice that is a crime or a cartoon ("murder") instead of a temptation real people feel ("control," "pride," "hiding").

Example: Lands: "Keeping score." Both rival florists track every slight, and it costs each of them something. Fails: "Being evil."

**The virtue.** The virtue is the right value or behavior that leads to success, the second half of Williams's moral premise. It is usually the opposite of the vice. Useful for many books. The virtue is what your hero must learn or choose by the end. It shows you what the climax must test.

The common mistake: Picking a virtue that does not truly oppose the vice, so the theme splits in two.

Example: Lands: vice "keeping score," virtue "giving without counting." The climax: one florist gives away her best arrangement with no hope of return. Fails: vice "pride," virtue "courage."

**The moral premise, as one sentence.** The full moral premise in one sentence: "[Vice] leads to [defeat], but [virtue] leads to [success]." Stanley D. Williams argues that every successful story argues one such truth. Useful for many books. One sentence lets you check any scene: does it show the vice failing or the virtue working? Scenes that do neither may not belong.

The common mistake: Writing a sermon. The premise guides you; the story should prove it through events, never announce it.

Example: "Keeping score leads to loneliness, but giving without counting leads to belonging." The two florists then embody each half until one changes.

### Story world: Controlling idea

*From Robert McKee: the meaning of the story in one sentence, naming the value at stake and the cause that changes it.*

**Controlling idea.** Robert McKee's controlling idea: the meaning of the story in one sentence, naming the value at the end (love, justice, life) and the cause that brought it there. A value is something characters care about that can swing positive or negative. Useful for many books. It forces you to say both what happens to the value and why. That cause is your theme in action, and it keeps the climax honest.

The common mistake: Naming only a topic ("grief") instead of a value and a cause.

Example: Lands: "Love returns when we stop guarding our old wounds." Fails: "This book is about love."

### Story world: Time

*When the story happens and how long it lasts. These fields anchor the calendar so dates, seasons and ages stay consistent.*

**Era or year.** When the story takes place: a specific year, a decade, an age of an invented world. Most books need this. Era decides technology, manners, money, speech and what characters can know. A story set in 1985 has no cell phones, and that changes how secrets travel.

The common mistake: Choosing an era for flavor and then writing modern people with modern assumptions in it.

Example: Lands: "Summer 1977. The blackout hits the city in the middle of the second act." The era supplies a real event that drives plot.

**Season.** The season the story mainly happens in, or the season it opens with. Useful for many books. Season sets weather, light, holidays and work rhythms, and it can mirror the story's mood. A story that runs from autumn to spring gives you a built-in arc of dark to light.

The common mistake: Forgetting the season after chapter one, so weather never touches anyone.

Example: "It opens in the first week of December. Every chapter moves closer to the holiday the family is dreading."

**The atmosphere now.** The overall mood of the story's world at the time the book opens: the social weather, what everyone is feeling or fearing. Useful for many books. Characters do not live in a vacuum. A town bracing for a layoff, a city during a heat wave, a kingdom after a war: the general mood colors every private scene.

The common mistake: Describing a mood that never touches the characters. The atmosphere should press on someone's choices.

Example: "The whole island is waiting to hear if the ferry company will cut service. Every conversation circles back to it."

**How long the story lasts.** How much story time passes from the first page to the last: a weekend, a season, a year, a lifetime. Most books need this. Duration sets pace and pressure. A short span creates urgency. A long one allows slow change, but needs clear markers so readers do not lose track.

The common mistake: Never deciding, so the timeline stretches or shrinks to fit each scene and the reader cannot tell if a day or a month has passed.

Example: Lands: "Eleven days, from the funeral to the reading of the will." Fails: "A while."

**Name of your story clock.** A name for your story's internal clock, used to label when each scene happens, such as "day of the feud" or "days since the storm." Useful for many books. A story clock lets you place every scene on one timeline and spot problems, like two events on the same day that cannot both happen. It is a tool for you, not something the reader necessarily sees.

The common mistake: Using real calendar dates when the story's own count would be clearer, or using no count and losing track of time.

Example: "Day of the siege." Scene cards then read Day 1, Day 4, Day 9, and you can see at a glance that nothing happens for five days in the middle.

### Story world: Viewpoint

*How the story is told: whose eyes we see through, in which grammatical person and tense, and what rule governs switching. These are decisions about every sentence in the book.*

**Viewpoint approach.** Your overall plan for viewpoint: whose eyes we see through, how many viewpoint characters you have, and how you move between them. Viewpoint (POV) means the character whose thoughts and senses the reader is given. Most books need this. Viewpoint decides what the reader can know and feel. One narrator gives intimacy and limits. Several narrators give range and let the reader know more than any one character.

The common mistake: Adding viewpoint characters whenever you need information delivered, until no one voice holds the reader.

Example: Lands: "Alternating first person between the two rival florists, one chapter each." Fails: "Whoever is in the scene."

**Viewpoint rule.** The rule for when viewpoint can change: once per scene, once per chapter, or freely. Most books need this. A clear rule keeps readers oriented. Breaking your own rule mid-scene (called head-hopping, jumping from one mind to another without a break) confuses them about whose feelings they are reading.

The common mistake: Setting a rule and then breaking it by accident when a scene gets busy.

Example: "One viewpoint per scene, marked by a scene break." Then if the lighthouse keeper's scene suddenly tells us what the sailor is thinking, you know it breaks the rule.

**Default narrative person.** The default grammatical person: first ("I"), second ("you"), or third ("she," "he," "they"). Most books need this. First person feels close and confessional. Third person allows more range and can move closer or farther from a character. The choice sets the voice of every line.

The common mistake: Picking first person for intimacy but then needing to show scenes the narrator could never witness.

Example: A mystery told in first person by the detective keeps the reader as puzzled as she is. Told in third, you could show the killer's scenes too. Each choice makes a different book.

**Default tense.** The default verb tense of the narration: past ("she ran") or present ("she runs"). Most books need this. Past tense is the familiar storytelling voice and feels invisible to most readers. Present tense feels immediate and breathless. Both work; consistency matters most.

The common mistake: Slipping between tenses without meaning to, especially during action scenes.

Example: "Past tense throughout, except the prologue, which is present tense because it happens after the ending." A deliberate exception, written down.

### Story world: Knowledge

*Whether the book keeps a secret from the reader. Turning this on lets Story Canon track what the reader knows versus what is true.*

**Does this book hide a truth from the reader?.** Whether your book keeps a significant truth from the reader on purpose, to reveal later. Your answer reminds you and the collaborator; the Who knows what view in Narrative Zoom, which tracks what the reader knows versus what is really happening, is always there for you to use. Only for some books. Mysteries, thrillers and twist stories depend on managing what the reader knows. Tracking the gap keeps clues fair and the reveal satisfying instead of cheap.

The common mistake: Hiding a truth by having a viewpoint character simply not think about it, which readers feel as a cheat.

Example: Lands: the baker's mentor is secretly her father, and three small clues are planted before the reveal. Fails: the truth appears from nowhere in the last chapter.

## Story rules: every field

A Story rule records something that must always be true in your book: how magic works, what a law allows, what a character can never do. Readers forgive almost anything except a story that breaks its own rules. Writing them down keeps you consistent across hundreds of pages, and the program and collaborator can check against them.

**When to use it.** Add one the moment you invent a limit or a law, especially in fantasy, mystery and science fiction.

**The common mistake.** Writing rules you then quietly break to get out of a corner. If the ending needs the rule bent, the rule needs rewriting early.

**Example.** Lands: "The ferry cannot cross after dark; the harbor master locks the chain." Every late-night plan has to work around it. Fails: the chain is simply forgotten in the climax.

**Description.** The rule, stated plainly, and any exceptions you've decided. So every scene obeys it, and you notice before a scene breaks it.

## Research: every field

A Research record holds something you looked up: a fact, a source, a detail of a job or a place. Research is easy to lose and hard to find again. Keeping it with the novel means the detail you need in November is one search away, with where you found it.

**When to use it.** Add research as you find it, and during drafting leave a note for later instead of stopping to look things up.

**The common mistake.** Researching instead of writing. Collect enough to write the scene, mark the rest as a note for later.

**Example.** How long a sourdough starter keeps in a fridge, with the bakery website it came from, saved before you write the scene where it's forgotten.

**Description.** What you found, and where you found it. So you can trust it later and check the source again.

## Guardrails: every field

A Guardrail is something that must stay true about a character or the story. The collaborator checks every suggestion against it. Guardrails protect the decisions you care about most from drift, your own and the collaborator's. They are how you say: whatever else changes, not this.

**When to use it.** Add one when you catch yourself, or the collaborator, pulling a character somewhere they would never go.

**The common mistake.** Writing guardrails as plot points. A guardrail is a boundary ("she never lies to her daughter"), not an event.

**Example.** "The baker never sells her mother's recipe, whatever it costs her." Any suggestion that has her sell it is flagged.

**Description.** The thing that must stay true, in one sentence. So nothing quietly breaks it.

## Soundtrack: every field

A Song record holds one piece of music that matters in your story: a song a character loves, one that plays at a key moment, or one that defines an era. Together they form your book's soundtrack. Music carries memory and emotion faster than description. A single title can bring back a first dance, a dead parent's car, a summer. Keeping songs in records lets you reuse them on purpose, so a song heard happily in chapter two can wound in chapter twenty.

**When to use it.** Add songs whenever one shows up in a scene or in your head while drafting. Review them when planning echoes and callbacks.

**The common mistake.** Quoting lyrics. Song lyrics are usually copyrighted and costly to license. Use titles and artists only, and describe the feeling.

**Example.** A widow hears the song from her wedding on a grocery store speaker and leaves her cart in the aisle. You never quote a line. The title and her reaction do all the work.

### Soundtrack: The song

*The facts about the song and the job it does in your story. Titles and artists can be named freely; lyrics should not be quoted.*

**Artist.** Who performed or wrote the song. For an invented song, the invented artist. Useful for many books. The artist places the song in a time and a culture, and says something about the character who loves it.

The common mistake: Getting a real artist or release detail wrong, which readers who love that music will catch.

Example: A teenager in 2026 who loves a band from 1972 tells us something about who raised her.

**Year it came out.** The year the song was released. Useful for many books. The year keeps your timeline honest. A character cannot hear a song before it existed, and the year can date a memory precisely.

The common mistake: Anachronism: a song playing on the radio years before its release.

Example: Lands: a scene set in summer 1985 uses a song from 1984, still on the radio. Fails: the same scene uses a song released in 1987.

**What it carries.** The feeling or memory this song holds in the story: what it means to the characters who hear it. Titles only, never lyrics. Most books need this. This is why the song is in the book. Once a song carries a meaning, every later appearance can call it back without explanation.

The common mistake: Adding songs as background with no meaning, which makes them noise.

Example: Lands: "The song her father whistled while fixing the boat. After he drowns, she cannot stand it." Fails: "A popular song from that year."

**When it plays.** Where and when the song appears in the story: on a radio, in a memory, at the wedding, as a chapter title. Useful for many books. Placement turns a song into a structural echo. The same song early and late can show how much a character has changed.

The common mistake: Playing a song once and never again, wasting its power to return.

Example: "Chapter 2 at the county fair, happy. Chapter 19 at the hospital, on someone else's radio." The second time hits because of the first.

## Acts: every field

An Act is one of the large movements of your story. Most beat sheets use three acts, with the second split in two. Acts give you the big shape: the world before, the upside-down middle, and the change at the end. Knowing which act a scene is in tells you what job it has.

**When to use it.** Acts come with your beat sheet. You rarely edit them directly.

**The common mistake.** Treating acts as fixed page counts. They are movements, and their length follows the story.

**Example.** Act One ends when the baker signs the lease on the second shop: there's no going back.

**Description.** Notes about this act. So you can see its job at a glance.

## Sequences: every field

A Sequence is a run of scenes that together make one movement, such as a chase, a courtship or a heist. Some stories think best in sequences of eight or so, each with its own rise and turn. Naming them helps pacing in long books.

**When to use it.** Use sequences if your method uses them. Otherwise you can ignore them.

**The common mistake.** Forcing every book into sequences. They help some stories and not others.

**Example.** The sabotage sequence: four scenes in which the rival florists ruin each other's weddings, each worse than the last.

**Description.** Notes about this sequence. So you can see where it starts, turns and ends.

## Kinds of beings: every field

A Kind of being record describes one non-human kind in your story: a species, a race of spirits, a breed of monster, an alien people. It covers what they are, what they can do, and how they live with others. Readers need to understand a kind of being well enough to fear it, love it, or predict it. Clear abilities and, even more, clear limits let you build suspense and fair solutions. A being that can do anything removes all danger from a scene.

**When to use it.** Create one when a non-human kind first enters your story. Fill limits before you write any scene where they face a problem.

**The common mistake.** Listing powers and skipping weaknesses. Limits create plot; powers mostly end it.

**Example.** The river folk can breathe water and outswim any boat, but they cannot cross running water downstream of a bridge. A hunted child learns to run across bridges, and suddenly every chase has a rule.

### Kinds of beings: The kind

*What this kind of being is: its nature, origin, body, lifespan, abilities and limits. These are the facts that control what they can do on the page.*

**What they are.** A plain description of this kind of being: what they look like and what they are in your world's terms. Most books need this. This is your anchor, so every description stays consistent and readers form a clear picture.

The common mistake: Relying on a familiar label ("vampires") without saying how yours differ, so readers fill in someone else's rules.

Example: Lands: "Shore-wraiths: the drowned, kept half alive by the tide. They look like the person they were, but wet and gray." Fails: "Ghosts, basically."

**Where they come from.** Where this kind came from: created, evolved, fallen, born of a curse, arrived from somewhere else. Useful for many books. Origin often explains behavior and sets up history or a secret. It can also matter to how others treat them.

The common mistake: Writing a long origin legend that never touches the plot.

Example: "They were the lighthouse keepers who failed. The sea took them and kept them." Now every keeper fears becoming one.

**Body and senses.** How their bodies work and what they perceive: what they sense that humans cannot, and what they are blind or deaf to. Useful for many books. Senses decide what they can notice and what can be hidden from them. That is the raw material of suspense and stealth.

The common mistake: Giving them perfect senses, which makes hiding from them impossible and boring.

Example: Lands: "They smell fear across a whole valley but cannot see anything that does not move." Fails: "They sense everything."

**Lifespan.** How long they live, and how they age. Useful for many books. Lifespan shapes how they see time, love and loss. A being that lives a thousand years experiences a human friendship as a brief season.

The common mistake: Forgetting the consequences, like long-lived beings who act as rushed and young as humans for no reason.

Example: "They live about three hundred years and stop counting birthdays after fifty. A human friend's death is something they expect, and it still surprises them."

**Abilities.** What they can do that others cannot. Add one entry per ability. Useful for many books. Abilities define their role in the story: threat, ally, tool. Clear abilities let readers anticipate and worry.

The common mistake: Adding a new ability whenever the plot needs rescuing. Readers feel that as a cheat.

Example: "Can hold their breath for an hour." "Can hear a lie." Both established early, so when one hears the hero lie in chapter twelve, the reader already braced for it.

**Limits and weaknesses.** What they can never do, and what weakens or harms them. Add one entry per limit. Most books need this. Limits make better stories than powers. They give heroes a way to fight back and give beings inner struggle. A solution feels earned only if readers knew the limit beforehand.

The common mistake: Revealing a weakness at the exact moment the hero needs it. Plant it early.

Example: Lands: "Cannot enter a home uninvited," shown in chapter three, used in chapter twenty. Fails: in the climax, they suddenly turn out to be allergic to copper.

### Kinds of beings: Among others

*How this kind relates to humans and to each other. These fields turn a creature into a people, with attitudes, misunderstandings and a society.*

**How they see humans.** How this kind views humans: as prey, pets, children, threats, curiosities or equals. Useful for many books. Their view of humans drives how they act in scenes with people, and gives you misunderstandings and cultural friction.

The common mistake: Making all of them think the same way, which flattens a people into a monster.

Example: "Most think humans are loud and short-lived, like birds. A few have started to envy how fiercely humans love in so little time."

**How humans see them.** How humans in your story see this kind: feared, worshiped, denied, hunted, unknown. Useful for many books. The gap between what they are and how humans see them is a source of drama and irony.

The common mistake: Making human views match the truth exactly, which wastes the chance for misunderstanding.

Example: "The villagers think they steal children. In truth they return lost ones, and get blamed for the children who never come back."

**How they live among their own.** How they live among their own: families, leaders, customs, conflicts within the kind. Only for some books. A kind with its own internal life feels real and gives you characters, not just monsters. It also creates insiders and outcasts.

The common mistake: Showing only how they treat humans, as if they have no lives when people are not looking.

Example: "They live in pairs, never alone. One who loses a partner is considered dead by the others, even though she still walks."

## Magic and powers: every field

A Magic or power record describes one system of magic, power, or extraordinary ability in your world: where it comes from, who can use it, what it costs, and what it can never do. Magic that follows rules can solve problems satisfyingly, because readers understood the rules before the solution came. Magic without rules can create wonder or dread, but it cannot fairly rescue anyone. A real cost turns every use into a choice, which is where drama lives.

**When to use it.** Create this before any scene where magic solves or causes a problem. Fill cost and limits first.

**The common mistake.** Building elaborate mechanics and forgetting cost. A free power quietly removes the stakes from every scene it touches.

**Example.** A gardener can make anything grow, but each plant she forces takes a day off her own life. She keeps a tally on the shed wall. When her daughter's garden fails, the reader knows exactly what helping would cost.

### Magic and powers: The power

*The rules of this magic or ability: source, users, cost, limits, misuse, how it shows, and who knows of it. Clear rules here let magic create tension and fair solutions.*

**Where it comes from.** Where the power comes from: a god, the land, the user's own body, a bargain, an object, a bloodline. Useful for many books. Source tells you what can threaten the magic (cut off the source) and what it means (power from a bargain feels different from power from love).

The common mistake: Leaving the source vague when the plot later depends on it being taken away.

Example: "It comes from the old wells. Wherever a well has been filled in, no one can work magic." Now a building project becomes a threat.

**Who can use it, and how they learn.** Who can use the power and how they learn it: born with it, trained, chosen, purchased. Useful for many books. This sets up who has power in society and who wants it. Training also gives you a structure: lessons, failures, mastery.

The common mistake: Letting the hero master it instantly with no learning, which makes later victories feel unearned.

Example: "Anyone can learn it, but only by apprenticing for seven years. The hero has had two." Her gaps become plot.

**What it costs the one who uses it.** What using the power takes from the person who uses it: energy, memory, health, years, relationships, sanity. Most books need this. Cost is what makes magic dramatic. If it costs something, every use is a decision, and the reader feels the weight of it.

The common mistake: Naming a cost but never making it hurt. A cost has to land on something the character loves.

Example: Lands: "Every spell erases a memory, chosen at random." She casts to save a friend and loses the day her mother died. Fails: "It makes you a bit tired."

**What it can never do.** What the power can never do. Add one entry per limit. Most books need this. A magic system's limits make better stories than its powers. Readers enjoy a magical solution only when they understood the rules before it. Limits also force heroes to find non-magical solutions.

The common mistake: Bending a limit at the climax to save the day. That breaks trust with the reader.

Example: Lands: "It cannot bring back the dead," established early, so the hero must accept a loss instead of undoing it. Fails: "It can do almost anything."

**What happens when it is misused.** What happens when the power is used wrongly, too much, or for the wrong reasons. Useful for many books. Misuse gives you villains, warnings and temptations. It shows the danger of power before your hero feels tempted.

The common mistake: Having no consequence for misuse, so the villain's choices cost nothing.

Example: "Those who use it to harm slowly turn to salt from the fingertips up." The old woman at the market wears gloves in summer.

**What it looks, sounds or feels like.** What the power looks, sounds, smells or feels like when it is used. Useful for many books. Signs let you show magic instead of announcing it, and let characters detect when someone else is using it.

The common mistake: Using only light shows. Smell, temperature or a sound can be more unsettling and memorable.

Example: Lands: "The air tastes like pennies, and every clock in the room stops." Fails: "Blue light comes out of her hands."

**Who knows it exists.** Who knows this power exists: everyone, a secret few, no one but the user. Useful for many books. Secrecy shapes how characters hide, reveal and fear. A known power creates rules and law. A secret one creates paranoia and discovery.

The common mistake: Forgetting who knows, so characters react to magic in ways that do not fit their knowledge.

Example: "Only the three keepers of the archive know. Everyone else thinks the fires are bad wiring."

## Cultures and peoples: every field

A Culture record describes one people or community in your story: their customs, values, taboos, food, dress, celebrations and way of speaking. It can be an invented people or a real community you are portraying. Culture explains why characters act the way they do. Two characters from different cultures can disagree without either being wrong, and that kind of conflict is rich. Specific customs make a world feel lived in rather than painted.

**When to use it.** Create one when a group's ways start to shape characters' choices. Fill values early, since everything else flows from them.

**The common mistake.** Building a culture from surface details (clothes, food) with no values underneath, or making a whole people think alike. Real cultures argue with themselves.

**Example.** In one coastal town, refusing a second cup of tea is an insult. A newcomer baker refuses, and spends half the book wondering why no one will buy her bread.

### Cultures and peoples: The people

*The customs, values and daily textures of this culture. Values come first, because customs, taboos and celebrations usually grow from what a people care about most.*

**Customs.** The everyday habits and expected behaviors of this people: greetings, manners, how guests are treated. Add one entry per custom. Useful for many books. Customs give texture and create easy friction for outsiders. Breaking one can start a scene's conflict without a single villain.

The common mistake: Inventing customs that have no reason behind them. Tie each to a value or history.

Example: "You never hand someone a knife; you set it on the table." Rooted in an old feud where a handed knife was a challenge.

**What they value most.** What this people care about most: honor, family, thrift, hospitality, independence, faith. Add one entry per value. Most books need this. Values drive choices. When a character's personal want clashes with what their culture values, you have inner conflict.

The common mistake: Listing virtues everyone admires. Real values cost something and sometimes conflict with each other.

Example: Lands: "Family first, even before truth." Now a daughter must choose between protecting her brother and telling the police. Fails: "They value kindness."

**Taboos.** Things this people forbid or fear to do or speak of. Add one entry per taboo. Useful for many books. Taboos create tension immediately: a character who must break one faces shame, punishment or exile.

The common mistake: Creating a taboo and never letting anyone come near it. A taboo matters when the story pushes someone toward it.

Example: "No one speaks the name of the drowned." The heroine needs to ask about her mother, who drowned.

**Food and drink.** What they eat and drink, and what food means to them: comfort, status, ritual, survival. Useful for many books. Food is one of the fastest ways to make a culture feel real, and meals are natural settings for conflict and closeness.

The common mistake: Describing food only as decoration. Show who cooks, who eats first, what is offered to guests.

Example: "The eldest woman serves the bread and decides who gets the heel. Getting the heel means you are favored."

**Dress.** How they dress, and what clothing signals: rank, marriage, mourning, belonging. Only for some books. Dress lets readers recognize groups at a glance and lets characters signal or hide who they are.

The common mistake: Long costume descriptions with no meaning. One meaningful detail beats a catalog.

Example: "Widows wear a blue thread at the wrist for one year. When she cuts hers early, the whole village notices."

**Celebrations and rites.** Holidays, festivals and rites of passage: births, coming of age, weddings, funerals. Add one entry per celebration. Useful for many books. Celebrations gather characters in one place with high emotion, which makes them natural set pieces for confrontations and reveals.

The common mistake: Describing a festival as a tour. Put a character's want or fear at its center.

Example: "The harvest dance, where you must dance with anyone who asks." The heroine's rival asks.

**How they treat outsiders.** How this people treat strangers and newcomers: welcome, suspicion, testing, exclusion. Useful for many books. If your hero or a viewpoint character is an outsider, this field tells you how hard every door will be to open.

The common mistake: Making a culture hostile to outsiders with no reason. Show the history behind it.

Example: "Strangers are fed well and watched closely. Last time they trusted one, he left with the church silver."

**Their language and way of speaking.** Their language and way of speaking: dialect, expressions, formality, what is said and what is left unsaid. Useful for many books. Speech patterns let readers hear who is from where. They make dialogue distinct without tags.

The common mistake: Writing heavy phonetic dialect that is hard to read. A few chosen words and rhythms work better.

Example: Lands: "They never say no directly. They say 'we'll see.'" Fails: whole pages of misspelled words meant to show an accent.

## Factions and institutions: every field

A Faction record describes one organized group in your story: a gang, a church, a company, a guild, a family business, a government office. It tracks what the group wants and how it gets it. Groups create pressure that no single villain can. A faction keeps acting when its leader is offstage, has resources and rules, and can split or betray. Knowing what a faction wants tells you how it will react to anything your hero does.

**When to use it.** Create one when a group starts to push on your characters. Fill what they want and their methods before writing a scene where they act.

**The common mistake.** Making a faction purely evil or purely a backdrop. A strong faction believes it is right and has a goal that makes sense from inside.

**Example.** The town's garden club wants to keep Main Street pretty for tourists. That reasonable goal makes them the enemy of a florist who sells from a van, and they have the mayor's ear.

### Factions and institutions: The group

*What the group wants, who leads it, what it controls, where it sits in the web of power, how it acts, and what it hides. These fields make a group a force with a will.*

**What they want.** What the group is trying to achieve: its goal as a whole. Most books need this. Want drives action. Once you know what a faction wants, you can predict how it will help or block every character.

The common mistake: Vague wants like "power." Name what power would get them, specifically.

Example: Lands: "To buy every storefront on the block before the new highway exit opens." Fails: "To rule the town."

**Who leads them.** Who leads the group, and how they hold that position. Useful for many books. A leader gives the group a face, and the way they lead (fear, charm, tradition) tells you how loyal members are.

The common mistake: Making the leader the whole faction. Groups also have rivals within, doubters and successors.

Example: "Mrs. Ferrante has run the garden club for thirty years. No one has dared to run against her since 1998."

**What they control.** What resources, places or powers the group controls: money, land, information, law, supply. Useful for many books. Control is pressure waiting to be used. It tells you what the faction can withhold or grant, which is how it pressures characters.

The common mistake: Listing control that never comes into play. Use it to squeeze someone.

Example: "They own the only cold-storage warehouse within fifty miles." No warehouse, no flowers.

**Who they hold power over, and who holds power over them.** Who this group holds power over, and who holds power over them. Useful for many books. No faction is at the top of everything. Knowing who can pressure them gives your hero a way to fight back.

The common mistake: Making a faction all-powerful, which leaves your hero no path.

Example: "They control the market permits, but the county health inspector can shut them down." The hero now knows whom to call.

**How they get what they want.** How the group gets what it wants: bribery, law, violence, rumor, charity, patience. Useful for many books. Methods determine the kind of scenes the faction produces. A group that uses lawsuits gives you different scenes from one that uses fire.

The common mistake: Giving the group methods that do not fit its character or resources.

Example: Lands: "They never threaten. They file complaints, politely, every week." Fails: a polite garden club that suddenly hires hit men.

**What they hide.** What the group hides from outsiders, or from its own members. Add one entry per secret. Useful for many books. Secrets give your hero something to discover and use. They also make the faction more human and vulnerable.

The common mistake: Revealing secrets with no setup. Plant a hint before the reveal.

Example: "The club's treasurer has been quietly paying off the leader's gambling debts from club funds."

## Beliefs and faiths: every field

A Belief record describes one religion, faith, philosophy or spiritual tradition in your story: what believers hold true, how they practice it, who speaks for it, and how it shapes ordinary life. Belief shapes how characters judge right and wrong, what they fear and what comforts them. A faith gives you ritual scenes, inner conflict when doubt creeps in, and outer conflict between believers. It is one of the deepest motives a character can have.

**When to use it.** Create one when a faith influences characters' choices or the world's laws. Fill core beliefs and daily life first.

**The common mistake.** Writing a faith as costume (robes, chants) without convictions underneath, or treating every believer as identical. Real believers range from devout to doubting.

**Example.** A lighthouse keeper's people believe the sea keeps the souls of the drowned. When a survivor washes up, some see a miracle and others see a theft from the sea.

### Beliefs and faiths: The faith

*What believers hold true and how it touches their lives: the powers they honor, their rituals, their leaders, their doubters and their daily habits.*

**Gods, spirits or powers.** The gods, spirits, ancestors or powers this faith honors or fears. For a philosophy, the principles in their place. Useful for many books. Who believers pray to, or fear, shapes the tone of their faith: loving, stern, distant, bargaining.

The common mistake: Building a large pantheon readers cannot keep track of. Focus on the few that touch your story.

Example: "One god of the harbor, who is fair but never forgives. Fishermen apologize to him before they lie."

**What believers hold true.** What believers hold to be true: about life, death, right, wrong, and how the world works. Most books need this. Core beliefs set what a believing character will die for and what would shake them. That is where inner conflict comes from.

The common mistake: Stating beliefs that never touch a decision anyone makes.

Example: Lands: "A promise made at sea binds you after death." A sailor's deathbed promise now traps his son. Fails: "They believe in goodness."

**Rituals.** The practices believers perform: prayers, offerings, feasts, fasts, rites. Add one entry per ritual. Useful for many books. Rituals are visible and dramatic. They gather people, mark change, and give characters something to keep or break.

The common mistake: Describing rituals with no meaning to the people doing them.

Example: "Before a voyage, you pour a cup of fresh water into the sea." The day she refuses, her crew will not sail.

**Who speaks for it.** Who has authority to interpret the faith: priests, elders, a book, a council, every believer alone. Useful for many books. Authority means power. Whoever speaks for the faith can bless, condemn or exclude, which makes them a force in your plot.

The common mistake: Ignoring that leaders can be wrong, corrupt or divided.

Example: "The oldest fisherman's widow reads the tides for everyone. When she dies, two families claim her role."

**Heresies and doubts.** Beliefs the faith rejects, and doubts its believers carry quietly. Add one entry per heresy or doubt. A heresy is a belief the faith's authorities call false. Useful for many books. Heresies and doubts are where faith becomes drama. A believing character who starts to doubt has an arc built in.

The common mistake: Making the heretics automatically right. Let both sides have reasons.

Example: "Some whisper that the drowned are not kept by the sea at all, that they are simply gone." The heroine half believes it after her brother drowns.

**How it shapes daily life.** How the faith shapes ordinary days: food, work, dress, speech, what people avoid, what they say at meals. Useful for many books. Daily habits show faith without sermons. They make the belief part of the world rather than a lecture.

The common mistake: Showing faith only in temple scenes, as if it vanished the rest of the week.

Example: "No one whistles on a boat. Children learn it before they learn to swim."

## Languages: every field

A Language record describes one language or dialect in your story, real or invented: how it sounds, how names are formed, key words and their meanings, and who speaks it. A consistent language makes names feel like they belong to one people, and a few chosen words add texture without slowing the reader. Knowing who speaks it and how well creates moments of misunderstanding, secrecy and belonging.

**When to use it.** Create one when you start naming people and places from a culture, so names stay consistent. Add words as you invent them.

**The common mistake.** Loading pages with invented words the reader must decode. Use few, and make each one clear from context.

**Example.** A family switches to their grandmother's language whenever they argue, so the new son-in-law knows a fight is happening without knowing what it is about. Three words are all the reader needs.

### Languages: The language

*The sound, naming patterns, vocabulary and speakers of this language. These keep invented names and words consistent across the whole book.*

**How it sounds.** How the language sounds: soft or harsh, which letters and sounds it favors, rhythm, and how it looks on the page. Useful for many books. A consistent sound makes names from one culture feel related, and different from another culture's. Readers sense that even if they cannot say why.

The common mistake: Mixing sounds randomly so names from the same people seem to come from different worlds.

Example: Lands: "Lots of l, m and long vowels; no hard k sounds." Names: Lumia, Elomar, Seyla. Fails: Lumia, Krax and Zyth as siblings.

**Naming patterns.** How people and places are named: family names, given names, titles, prefixes, what a name says about status or birth. Useful for many books. Naming patterns make naming easy for you and meaningful for readers. A name can carry family, place or rank.

The common mistake: Inventing every name from scratch with no pattern.

Example: "Daughters take their mother's name with -ra added. Seyla's daughter is Seylara." Readers start reading family ties in the names themselves.

**Words and their meanings.** Words in this language and what they mean. Add one entry per word: the word, then its meaning. Useful for many books. A short list keeps spellings and meanings consistent across drafts. A few words, used well, add more flavor than many.

The common mistake: Using invented words where a plain English word would do, or never defining them through context.

Example: Lands: "maren: the hush just before a storm." Used once in a scene where everyone stops talking. Fails: a sentence with four invented words in a row.

**Who speaks it, and how well.** Who speaks the language, and how fluently: natives, scholars, children of immigrants, no one living. Only for some books. Fluency differences create secrets, power and humor. Who understands and who is left out can drive a scene.

The common mistake: Letting every character understand everything when the plot is easier that way.

Example: "The grandmother is fluent; her son understands but will not speak it; her granddaughter knows only the swear words."

## Sources and retellings: every field

A Source record holds the original story your book retells or draws from: a myth, a fairy tale, a legend, a classic novel, a folk tradition. It tracks what the original says and how your version differs. A retelling succeeds by its changes. Readers who know the original enjoy watching you keep, bend and break it. Writing down exactly what you keep and change keeps your version purposeful. It also helps you check whether the source is free to use and how to treat a living tradition with respect.

**When to use it.** Create this at the start of any retelling, before plotting. Return to it whenever you are tempted to follow the original out of habit.

**The common mistake.** Retelling scene by scene with new names. If nothing meaningful changes, readers wonder why they should not read the original.

**Example.** A retelling of a selkie legend keeps the stolen sealskin but changes who holds it: the wife hides it herself, so the story becomes about why she chose to stay.

### Sources and retellings: The original

*The facts about the source: where it comes from, what happens in it, and whether it is free to use. Accuracy here protects you legally and creatively.*

**Where it comes from.** Where the source comes from: the myth, culture, tradition or author. Most books need this. Knowing the origin tells you its history, its versions and the people it belongs to. Many old stories have many versions, so say which one you are using.

The common mistake: Treating one popular version as the only one, when older or regional versions differ a lot.

Example: "A Scottish selkie legend, in the version where the husband hides the skin in the rafters."

**What happens in the original.** A summary of what happens in the original, in your own words. Most books need this. This is your reference point. Every change you make is measured against it, and you need it clear to know what readers will expect.

The common mistake: Working from a vague memory of the original and then being caught by readers who know it well.

Example: Write the main turns in a few plain sentences: who wants what, what goes wrong, how it ends. Enough to compare against your plot.

**Is the original in the public domain?.** Whether the original is free for anyone to use. Public domain means a work's copyright has expired or never existed, so it can be retold freely. Options: Yes, No, Not sure. Most books need this. Retelling a copyrighted work can cause legal trouble. Myths and very old works are usually free, but a particular modern translation or film version may not be.

The common mistake: Assuming a story is free because it feels old, or borrowing details that only appear in a modern copyrighted version.

Example: An old fairy tale is free to retell. A recent film's added sidekick and new ending are not.

### Sources and retellings: Your version

*Your choices as the reteller: what you keep, what you change and why, and how you honor where the story came from.*

**What you keep.** The elements of the original you keep. Add one entry per element: a character, an object, a turning point, an image. Useful for many books. What you keep is what lets readers recognize the source. Kept elements become promises: readers expect them to matter.

The common mistake: Keeping so much that your version feels like a copy, or so little that no one sees the connection.

Example: "The sealskin. The seven years. The children who find it." Three anchors, and everything else can change.

**What you change, and why.** What you change, and why. Add one entry per change, with its reason. Most books need this. Changes are where your book's meaning lives. Writing down the why keeps each change doing work for your theme rather than being random.

The common mistake: Changing things for novelty with no reason. Readers feel arbitrary changes.

Example: Lands: "She hides the skin herself, because I want the story to be about choosing a life, not being trapped in one." Fails: "Moved it to Ohio."

**How you honor the source.** How you show respect to the source, especially a living tradition or a culture not your own. A living tradition is one people still practice and hold sacred today. Only for some books. Retelling someone else's sacred or cultural story carries responsibility. Getting it wrong can hurt real communities and lose readers' trust.

The common mistake: Treating a living faith or culture's stories as free decoration, or skipping research and readers from that culture.

Example: "I read three versions collected by members of the community, and two readers from that tradition will review the draft."

## In-world documents: every field

An In-world document record holds one piece of writing that exists inside your story: a letter, diary, newspaper clipping, will, text message, report or map note. It may be shown to the reader or only described. Documents let you bring in another voice, another time, or a piece of evidence without a viewpoint character explaining it. They can deliver clues, contradict a narrator, or reveal what a character could never say aloud. Tracking who wrote them and whether they can be trusted keeps clues fair.

**When to use it.** Create one when you plan a document as a clue, a reveal or a structural device (like chapter-opening letters).

**The common mistake.** Using a document as a convenient info dump. A good document has a writer with a motive and a voice.

**Example.** A baker finds her late mother's recipe card with one ingredient crossed out and another written in a stranger's hand. One card raises the question that drives the whole book.

### In-world documents: The document

*The facts about this piece of in-world writing: its form, author, date, contents, reliability, and where it appears in your book.*

**What kind it is.** What kind of document it is: letter, diary, will, email, police report, newspaper article, sign. Useful for many books. Form shapes voice and credibility. A police report sounds cold and official; a diary is private and may lie to itself.

The common mistake: Choosing a form that does not fit what it says, like a formal report full of private feelings.

Example: "A grocery list on the back of a funeral program." The form itself tells a story.

**Who wrote it.** Who wrote the document, real or claimed. Most books need this. The writer's motive colors everything it says. Knowing the author tells you what they would include, leave out, or distort.

The common mistake: Forgetting the author's voice, so every document sounds like the narrator.

Example: "Written by the lighthouse keeper's predecessor, who was proud and hated admitting mistakes." His log will skip his failures.

**When it was written.** When the document was written, in story time. Useful for many books. Date places the document on your timeline and decides what its writer could know. A letter written before a death cannot mention it.

The common mistake: Including information the writer could not have had at that date.

Example: "Three days before the shipwreck." So when it mentions the storm, the reader realizes the writer saw it coming.

**What it reveals.** What the document tells the reader or characters: the information, clue or emotion it delivers. Most books need this. This is the document's job in the story. Without a clear purpose, a document becomes filler.

The common mistake: Revealing everything at once, which kills mystery.

Example: Lands: "It reveals that the bakery was promised to someone else first." Fails: "Background on the family."

**Can it be trusted?.** Whether the document tells the truth: accurate, mistaken, partial, or a deliberate lie. Useful for many books. An unreliable document can mislead readers fairly and set up a twist. Tracking it keeps you from contradicting yourself later.

The common mistake: Forgetting a document was a lie and later treating its contents as true.

Example: "Partly reliable: the dates are real, but he lies about who was with him." The reader can catch the lie with an earlier clue.

**Where it appears in the book.** Where the document appears in the book: which chapter, scene or beat, and whether it is shown in full or summarized. Useful for many books. Placement controls when the reader learns. The same letter can be a setup in chapter two or a twist in chapter thirty.

The common mistake: Placing documents wherever there is room rather than where the information does the most work.

Example: "Opens Part Three, right after the midpoint, so readers hold it in mind while the hero walks into the trap."

## World history: every field

An Era record holds one period of your world's history: a war, a golden age, a plague year, the founding of a town, the night everything changed. Together, eras form your world's timeline. History is why the present is the way it is. Old wars explain current grudges; a lost golden age explains a people's pride or grief. When you know your world's past, characters carry it in their habits, fears and loyalties, and the world feels older than the first page.

**When to use it.** Create eras when a character's attitude or a place's condition needs an explanation, or when backstory affects your plot.

**The common mistake.** Writing long history the story never uses, or opening the book with a history lesson. Let the past show up through what it left behind.

**Example.** Fifty years ago, the bridge collapsed with a school bus on it. Today the town has no bridge, a ferry, and a memorial no one looks at. Every character's route to work carries that day.

### World history: The era

*When the period was, what happened, what it left behind, and who remembers it. These fields connect your world's past to the present of your story.*

**When.** When this era happened: years, an age, or a relative marker like "before the Breaking." Useful for many books. Span places the era on your timeline and tells you how many generations have passed. That decides whether people remember it firsthand or as legend.

The common mistake: Leaving dates so vague that characters' ages and memories contradict them.

Example: "Forty years before the story opens." So the town's oldest residents lived through it, and the younger ones have only heard.

**What happened.** A summary of the era's main events. Most books need this. This is your reference for history, keeping every character's version comparable to the truth.

The common mistake: Writing more history than the story will ever touch. Focus on what still matters.

Example: "A fever swept the valley; one family's well stayed clean, and they refused to share it." That one fact can carry a feud for decades.

**What it left that the story still feels.** What the era left behind that your story still feels: grudges, ruins, laws, traditions, fears, wealth. Most books need this. Legacy is the reason history belongs in the book. It turns the past into pressure on the present.

The common mistake: Inventing an era with no legacy. If nothing remains of it, the reader does not need it.

Example: Lands: "Nobody from the Hill marries anyone from the Flats, and no one under sixty can say why." Fails: "It was a sad time."

**Who remembers it, and how.** Who remembers the era, and how: firsthand, through family stories, in official records, as a legend, or not at all. Add one entry per group or person. Useful for many books. Different memories of the same events create conflict and mystery. The official version and the family version rarely match.

The common mistake: Having everyone remember the past the same way. Memory bends toward each group's interest.

Example: "The well family remembers protecting their children. The other families remember being turned away. The town history says nothing."
