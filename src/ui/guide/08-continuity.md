# Catching problems: the continuity checks

This chapter covers the second row of tabs in Narrative Zoom, the **Continuity checks**. Each check lays your whole story out in one way (by time, by viewpoint, by secret, by clue, by character and so on) so that slips show up while they are still easy to fix. Read it once you have a few scene cards or written scenes, or whenever you start revising. You can skip any check you do not need; none of them is required, and none of them changes your story.

## What continuity means, and how the checks talk to you

**Continuity** means a story stays consistent with itself. A character is not in two places on the same morning. A secret is not revealed before anyone could know it. A clue that matters in chapter 30 was actually planted somewhere earlier. A name is spelled the same way on page 12 and page 312.

Slips like these hide in the gaps between chapters you wrote weeks apart. You cannot hold a whole novel in your head at once, and you should not have to. The checks hold it for you.

### Continuity checks: questions, never errors

Story Canon's motto is "Story Canon remembers. The author decides." The checks follow it. They never say something is wrong. They ask.

That is not politeness. A program cannot know your intentions. A flashback *is* an effect shown before its cause. An unreliable narrator *is* supposed to contradict the facts. A character who vanishes for ten chapters might be the twist. So every check ends with "Intended?" or a question like it, and you answer.

Three things to know before you open any check:

- **Nothing a check says changes your story.** The checks only read.
- **A check only knows what you have told it.** If you never recorded that a scene reveals a secret, no check can see the reveal. An empty check usually means "nothing recorded yet", not "nothing wrong".
- **Your answers are kept.** When you answer a question in the **Story review** (the last check), it stays quiet for good, and your reason is saved.

**Do most writers need the checks?** Not on day one. Most writers start using them once the plan has a shape, and lean on them hardest in revision. Mysteries, books with two timelines, and books with several narrators get the most from them.

**The common mistake** is reading a question as a verdict and "fixing" something you did on purpose. If it is intentional, say so, write down why, and move on.

### Continuity checks: the words this chapter uses

A few Story Canon words come up again and again in the checks. Here they are, in plain terms.

- **Narrative Zoom** is the view where you see your story's structure: the whole book, each act, each beat, each scene card. It is taught in [Narrative Zoom](#narrative-zoom). The continuity checks live inside it.
- **The Writing Room** is the view where you write your pages. A **written scene** is a scene that exists there, with words on the page (or ready for them).
- **A planned scene card** is a scene card that has no written scene in the Writing Room yet. It is a plan for a scene.
- **The beat sheet** is the list of beats your novel follows, such as Save the Cat's Setup, Catalyst, Midpoint and All Is Lost. You chose it in Novel setup.
- **Placing a card on a beat** means saying which beat a scene card belongs to. You can place it as decided ("It goes here") or as a maybe ("It might go here"). Narrative Zoom explains both.
- **The story world** is one special record for the whole book. It holds the book's viewpoint, tense, viewpoint rule and other book-wide facts. Each novel has exactly one.
- **Story time** is when something happens inside your story, as opposed to where it falls in the book.

### Continuity checks: making a connection

Many checks read **connections**. A connection is a link you draw between two records, and it reads as a short sentence: "The empty boat **reveals** Cyrus's secret", "Ana Ruiz **appears in** The clinic at night". Fields hold facts about one record; connections hold how two records relate. The checks are built mostly from connections, so this is the one skill every check needs.

**How to make a connection:**

1. Open the record the sentence starts from (for a scene, open its scene card).
2. Turn to its **Notes and connections** page, the last one. (On a character, connections are on the **Their world and their people** page instead.)
3. Under **Connections**, find **Make a connection**. Your record's name is already written at the start of the line.
4. In the first dropdown, choose the connection word, such as **reveals**, **appears in** or **plants**. They are grouped by purpose: Cause and effect, Setups, clues and secrets, Who knows what, Where and who, and so on.
5. In the second dropdown (**Choose…**), choose the other record.
6. Read the line under it, which starts **Reads:**, to check the sentence says what you mean.
7. Click **Connect**.

The connection now shows on both records, read the right way round on each. Click **Remove** beside a connection to take it away.

**The common mistake** is drawing the connection from the wrong end. "The logbook **reveals** the secret" is right; "The secret **reveals** the logbook" means nothing to the checks. Each dropdown offers both directions (for example **reveals** and **is revealed in**), so you can start from either record as long as the **Reads:** line makes sense.

## Where the continuity checks are

**Where:** open **Narrative Zoom** from the list on the right. Under the first row of tabs (Zoom, Board, Where beats fall, Alternate structures) is a second row labeled **Continuity checks**. Click any name in that row. The one you are on turns into a filled pill.

The checks, in the order they appear:

| Check | What it shows you |
| --- | --- |
| **Timelines** | The order the reader meets scenes, and the order things happen |
| **Viewpoint** | Whose eyes each scene is seen through, in what person and tense |
| **Who knows what** | Each secret: the truth, when the reader meets it, when each character learns it |
| **Setups and payoffs** | Every clue: where it is planted, what it means, where it pays off |
| **Storylines** | Which plot threads each scene moves forward |
| **Cast** | Which characters are in which scenes, who meets whom, and the Bechdel test |
| **Pacing** | Every written scene as a bar, by length |
| **Reader response** | The feeling each chapter leaves, as colored tabs |
| **Soundtrack** | The songs in your story, in the order they play |
| **Names** | Every name on the page, counted, and possible misspellings |
| **Story review** | Every question the program can ask right now, in one place, for you to answer |

### How this view works: the fold-out on every check

Under each check's title is a folded line, **How this view works**, with a small triangle. Click it to open three short answers:

- **What it shows**
- **Where it comes from**: which fields and connections feed it
- **What to do with it**

Once you have opened it, Story Canon remembers that you have read it (on this computer), and it stays folded quietly from then on. Click it again any time.

### Continuity checks with pages

Four checks hold more than fits comfortably on one screen, so they are split into numbered pages, the same way a record is: **Timelines**, **Who knows what**, **Setups and payoffs** (when you have motifs) and **Cast**. Under the check's opening lines is a row of page tabs, each with a number and a name, and **All pages** at the end of the row.

- **Click a tab** to turn to that page. One page shows at a time.
- **A page number turns ink blue** when that page has something on it.
- **At the bottom of each page**, links name the page before and the page after. Click one to turn to it.
- **All pages** shows every page one after another, for reading the whole check straight through.
- **Story Canon remembers** which page you were on in each check while the program is open, so a check opens where you left it.

Each check's section below names its pages.

### Planned cards in the checks

Most checks show your whole plan, not only what you have written. A planned scene card appears beside written scenes with a small tag:

| Tag | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| (no tag) | A written scene in the Writing Room |
| **planned** | A scene card decided on a beat, not written yet |
| **planned, might go here** | A card that might go on a beat, not decided or written yet |
| **planned, no beat yet** | A card with no beat at all |

Written scenes and cards on a beat are numbered in reading order. A card with no beat has no place in the order yet, so it has no number and is listed at the end.

**Why "might" cards appear but never raise questions.** The views show "might go here" cards so you can see your whole plan. The *questions* (in the Move dialog, the window that shows what moving a card on the Board would change before anything moves, and in the Story review) count only decided cards and written scenes, so a guess never raises a problem. See [Narrative Zoom](#narrative-zoom) for placing cards.

Four checks work only from written pages: **Viewpoint**, **Pacing**, **Reader response** and **Names**. They show only scenes that exist in the Writing Room, and each one says so.

## Timelines: reading order and story order

**Where:** **Continuity checks** > **Timelines**. The page is titled **Two timelines**.

**What it shows.** Every scene and scene card twice, each on its own numbered page, with page tabs like a record's:

- **1 In the order of the book**: the order the reader meets them.
- **2 In the order things happen**: the order the events take place in your story world.

Click a tab to turn to that page, or use the link at the bottom of a page, which names the other page. **All pages**, at the end of the row, shows both tables one after the other, which is the easiest way to compare them. The number of **In the order things happen** turns ink blue once at least one scene has a year, a day or a date. While the program stays open, Story Canon remembers which page you were on.

**Why it matters.** The two orders can differ on purpose: a flashback, a prologue from years later, a book told in two time periods. Seeing them side by side shows whether the difference is the one you meant. It is also where a week that has quietly become three days shows itself.

**Do you need it?** Any book where time matters does: a ticking clock, a long span of years, flashbacks, two timelines. A book told straight through over one weekend may never need it.

### Timelines: the columns

Both pages have the same columns.


| Column | What it holds | Where it comes from |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Scene** | Its number in reading order | The Board and the Writing Room |
| **Title** | The scene or card, with its planned tag | Click to open the card |
| **Beat** | The beat it sits on, or "no beat" | Its placement |
| **When** | Your words for when it happens | **When (story time)** on the card's page **The scene**, or beside the page in the Writing Room |
| **Year, day or date** | The sortable time | **Year**, **Day in the story** or **Date**, on the card's page **When it happens** |
| **Time** | Time of day | **Time of day**, on the card's **When it happens** page |
| **Where** | The place | **Where**, on the card's **The scene** page |
| **Season, weather** | The season, or the weather | **Season** or **Weather**, on the card's **Mood and feeling** page |

"not set" means the field is empty.

Under a title you may see small gray lines, **Calls up:** and **Called up by:**. They come from the **calls up** connection, for a scene that brings an earlier one back to mind: a memory, an echo, a callback.

### Getting scenes into story order

The second page, **In the order things happen**, only includes scenes with a **Year**, a **Day in the story** or a **Date**. Until one has, the page says "No scene has a year, day or date yet." Words like "the next morning" show in the **When** column but cannot be sorted. Below the table, a line counts the scenes that have no year, day or date yet.

1. In **Timelines**, click a scene's title to open its card.
2. Turn to its **When it happens** page.
3. Beside **Day in the story**, click **Add**, type a number on your own story clock (such as 3), and click **Save**. Or fill in **Date** or **Year** the same way.
4. Come back to **Continuity checks** > **Timelines** and turn to the page **In the order things happen**. The scene is now in that table.

Scenes on the same day are sorted by **Time of day** when it uses words like dawn, morning, noon, afternoon, evening or night.

**What to look for.** A scene out of story order that you did not mean as a flashback. Two scenes on the same day that cannot both fit. A long gap with nothing in **Time since the previous scene** (also on the card's page **When it happens**).

**The common mistake** is filling in only **When (story time)** in words and wondering why the page **In the order things happen** is empty. Words are for you; numbers are for sorting. Use both if you like.

**Example.** In *The Keeper of Gull Point*, the empty boat washes onto the rocks on day 1, and the book covers eleven days. One scene is a memory of the night Eli drowned, twelve years earlier. Every card gets a **Day in the story**; the memory scene gets day 0 and the **Year** 2012. In the order of the book, the memory sits in chapter 9. In the order things happen, it jumps to the top. If a card for day 6 was accidentally typed as day 16, it drops to the bottom of that page, where you can see it.

## Viewpoint: whose eyes, and can we trust them

**Where:** **Continuity checks** > **Viewpoint**.

**Some terms, first:**

- **Viewpoint** (or point of view): the character whose eyes and thoughts a scene is told through.
- **Person:** first person ("I opened the door"), third person ("she opened the door"), or second ("you open the door").
- **Tense:** past ("she opened") or present ("she opens").
- **Reliability:** how far the reader can trust the teller. **Reliable** means they tell it straight. **Unreliable** means they may mislead, on purpose or not. **Selectively informed** means honest, but missing pieces.
- **Second viewpoint:** another character whose thoughts we also hear in the same scene. Switching heads in the middle of a scene is often called **head-hopping**.

**What it shows.** Each chapter of the Writing Room, and in each one, every written scene: whose eyes we see it through, in what person and tense, how far the reader can trust the teller, and whether we also hear a second character's thoughts.

**Why it matters.** Viewpoint slips are among the hardest to see in your own pages, because you know everything and your reader does not. A scene that dips into the wrong head can spoil a secret or blur two characters together.

**Do you need it?** If your book has one narrator from start to finish, you can set the viewpoint once and rarely look again. If it has two or more viewpoint characters, use it.

**Where it comes from.** Three places, in this order:

1. A scene's own viewpoint wins. It is the same setting as **Viewpoint character** beside the page in the Writing Room.
2. A scene with no viewpoint of its own takes its chapter's.
3. Person and tense fall back to the book's, from the story world or from what you chose in Novel setup.

### What's on the Viewpoint page

- **Book defaults:** one line with the book's person and tense, such as "Book defaults: Third person limited; past tense."
- **Story world and viewpoint rules:** a button that opens your book's story world record right here, so you can set the book's person, tense and **Viewpoint rule**. They are on the story world's **Time, viewpoint and knowledge** page. Click **Close** under it when you are done.
- **Gaps in a character's viewpoint**, as quiet notes, such as "Cyrus Hale: no viewpoint scene from Chapter 4 to Chapter 9 (6 chapters). An observation, not a rule."
- **One section per chapter**, with a **Viewpoint** dropdown at the right for the chapter as a whole ("None set" until you choose).
- **A table of the chapter's scenes:**
  - **Scene:** click a title to open it in the Writing Room.
  - **Viewpoint:** the scene's own viewpoint. If it follows the chapter, the dropdown says so, such as "Marisol Vega (from chapter)".
  - **Person** and **Tense:** type to override the book's. A gray "Same as book" means it follows the book. Your change saves when you click away.
  - **Reliability:** **Not set**, **Reliable**, **Unreliable** or **Selectively informed**.
  - **Second viewpoint:** **None**, or a character.

If the Writing Room has no chapters yet, the page says so. Planned cards do not appear here, because viewpoint is about written scenes.

### Setting viewpoint the quick way

1. Pick the chapter's viewpoint in the **Viewpoint** dropdown at the right of the chapter's heading. Every scene in that chapter follows it.
2. Change a single scene only where it differs.

### Viewpoint: the questions it can raise

These appear in the Story review:

| Question | Where it comes from | What to do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A scene lists more than one viewpoint | Your story world's **Viewpoint rule** says one per scene, and a scene has a **Second viewpoint** | Cut the second head, or answer **It's intentional** |
| A chapter has more than one viewpoint | Your rule says one per chapter | Split the chapter, or answer **It's intentional** |
| A scene is in a secret keeper's head too early | A character's **Viewpoint withheld until** (on the character's **Who they are** page) names a beat, and a scene placed before that beat is in their viewpoint | Move the scene later, or change its viewpoint |
| A character has no viewpoint scene for a stretch | Simple counting, three chapters or more | Only an observation. Decide whether the absence serves the book |

**The common mistake** is setting the viewpoint scene by scene in a book where every chapter belongs to one character. Set it once on the chapter.

**Example.** Most of *The Keeper of Gull Point* is in Marisol's viewpoint, but a few chapters belong to Cyrus Hale, the harbor master who knows more than he says. Each chapter's **Viewpoint** is set once. The story world's **Viewpoint rule** says "One viewpoint per chapter", and Cyrus's **Viewpoint withheld until** says Break into Three, because the reader must not hear his thoughts about Eli's drowning too soon. When a Cyrus scene placed at the Midpoint slips into his head, the Story review asks about it.

## Who knows what: secrets, reveals and the reader

**Where:** **Continuity checks** > **Who knows what**. The page is titled **Who knows what, and when**.

**What it shows.** Three things, each on its own numbered page:

1. **Secrets and who learns them**: each secret or fact that a scene reveals, in its own section. Until a scene reveals something, it says "Nothing is revealed in any scene yet."
2. **The reader's view, scene by scene**: a table.
3. **Secret operations (author only)**: a log of what a schemer does behind everyone's back, if you keep one, and the form for adding to it.

Turn pages with the tabs, or with the links at the bottom of each page; **All pages** shows all three (see [Continuity checks with pages](#continuity-checks-with-pages)).

**Why it matters.** In a mystery, a thriller or any book with a secret, the order in which people learn things *is* the plot. A character who acts on a truth before learning it, or a reveal that lands before the question is even raised, breaks the reader's trust.

**Do you need it?** If your book has a secret, yes. If nobody in it hides anything, you can skip it.

### Who knows what: each secret, and who learns it

On the page **Secrets and who learns them**, for each secret (a record, usually an event or a reveal, that some scene **reveals**), you see:

- **The truth:** what really happened, from the record's **What actually happened** field (on an event's **When, and who knows** page).
- **When it happens:** the event's own timing, from **When it happens in the story**.
- **When the reader meets it:** the first scene or card that reveals it, such as "scene 31, 'The logbook' (planned)".
- **Each character who learns it,** and where. A character counts as learning it in a revealing scene if they **appear in** that scene, or if it is in their viewpoint. If you connected a character to the secret with **knows**, **suspects** or **wrongly believes**, that is shown too.

"when not recorded" means the program knows the character is connected to the secret but cannot tell where they learn it.

**Reading it with judgment.** Everyone in the revealing scene is listed as learning it there, including a character who knew all along. If Cyrus is in the room when his own secret comes out, he "learns it" there. Read his line as "is there when it comes out".

### The reader's view, scene by scene

The second page is one table:

| Column | What it holds | Where it comes from |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Scene** | Each scene and card in reading order, with its planned tag | |
| **Reader suspects** | What the reader suspects by the end of the scene | **What the reader suspects by the end of this scene**, on the card's **The reader** page |
| **Revealed here** | Secrets this scene reveals, or "nothing" | **reveals** connections |
| **Who learns** | Who learns what here | Characters who **appear in** the scene |
| **Visible or hidden story** | Whether the scene shows what the characters believe (visible) or what is really going on (hidden) | **Visible or hidden story**, on the card's **The reader** page |

### Secret operations (author only)

A **hidden move** is something a character does in secret that the others misread or blame on someone else. The **Secret operations** log is "The only place the real story lives in order", and it is never shown in viewpoint summaries. It has a page of its own, the last one, **Secret operations (author only)**. Each schemer gets a table there: **When**, **The hidden move**, **What the others see**, **Who takes the blame**. The table appears once you have logged at least one move; until then the page holds only the form for adding one.

**To log a hidden move:**

1. Turn to the page **Secret operations (author only)**. At the bottom of it, beside **Log a hidden move**, choose the character in **Whose?**.
2. Type **What they secretly do**.
3. Click **Add**. It says "Added to the secret operations log."

Hidden moves are also kept in the Story Brain, under **Secret operations**, filed under that character. Open a move there to fill in **When (beat or rough point)**, **What the others see, and how they misread it** and **Who takes the blame**. Every field is listed in [Every kind and every field](#every-kind-and-every-field).

### Making a reveal show up here

1. In the Story Brain, make a record for the secret (an event works well). On its **When, and who knows** page, fill in **What actually happened**.
2. Open the scene card where the reader finds out.
3. On its **Notes and connections** page, make a connection: **reveals**, then the secret, then **Connect**.
4. Connect the characters who are there with **appears in**, if they are not already.
5. For a character who half-knows or has it wrong, open the character and connect them to the secret with **suspects** or **wrongly believes**.

**Asking "does anyone act before they know?"** One more pair of connections lets the Story review catch a character acting on a truth too early. Connect the character to the secret with **knows**, and connect the scene where they act on it with **depends on** the secret. If that scene would come before the scene where they learn it, the review asks.

### Who knows what: the questions it can raise

When a move or a placement puts things out of order, the Story review asks things like "Marisol Vega would act on 'Cyrus heard the mayday' in 'The harbor inquest' before learning it", or "'Whose boat is it?' would be answered in 'The registry' before the story raises it." (That second one comes from the **raises** and **answers** connections, for a question the story puts in the reader's mind and the scene that answers it.)

**The common mistake** is expecting the check to find reveals in your prose. It cannot read meaning. It knows a scene reveals something only when you connect it with **reveals**.

**Example.** The secret in *The Keeper of Gull Point*: the night Eli drowned, Cyrus heard his mayday on the harbor radio and did not launch the rescue boat. The reader meets it at All Is Lost, in "The logbook". Marisol learns it there (she appears in the scene). Ana Ruiz **wrongly believes** Eli's death was a plain accident. One look at this page shows that Ana never learns the truth at all. Intended?

## Setups and payoffs: every clue, start to finish

**Where:** **Continuity checks** > **Setups and payoffs**.

**Some terms, first.** A **setup** (or plant) is something placed early that matters later. A **payoff** is where it matters. A **clue** is a record in the Story Brain for one setup. The rule this page lives by: "If it pays off later, it has to be here first."

**Why it matters.** A payoff with no setup feels like a cheat. A setup with no payoff feels like a broken promise. Both are easy to lose across three hundred pages.

**Do you need it?** Mysteries and thrillers do, always. Most other novels have a handful of setups worth tracking: an object, a skill, a line of dialogue that comes back.

### Setups and payoffs: the clue table

The clue table is on the page **Clues**. If you keep any motifs, a second page, **Motifs**, sits beside it (see below); with no motifs there is only the one page, so no page tabs show at all. Until you have a clue, the page says "No clues yet. Add one in the Story Brain under Clues."

| Column | What it shows | Where it comes from |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **What** | The clue (click to open it), with "must pay off" under it if you marked it significant | The clue record |
| **Planted** | Where it is planted | Your words in **Where it is planted** on the clue, plus scenes connected with **plants** |
| **What it really means** | The truth behind it | **What it really means** on the clue |
| **Truth comes out** | Scenes that reveal it | **reveals** connections |
| **Pays off** | Where it pays off | Your words in **Where it pays off**, plus scenes connected with **pays off** |

**How your words show.** In **Planted** and **Pays off**, your own words come first, inked if Decided, in pencil if Developing. If your words name a scene card or a beat, the program finds it and shows it underneath: a card link with its planned tag, or "at the beat **Setup**". "none" means nothing is recorded.

**Motifs.** A **motif** is a recurring image or object, kept as a Motif record in the Story Brain. When you have at least one, the page tab **Motifs** appears after **Clues**. That page lists each motif (click its name to open it) and the scenes it appears in, or "Not placed in any scene yet."

**To fill it in:**

1. In the Story Brain, add a record under **Clues**.
2. On the clue, fill in **Where it is planted**, **What it really means** and **Where it pays off**.
3. If the reader must feel cheated without its payoff, set **Significant enough that it must pay off?** to "Yes, it must pay off".
4. If you prefer, connect scene cards to the clue with **plants** and **pays off** instead of (or as well as) writing the words.

### Setups and payoffs: the questions it can raise

These appear in the Story review:

| Question | What it means | What to do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "… pays off but nothing plants it first." | A payoff is recorded, and no plant is | Fill in **Where it is planted**, or connect the planting scene with **plants**. If the plant is truly missing, write it |
| "… is planted and marked significant, but nothing pays it off yet." | A clue you said must pay off has no payoff recorded | Record the payoff, or plan one |
| "The setup … would now come after its payoff …" | A move would put the payoff first | Leave the move, or move the setup earlier too |

**The common mistake** is marking every clue significant. Mark only the ones the reader will feel cheated without. Small textures can be planted and left.

**Example.** Eli's brass compass is planted in the Setup: "Where it is planted: the Setup, on the lamp-room windowsill, where Marisol has kept it for twelve years." It pays off in the Finale: "Where it pays off: the Finale, when she finds its twin in the empty boat." The page shows both, with the two beats linked. If you later drag the Finale scene onto an earlier beat, the Move dialog asks about it before anything changes.

## Storylines: the threads and how they braid

**Where:** **Continuity checks** > **Storylines**.

**Some terms, first.** A **storyline** is one thread of plot: the main plot, a romance, a mystery, a rivalry. Save the Cat calls the main one the A story and the next one the B story.

**What it shows.** A grid. Each storyline is a column; every scene and card is a row. A mark sits where a scene moves that storyline forward. Each column heading shows the storyline's letter and name (click to open it), and under it, its story type in slanted type, where it stands, and the values it carries. Above the grid: "The strongest scenes move more than one thread", with a count of the scenes that do.

**Why it matters.** A subplot that never touches the main plot feels like a second book stapled to the first. The grid shows exactly where your threads meet, and where they could meet more.

**Do you need it?** If your book has more than one thread, yes. A single-thread novella can skip it.

**Where it comes from.** Storyline records in the Story Brain, and **moves forward** connections from scenes and cards to them.

**To fill it in:**

1. In the Story Brain, under **Storylines**, add each storyline. Give it a **Letter (A, B, C…)** if you like.
2. Open a scene card. On its **Notes and connections** page, make a connection: **moves forward**, then the storyline, then **Connect**.
3. Repeat for each scene that moves a thread.

Until you have a storyline, the page says "No storylines yet."

### Storylines: the questions it can raise

- "… never shares a scene with another storyline. Does it run untouched beside the main story?" A thread that runs alone. Braid it in, or answer that it is meant to stand apart. (The A story is never asked this.)
- "… last appears in scene 12 of 40." A thread that stops early. This one is only an observation. It is fine if its later scenes are not mapped yet; if it really stops, does it end, or did you drop it? On the storyline, setting **Where it stands** to Dormant, Resolved or Intentionally unresolved (or saying it is held open in **Resolves now, or held open on purpose**) quiets both questions.

**The common mistake** is making a storyline for a value, like "trust" or "grief". Values ride on plot threads; they are not threads of their own. Put them on the storyline, under **Values it carries**.

**Example.** *The Keeper of Gull Point* has three storylines: "A: Who sent the boat", "B: Marisol and Ana", and "C: Cyrus's debt". Only "The harbor inquest" moves A and C together, so the line says "1 scene moves more than one." That tells you where the mystery and the harbor master's guilt touch, and that the romance with Ana never touches the mystery at all. A scene where Ana examines the boat's owner could braid all three.

## Cast: who is where, and the Bechdel test

**Where:** **Continuity checks** > **Cast**. The page is titled **The cast across the story**.

**What it shows.** A grid: each character is a row, every scene and card is a column in reading order, and a mark sits wherever a character is. Then, each on a page of its own: who shares scenes, groups that keep meeting, every pair, and the Bechdel test.

The Cast check has up to five numbered pages:

| Page | What is on it |
| --- | --- |
| **Across the story** | The gone-quiet notes, the key, and the cast grid |
| **Who is together** | Pick two or more characters and see every scene they share |
| **Groups that keep meeting** | Three or more characters who share two or more scenes. This page is there only when there are any. |
| **Two at a time** | Every pair that shares a scene |
| **The Bechdel test** | The scenes where two or more women are together, for you to answer |

Turn pages with the tabs or the links at the bottom of each page; **All pages** shows them all (see [Continuity checks with pages](#continuity-checks-with-pages)). The number of **The Bechdel test** turns ink blue only when the book passes.

**Why it matters.** Characters drift. A friend introduced in chapter 2 is forgotten by chapter 10. Two characters who should clash never share a scene. The grid shows your whole cast at once.

**Do you need it?** Any book with more than a handful of characters benefits. It is most useful in planning, before you write the scenes, and again in revision.

**Where it comes from.** Three places: your records (**appears in** connections on scene cards), each written scene's viewpoint, and the character's name found on the written page. Planned cards count too, so you can see the cast of a book before it is written.

### The top of the Cast page, and the page Across the story

Above the page tabs, so you see it whichever page you are on:

- **The Bechdel test result**, in one line: "The Bechdel test asks whether two named women talk to each other about something other than a man. Right now: passes, in 1 scene. It has its own page below." To see the scenes, click the page tab **The Bechdel test**.

On the first page, **Across the story**, above the grid:

- **Gone-quiet notes**, in boxes. Two kinds: "Ana Ruiz is away for 9 scenes, from … to …. Intended?" for a long absence, and "The ferry captain is introduced in … and not seen after …, 30 scenes before the end. Intended?" for a character who appears and then vanishes.
- **Not in any scene or card yet:** characters with a record who are not placed anywhere.

### Reading the cast grid

The key above the grid:

| Mark | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **●** (filled circle) | The character is the scene's **viewpoint** |
| **■** (filled square) | **In the scene (your records)**: connected with **appears in** |
| **○** (hollow circle) | **Named on the page**: their name appears in the written scene |
| Shaded column | A **planned scene card, not written yet** |

- **The top header** names the acts across the columns.
- **The numbers** are scenes and cards in reading order. A **·** (dot) is a card with no beat, so it has no number. Hover over a number to see its title, chapter and beat.
- **The number beside each name** counts the scenes and cards they are in.
- **Click any mark** to open that scene or card.

A ○ means only that the name was found in the text. If a mark looks odd, click it and check the page.

### Who is together, groups and pairs

Each of these has its own page.

**Who is together.** Click two or more names (they light up) to see every scene they share, with who else is there. Tick **Only scenes where no one else is there** to see only their scenes alone. **Clear** unpicks them.

Beside the result: **Their relationship** opens their relationship record, or **Make a relationship record** makes one, already connected to each of them. A relationship record can hold a pair, a family, a rivalry or a whole group.

**Groups that keep meeting.** Three or more characters who share two or more scenes. **Show scenes** lists them. When no group of three meets twice, this page is left out of the row.

**Two at a time.** Every pair that shares a scene, how often, and in which acts, with **Show scenes** and the same relationship links. Until any two characters share a scene, it says "No two characters share a scene yet."

### The Bechdel test

The **Bechdel test** is a well-known quick check of how women are written in a story: does it have at least two named women who talk to each other about something other than a man? It does not measure quality. A book can pass and be shallow, or fail for good reasons (a novel set on a fishing crew of men, say). It is a mirror, not a grade.

**How Story Canon runs it.** The program finds every scene and card where two or more women are together. Only you know what they talk about, so for each one it asks, and you answer:

1. Click the page tab **The Bechdel test**.
2. For each scene listed, with the women in it named beside it, click **They do** if they talk about something other than a man, or **Not here** if they do not.
3. Click the same button again to clear your answer.

**Gender comes only from what you write on each character's page, never from a name.** It is the **Gender identity** field, on the character's **Identity** page. A character with no gender recorded is listed under "No gender recorded for:", with a link to open them.

**The results you may see:**

| Result | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| "Passes, in N scenes." | At least one scene where you answered **They do** |
| "Waiting for your answers." | Scenes with two women exist, and some are unanswered |
| "Does not pass yet." | You have answered every scene, and none is **They do** |
| "No scene has two named women together yet." | No scene or card has two women in it |
| "Not enough named women recorded." | Fewer than two characters are recorded as women |

You may also see "Never in a scene with another woman:" followed by names.

**The common mistake** is treating the gone-quiet notes as orders. A mentor who vanishes after Act One may be exactly right. Answer "Intended?" in your head and move on.

**Example.** The grid for *The Keeper of Gull Point* shows Ana Ruiz in every early scene of the B Story, then nothing for nine scenes in Act Two. That is a long silence for the person Marisol is supposed to be learning to let stay. You add Ana to two Act Two cards. The Bechdel test lists "The clinic at night", where Marisol and Ana go over the boat's registration papers. You click **They do**, and the result reads "Passes, in 1 scene."

## Pacing: the shape of the book by length

**Where:** **Continuity checks** > **Pacing**.

**What it shows.** Each written scene as a bar, in reading order. The taller the bar, the longer the scene.

- **The left edge** shows the word scale, from 0 to your longest scene.
- **The dashed line**, labeled "usual", is your usual scene length: the median, meaning half your scenes are longer and half shorter. The line above the chart says what it is, such as "Your usual scene runs about 2,200 words."
- **The numbers under the bars** are scene numbers in reading order. On a long book, only every second, fifth or tenth is labeled.
- **"Ch 1", "Ch 2"** and thin lines mark where chapters begin.

**Hover over a bar** to see its title, chapter, words, viewpoint, beat, whether it is a scene or a sequel, its turn (the value at the start and end of the scene), its average sentence length, and how much of it is dialogue. **Click a bar** to open that scene in the Writing Room.

**As a table**, a fold-out under the chart, lists the same scenes with **#**, **Scene**, **Chapter**, **Words**, **Viewpoint**, **Beat**, **Turn** and **Dialogue**.

**Why it matters.** Rhythm is felt, not seen, while you draft. A run of long scenes tires a reader; a run of short ones can feel choppy. The chart makes rhythm visible.

**Do you need it?** Once you have written a good part of the book, yes. Before that, it has little to show.

**Where it comes from.** The words on the page in the Writing Room. Viewpoint, beat and turn come from each scene's card. Cards with no writing have no length, so they are not drawn; the line under the chart counts them.

### Pacing: the questions it can raise

These show as boxes above the chart:

- "… runs 8,400 words, well over your usual scene (2,200). Intended, or two scenes in one?" This appears only for a scene over 2,500 words and more than two and a half times your usual.
- "… starts and ends on the same value (Trust +). Every scene has to turn. Intended?" A scene's **turn** is how the value it is about (trust, safety, hope) changes from start to end. A scene that ends where it began may not be doing its job.

**What to look for.** A run of very long scenes, a run of very short ones, or one scene far longer than the rest.

**The common mistake** is making every bar the same height. Rhythm comes from variety: a long scene, then a short sharp one.

**Example.** The middle of *The Keeper of Gull Point* shows six bars near the dashed line, then one bar nearly four times as tall. Hovering shows it is "The storm night", and it is really two scenes: the rescue attempt and the long talk with Ana afterward. You split it.

## Reader response: the tabs a reader would stick in the book

**Where:** **Continuity checks** > **Reader response**.

**What it shows.** Chapter by chapter, the colored sticky tabs a reader would put in the book to find a spot again:

| Color | Tab |
| --- | --- |
| Purple | **Profound** |
| Pink | **Funny** |
| Yellow | **Relatable** |
| Green | **Shocking** |
| Gray | Any word of your own |

A line at the top counts how many chapters have each tab. Each chapter is listed with its number, its title, and its tabs, or "no tab yet".

**Why it matters.** Readers remember books by their moments. A chapter with no moment at all is a chapter a reader may skim.

**Do you need it?** It is optional, and most useful in revision. Some writers never use it; others love seeing the color of the whole book at once.

**Where it comes from.** Each scene card's **What you want the reader to feel**, on the card's page **The reader**. A chapter shows the tabs of all its scenes. Chapters belong to the Writing Room, so a card's tab shows here once its scene is written (with **Write this scene** on the card).

**To set a tab:**

1. Open a scene card and turn to its **The reader** page.
2. Beside **What you want the reader to feel**, click **Add**.
3. Click **Profound**, **Funny**, **Relatable** or **Shocking**, or type your own word.
4. Click **Save**. Add more than one if the scene earns it.

**What to look for.** A chapter with no tab may need a moment that makes the reader laugh, nod, gasp or stop. A long run of one color may want another kind.

**The common mistake** is tagging every scene with every feeling. A tab is a promise that this is the spot a reader would mark. Be choosy.

**Example.** *The Keeper of Gull Point* shows purple tabs in most chapters and green in only two: the empty boat in chapter 1 and the logbook near the end. The long middle has no shock at all. You move the discovery that the boat's registration was forged up to the Midpoint.

## Soundtrack: the songs in your story

**Where:** **Continuity checks** > **Soundtrack**.

**What it shows.** The songs in your story, in the order they first play in the book: title, artist, what the song carries, the chapters and scenes it plays in, and whose theme song it is. Titles and artists only, never lyrics.

**Why it matters.** Many writers use music as a private key to a mood or a character. Keeping it here keeps it consistent, and lets you hear the book's mood from start to end.

**Do you need it?** Only if music matters to you or to your story. Many writers never open it.

**Where it comes from.** Song records in the Story Brain, under **Soundtrack**, connected to scenes with **plays in**, or to a character with **is the theme song of**.

**To add a song:**

1. In the Story Brain, add a record under **Soundtrack** with the song's title. Fill in **Artist** and **What it carries**.
2. On its **Notes and connections** page, connect it to a scene card with **plays in**, or to a character with **is the theme song of**.

"Not placed in a scene yet" means the song has no **plays in** connection to a written scene.

**The common mistake** is quoting lyrics in your notes or manuscript. Song lyrics are usually under copyright, and quoting them in a published book often needs permission. Story Canon keeps titles only for that reason.

**Example.** The old lullaby Marisol's mother sang, "The Keeper's Waltz" (artist: Traditional), is connected to Marisol with **is the theme song of** and to the chapter 2 scene "The lamp room" with **plays in**. It shows as "Plays in Chapter 2, The lamp room. Theme song of Marisol Vega."

## Names: every name on the page, counted

**Where:** **Continuity checks** > **Names**. The page is titled **Names in the book**.

**What it shows.** Every name your written scenes use, how often, in how many scenes, and which record it belongs to. Names that look like misspellings come first.

**Why it matters.** A name spelled two ways is easy to miss on the page and easy to catch here. Copy editors charge for this.

**Do you need it?** Every writer with a draft benefits. It needs written scenes to work.

**How the program decides what is a name.** A capitalized word in the middle of a sentence counts as a name. At the start of a sentence every word is capitalized, so there a word counts only if it is a name from your records, such as a character, place or storyline.

**This can look broken, but it is working as designed.** If "Cyrs" (a typo) only ever starts a sentence, it is not counted, because the program cannot tell a misspelled name from any other first word. Once it appears mid-sentence ("Down at the dock, Cyrs was waiting"), it shows up.

### What's on the Names page

- **Spelled another way?** A box for each likely misspelling: "'Anna' appears 3 times; your record says 'Ana'. Intended?" (or "the book mostly says", when there is no record). Click a scene title in the box to open it and fix it.
- **Names with no record yet:** names used three times or more that have no record in the Story Brain, each with its count, such as "Ellery ×4".
- **The table:** **Name**, **Times**, **Record** (or "none") and **Scenes**, for up to 200 names.

**What to do with it.** Fix any misspelling you did not intend. Give a name you use often its own record, so the program can follow that person or place through the story (on the Cast check, for example).

**The common mistake** is making a record for every capitalized word. "Tuesday" and "Coast Guard" do not need records. Characters and places do.

**Door closed.** While the door is closed (see [Goals: door closed](#goals-door-closed)), this page shows "The door is closed until …, so possible misspellings of names wait until then." Click **Look anyway** to see it.

## Story review: every question in one place

**Where:** **Continuity checks** > **Story review**.

**What it shows.** "Questions, never verdicts." Every question the program can ask about your story right now, with the answers you have already given. A gray line says what it checks and when it last looked.

**Why it matters.** The other checks each look one way. The Story review gathers every question from all of them, plus a few of its own, so you can work through them in one sitting and keep a record of what you decided.

**Do you need it?** Once you have connections between scenes, cards on beats, or a few story times, yes. It is the one check worth opening regularly.

**Where it comes from.** Your records. It compares causes and effects across scenes and cards, beats on your beat sheet, viewpoint rules in your story world, clues marked significant, storylines, Ghost timing on character arcs, and characters in two places at the same story time.

### What each review question looks like

Each question has three lines:

1. **The question**, such as "'Eli's compass' pays off but nothing plants it first."
2. **What kind it is**, in gray: **A question**, **An observation** (a pattern worth noticing, not a problem), or **A fact from your records** (something certain, like a character in two places at once). After the kind comes the rule it checks, such as "payoff without a plant". Then up to three links to the records involved.
3. **Your answer:** a box, **Why it is this way (optional)**, and three buttons.

### Answering a review question

| Button | When to use it | What happens |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **It's intentional** | You did it on purpose | The question goes quiet for good. Your reason is kept under **Your answers** |
| **Not now** | You will deal with it later | It moves to **Set aside for later**, still visible, out of your way |
| **I've dealt with it** | You fixed it | The question goes quiet for good, under **Your answers** |

1. If you want to keep a reason, type it in **Why it is this way (optional)** first. "Flashback, on purpose" is enough.
2. Click the button.

**Your answers** lists every question you have answered, with "Intentional" or "Dealt with" and your reason. **Ask again** brings one back as an open question, for when you change your mind or change the book.

Questions under **Set aside for later** offer **It's intentional** and **I've dealt with it**, but not **Not now** (they are already set aside).

**The common mistake** is clicking **I've dealt with it** without changing anything, to clear the list. The question will stay quiet even if the problem is still there. If it is on purpose, choose **It's intentional** and write why. Future you will want the reason.

### When the review has no open questions

When nothing is open, the page says "No open questions." and explains: "Every check ran and none found anything to ask in what is recorded so far."

**This can look like the check did not run. It did.** Questions appear as your records give it something to compare: connections between scenes (causes, plants, reveals), cards decided on beats, story times and places, viewpoint rules. A fully planned book with no connections has little to compare.

### The questions the review can ask

| Rule (shown in gray) | What it asks about | Where to fix it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **cause before effect** | A scene that **causes**, **makes possible** or **motivates** another would come after it | The Board, or answer "flashback" |
| **dependency** | A scene that **depends on** another would come before it | The Board |
| **foreshadowing** | A scene that **foreshadows** another no longer comes first | The Board |
| **setup before payoff** | A plant would come after its payoff | Setups and payoffs, the Board |
| **raised before answered** | A question would be answered before the story **raises** it | The Board |
| **acting before knowing** | A character who **knows** something acts on it (**depends on** it) before learning it | Who knows what |
| **beat order** | A scene on an earlier beat comes after one on a later beat (an observation) | The Board, or the Writing Room |
| **beat sheet** | A beat starts later than your sheet advises; a "win" at a disaster beat; the Midpoint and All Is Lost both false victories (or both false defeats) | Where beats fall, the beat pages |
| **payoff without a plant** | A clue pays off with nothing planted | Setups and payoffs |
| **unpaid promise** | A significant clue never pays off | Setups and payoffs |
| **storyline weave** | A storyline never shares a scene with another | Storylines |
| **storyline continues** | A storyline stops early (an observation) | Storylines |
| **one viewpoint per scene** / **one viewpoint per chapter** | A head-hop against your viewpoint rule | Viewpoint |
| **withheld viewpoint** | A secret keeper's head too early | Viewpoint |
| **viewpoint balance** | A character has no viewpoint scene for a stretch (an observation) | Viewpoint |
| **two places at once** | A character in two scenes on the same day, at the same time of day, in different places (a fact) | Timelines, and each card's **When it happens** page |
| **Ghost timing** | A Ghost (a character's past wound) marked as happening during the story instead of before it | The character's arc |

**Door closed.** Like Names, the Story review waits while the door is closed (see [Goals: door closed](#goals-door-closed)): "The door is closed until …, so the story review waits until then." Click **Look anyway** to see it.

**Example.** In *The Keeper of Gull Point*, the card "The night Eli drowned" is connected to "Marisol stops leaving the island" with **causes**. You place the drowning scene at the Midpoint as a flashback, after the scene it caused. The review asks: "'Marisol stops leaving the island' is caused by 'The night Eli drowned', but would now come first in the book. Readers would meet the effect before the cause. Is that a deliberate flashback?" It is. You type "Flashback at the Midpoint, on purpose" and click **It's intentional**. The question moves to **Your answers**, reason and all, and never nags you again.
