# The words Story Canon uses, and getting help

This chapter teaches the small set of words that every screen in Story Canon uses, such as *record*, *field* and *Decided*. It then shows you how to read a record and your whole book at a glance, and every way to get help when something puzzles you. Read it right after [Welcome to Story Canon](#welcome-to-story-canon), before you start your first novel. Every later chapter uses these words freely, so when one of them feels fuzzy, come back here.

## Why the words come first

Story Canon gives a few ordinary words a particular meaning. Once you know them, every screen makes sense. If you skip them, a sentence such as "click **Decide this** beside the fact in the field" reads like a riddle.

Each word below is explained using only the words before it, so read them in order the first time. Every one comes with an example from the same book, *The Keeper of Gull Point*, a mystery with a heart:

- **Marisol Vega**, 41, is the keeper of the Gull Point lighthouse. She has never left the island.
- Her younger brother **Eli** drowned twelve years ago. That loss is her wound.
- The story starts moving when an empty boat washes onto her rocks.
- **Ana Ruiz** is the new doctor in the harbor town of **Port Ellery**. **Cyrus Hale** is the harbor master, who knows more than he says.

## The shared words, in the order you need them

### Novel

A **novel** is one book you are working on. Story Canon keeps each novel separate: its pages, its plan, its people and its history all belong to it and to nothing else.

- **Why it matters:** you can work on several books without them ever mixing. Switching books never shows you the other book's notes.
- **Do most writers need it?** Yes. You start by making one.
- **Example:** *The Keeper of Gull Point* is one novel. If you also have a half-finished romance, that is a second novel, with its own everything.

### Library

The **Library** is your shelf of novels. It is the first screen you see, and the only one you see before a novel is open. Each novel on the shelf shows its title, its stage, its word count and the next thing to do in it. Click a title to open it. To come back to the shelf from inside a novel, click **Library** at the top left of the page.

- **The common mistake:** looking for your novels inside **Settings** or **Import and export**. They are always on the Library shelf.
- **Example:** your Library shows *The Keeper of Gull Point*, 14,200 words, with "Next:" and the scene you planned to write.

The chapter [Your first hour: the Library and a new novel](#your-first-hour-the-library-and-a-new-novel) explains everything on the Library screen.

### View

A **view** is one of the main screens of a novel, picked from the list of names down the right edge of the window: **Where was I**, **The Writing Room**, **Story Brain**, **Narrative Zoom**, **Conversations**, then **Search**, **Story Trail**, **Import and export**, **Settings** and **Help**. The view you are on is lit cream and bold in that list. [Welcome to Story Canon](#welcome-to-story-canon) says what each one is for.

- **Why it matters:** this guide often says "in the Story Brain view" or "open the Writing Room". That always means: click that name in the list on the right.
- **Example:** to look up Port Ellery, you open the **Story Brain** view. To write about it, you open **The Writing Room** view.

### Scene

A **scene** is one stretch of your written book: a piece of continuous action in one place and time, written as prose. In Story Canon a scene is something you write on, in **The Writing Room**. Each scene has a title and its own word count.

- **Why it matters:** Story Canon saves, counts, plans and checks your book scene by scene. Most tools in the program point at a scene.
- **Example:** "The boat on the rocks" is a scene: Marisol, at dawn, climbing down to the empty boat.

### Chapter

A **chapter** is a group of scenes, in order. In the Writing Room, the column on the left (the binder) lists your chapters, each with its scenes under it. **Add chapter** adds a chapter at the end of the book; **Add scene** under a chapter adds a scene to it.

- **The common mistake:** thinking you must decide your chapters before you write. You can start with one chapter and one scene and split things up later.
- **Example:** Chapter 1 holds three scenes: "The light at dusk", "The boat on the rocks" and "Port Ellery in the rain".

### Record

A **record** is one thing in your story that gets its own page in the **Story Brain**. A character is a record. So is a place, a relationship, a clue, a question you have, a song on your book's soundtrack. Think of a record as an index card with a name written across the top.

- **Where you find records:** in the **Story Brain** view. The list on its left groups them, with how many you have of each.
- **Why it matters:** everything you know about one person or place sits on one page, so you stop hunting through old notebooks for the color of her coat.
- **Example:** Marisol Vega is a record. So is the lighthouse, and so is Port Ellery.

### Kind

A **kind** is the sort of record something is. Character is a kind. Place is a kind. Clue is a kind. Each record belongs to exactly one kind, and you choose it when you add the record (for example with **Add character** in the Story Brain). You can also add a kind of your own.

- **Why it matters:** the kind decides what a record's page offers. A character's page asks about goals and fears; a place's page asks what it is like and who is usually there.
- **Example:** Marisol and Cyrus Hale are both of the kind *Character*. The lighthouse is of the kind *Place*.

### Field

A **field** is one labeled place to write on a record. Each kind of record offers its own set of fields, grouped under headings on the page. A character has fields such as *Role*, *Who they are* and *What they stand to lose*. A place has fields such as *What it is like* and *Details to reuse*.

- **Every field is optional.** A field is a place to put what you know when you know it. An empty field is never a mistake, and no book needs all of them.
- **How to write in one:** click **Add** at the right of the field's label. The steps are in [Writing a fact and choosing its status](#writing-a-fact-and-choosing-its-status).
- **Example:** Marisol's record has a field called *What they stand to lose*.

### Fact

A **fact** is one entry written in a field. It is more than its words: each fact carries a **status** (how sure you are, explained next), a date and time, and, when it didn't come from you, a **source** (where it came from). All three are shown in a narrow column to the right of the words, called the **margin**.

A field can hold more than one fact. That is on purpose: you can keep two or three possible answers side by side while you make up your mind.

- **Why it matters:** because each fact remembers when it was written and who said it, you can always tell your own decision from an idea you once copied in.
- **Example:** Marisol's *What they stand to lose* field holds two facts: "The lighthouse itself" and "The last thing she has of Eli". Both stay until you choose.

### Status

A **status** is how sure you are about a fact. There are six, and Story Canon always calls them by these words:

| Status | Means |
| --- | --- |
| **Decided** | You have made up your mind. |
| **Developing** | You are still working on it. |
| **Still open** | A question that matters, which you have not answered yet. |
| **Example** | An illustration of what an answer could look like. Never part of your story. |
| **Suggestion** | Someone else's idea, waiting for your choice. |
| **Set aside** | Something you have let go of, but kept. |

Each status looks different on the page, the way ink looks different from pencil, so you can tell them apart without reading a word. [Reading a record at a glance](#reading-a-record-at-a-glance) shows each look and what you can do with it.

- **Example:** "Eli drowned twelve years ago" is **Decided**. "Who owned the empty boat?" is **Still open**.

(You can rename the status words in **Settings**, under **Words for decisions**. This guide always uses the words above.)

### Canon

**Canon** is what is officially true in your story. In Story Canon, canon is everything you have marked **Decided**. Developing facts might become canon. Still open questions are not canon yet. Examples and set-aside facts never are. A suggestion becomes canon only if you decide it.

- **Why it matters:** the rest of the program builds on canon. The checks in Narrative Zoom and the collaborator (next) treat Decided facts as true and everything else as possible.
- **The common mistake:** marking something Decided just so the page looks finished. If you aren't sure, leave it in pencil. Pencil costs nothing.
- **Example:** "Marisol has never left the island" is canon. "Maybe Ana grew up in Port Ellery" is not, until you decide it.

### The collaborator

**The collaborator** is a writing partner built into Story Canon, powered by Claude, an AI made by the company Anthropic. It is optional, and it is off until you turn it on, novel by novel, in **Settings** under **The collaborator**, using your own Claude API key (a private code, like a password, that you get from Anthropic and pay for by use).

Before it answers, it reads your decisions, your guardrails (rules your story must never break), your open questions and your **About you** answers, so it knows your book and how you like to be helped. It never types into your manuscript and it never decides anything.

Some screens, and planning you bring in, mention an AI by name, such as ChatGPT or Claude. Those are outside chats your planning came from. Inside Story Canon, the collaborator is the one partner. It has its own chapter: [Conversations and the collaborator](#conversations-and-the-collaborator).

- **Example:** you ask the collaborator, "Why would Cyrus hide what he knows about the boat?" It answers in the conversation, and anything it thinks belongs on a record arrives as a suggestion (next).

### Suggestion and slip

A **suggestion** is an idea about your story that came from someone other than you: the collaborator, or an outside chat (with ChatGPT or Claude, say) that you brought in with your planning. On a record, a suggestion is drawn as a **slip**: a small sheet of paper tucked in between the pages at a slight angle, in a handwriting typeface, rather than written on the page itself.

- **Why it matters:** you see the idea right where it applies, but it never counts as yours, and never becomes canon, until you choose it.
- **What you can do:** take it in, keep it for later, or let it go. The buttons are explained in [Suggestion: a slip, tucked in](#suggestion-a-slip-tucked-in).
- **The common mistake:** saying "sure, sounds good" to an AI in a chat and assuming that settled it. It didn't. If you want the idea, accept it on the record, on purpose.
- **Example:** a slip on Cyrus Hale's record reads "He was the last person to see Eli alive." It is the collaborator's idea. It waits.

You can't give one of your own facts the Suggestion status. Suggestions only arrive from somewhere else.

### Beat

A **beat** is a turning point in a story: a place where the story has to change direction, so that afterward the hero can't go back to how things were. A **beat sheet** is a list of these turning points in order, with a rough idea of where each one falls in the book (for example, "about 10% of the way in"). An **act** is a large section of the story; most beat sheets group their beats into three acts: the beginning, the middle and the end.

In Story Canon, each beat of your beat sheet is a record of the kind *Beat*, and you see them laid out in **Narrative Zoom**. A beat sheet is optional. If you find your story as you write, you can work without one.

- **The common mistake:** treating a beat as a slot to fill with an event. A beat is a *turn*. If nothing is different for the hero afterward, the beat isn't there, however much happens.
- **Example:** in the beat sheet called *Save the Cat*, the Catalyst is the beat that kicks the story into motion. In *The Keeper of Gull Point*, the Catalyst is the empty boat washing onto Marisol's rocks. Her quiet routine is broken, and she can't pretend it isn't.

### Scene card

A **scene card** is your plan for one scene, like the index cards novelists pin to a corkboard. It is a record of the kind *Scene card*. It holds what the scene must do, whose goal drives it, what stands in the way and how it turns. You can place a scene card on a beat, to say "this scene happens here".

A scene card and a scene are two different things. The **scene** is the written page, in the Writing Room. The **scene card** is the plan for it. A card can exist long before its scene does, and you can write a scene with no card at all.

- **Example:** the scene card "The boat on the rocks" says: Marisol wants to know whose boat it is; Cyrus arrives and tows it away before she can look inside. It is placed on the Catalyst. The scene with the same name is the prose you write from that plan.

### Page and All pages

A record is laid out like a small notebook with numbered **pages**. Under the record's name is a row of page tabs (1, 2, 3 and so on, each with a name), and at the end of the row, **All pages**. Each page holds the fields that belong together. One page shows at a time; **All pages** shows every page at once, one after another.

- **Why it matters:** a character can have dozens of fields. Pages keep them in sensible groups so a record never becomes one long scroll.
- **Example:** Marisol's record has a page called *Inner life*, where her wound (Eli's drowning) is written.

How to read and use the pages is in [Record pages: the numbered tabs](#record-pages-the-numbered-tabs).

### Capture

**Capture** is the button at the top right of the page for catching a passing thought without losing your place. You press it (or **Ctrl+Shift+N**), type the thought, choose what it is, and save. The thought is kept exactly as you wrote it, and you are back where you were.

- **Why it matters:** an idea that arrives mid-sentence is easy to lose and expensive to chase. Capture keeps it in ten seconds.
- **Example:** halfway through a scene you think, "Does Ana know how to swim?" You capture it as a question and keep writing.

The full steps are in [Capture: catching a thought without losing your place](#capture-catching-a-thought-without-losing-your-place).

### Help and F1

**Help** is this guide, inside the program. Press **F1** on your keyboard to open it, or click **Help** (the last name in the list on the right inside a novel, or the **Help** button at the top right of the Library). Details are in [Getting help](#getting-help).

- **Example:** you are not sure what a beat sheet is. Press **F1**, type *beat* in **Search the guide**, and click the section you want.

### Why it matters

**Why it matters** is the small link under a field on a record. Under many fields is one line saying what the field is for, followed by **Why it matters**. Click it to fold out more: whether most books need the field, why it helps, the common mistake and an example. Click **Less** to fold it away.

- **Example:** under Marisol's *What they stand to lose* field, **Why it matters** explains that stakes are what make a reader afraid for a character, with an example.

## Reading a record at a glance

When you open a record in the Story Brain, you see, from the top down:

1. **The record's name**, in large letters. Click it and type to rename the record.
2. **A row of small controls**: **Kept for you**, **Ask about this**, and sometimes **Where did this come from?** These are explained in [The Story Brain](#the-story-brain).
3. **The status legend**, a single line of five samples (explained next).
4. **Suggestions waiting for you**, if the collaborator has left any for this record.
5. On a character, **Low-energy day? One line counts.** with a **Fill in one line** button, while any of the simplest fields are still empty.
6. **The page tabs**, numbered, with **All pages** at the end.
7. **The page itself**: groups of fields, each field with its facts.

### The status legend

Just under the record's name is a line of small samples, one for each status you will meet on the page:

*Decided (inked)*, *Developing (pencil)*, *Still open (a blank line)*, *Example, never canon*, *Suggestion (a slip, tucked in)*

Each sample is drawn the way that status looks on the page, so the legend is a key you can glance at whenever you forget which look is which. If you renamed the status words in **Settings**, the legend shows your words. Set aside does not appear in the legend, because set-aside facts are folded away out of sight (see [Set aside: folded away, never deleted](#set-aside-folded-away-never-deleted)).

### Status inks: how each status looks

On a record, the color is on the words themselves, not only on a label. Each status has its own ink:

| Status | How its words look | In the margin |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Decided** | Ink blue, with a small blue square in front | **DECIDED**, in blue, with its own small square |
| **Developing** | Pencil gray, with a dashed underline and a short dashed mark in front | **DEVELOPING**, in gray |
| **Still open** | Slanted gray words sitting on a ruled line, like a form waiting to be filled, with a short solid dash in front | **STILL OPEN** |
| **Example** | Sepia (a warm brown), slanted, with a small boxed label reading "Example" in front | **EXAMPLE**, in sepia |
| **Suggestion** | A white slip with a purple left edge, tucked in at a slight angle, written in a handwriting typeface | **SUGGESTION**, and who suggested it |
| **Set aside** | Hidden from the field. When you show it, it is faded and small, with "(Set aside" and its date after it | |

In dark mode the inks are lighter versions of the same colors, so they still read clearly.

### Decided: ink blue with a square

- **What it means:** you have made up your mind. This is canon.
- **How it looks:** ink blue words with a small blue square in front, like something written in pen.
- **Why it matters:** you can build on it without second-guessing. The checks and the collaborator treat it as true.
- **What you can do:** **Not decided after all** turns it back into pencil (Developing). **Set aside** retires it.
- **The common mistake:** inking things to make a page look finished. If you aren't sure, leave it in pencil.
- **Example:** "Eli drowned twelve years ago, off the north rocks." You know this. It is in ink.

### Developing: pencil gray, dashed

- **What it means:** you are still working on it.
- **How it looks:** pencil-gray words with a dashed underline and a short dashed mark in front.
- **Why it matters:** you can keep several versions side by side and compare them. Nothing forces you to choose before you're ready.
- **What you can do:** **Decide this** makes it Decided. If the field already had a decided answer, the old one is kept in the field's history, never thrown away. (A few fields, such as a character's *Values*, are lists: there, several decided facts can stand together, and deciding one replaces nothing.)
- **The common mistake:** thinking two pencil answers in one field are a problem to fix. They are a choice you haven't made yet.
- **Example:** Marisol's two possible losses, "The lighthouse itself" and "The last thing she has of Eli", both in pencil.

### Still open: a blank line

- **What it means:** a question that matters, and that you have not answered yet.
- **How it looks:** slanted gray words sitting on a ruled line, with a short solid dash in front. If nothing has been written at all, the line reads *Still open: nothing written yet* and offers **Write it**.
- **Why it matters:** "I haven't decided" is different from "I forgot". Marking a question open says *this matters, and it isn't settled*. Story Canon lists it for you (on **Where was I**, under **Questions still open**) and never fills it in by guessing.
- **What you can do:** **Write it** opens the field so you can answer. If the open line already has words (often the question itself, or a note about what is undecided), **Decide this** settles that wording as your answer.
- **The common mistake:** feeling you must answer every open line before you start writing. You don't. Many answers turn up while you write.
- **Example:** on the record for the empty boat: "Whose boat is it? Not Eli's, but someone wants her to think so." The answer is the heart of the mystery, and you haven't settled it.

### Example: sepia, with a boxed label

- **What it means:** an illustration of what an answer *could* look like. An example is never part of your story.
- **How it looks:** sepia (warm brown) slanted words, in a book typeface, with a small boxed label reading "Example" in front, so you can't mistake it for a plan.
- **Why it matters:** an example teaches the shape of an answer without sneaking in as the answer.
- **What you can do:** **Set aside**, or **Where did this come from?** when it came from somewhere. There is no **Decide this** beside an example. That is not broken: an example can't become canon. If you like it, write your own version in the same field and mark that one Decided.
- **The common mistake:** reading a vivid example as the plan. Vivid is not the same as decided.
- **Example:** on Cyrus Hale's *Who they are* field, an example reads "A gruff harbor master who protects the town by keeping its secrets." It shows what a *Who they are* line can sound like. It does not say who Cyrus is.

### Suggestion: a slip, tucked in

- **What it means:** an idea from someone other than you, waiting for your choice.
- **How it looks:** a white slip of paper with a purple left edge, tilted very slightly, in a handwriting typeface, as if someone else had written it and tucked it between the pages. The margin says **SUGGESTION** and names who suggested it, such as *Suggested by ChatGPT* or *Suggested by the collaborator*.
- **Why it matters:** you see the idea right where it applies, but it never counts as yours until you choose it.

Suggestions reach a record in two ways, and the buttons differ:

| Where you see it | Where it came from | What you can do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Inside a field, as a fact | Planning you brought in (for example a chat with ChatGPT) | **Decide this** takes it in as Decided. **Set aside** lets it go. |
| Under **Suggestions waiting for you**, above the page tabs | The collaborator | **Accept as Decided** or **Accept as Developing** takes it in; the fact then lands in its field, the margin adds *accepted by you*, and the record turns to the page where it landed. **Not yet** keeps it waiting (marked "you said not yet"). **No** lets it go. A suggestion for something other than a fact (a new record, or a connection between two records) has a single **Accept** button instead. |

- **The common mistake:** leaving slips to pile up. They do no harm, but they are decisions you haven't made. **Choices ahead** in the Story Brain gathers them all in one list.
- **Example:** under **Suggestions waiting for you** on Cyrus's record: "He was the last person to see Eli alive." You click **Not yet**. It stays, marked "you said not yet", until you are ready.

### Set aside: folded away, never deleted

- **What it means:** you have let this go, but it is kept.
- **Where it goes:** it disappears from the field. Under that field a link appears: **Show earlier and set-aside versions**, with a count in brackets. Click it to see retired facts, faded and small, each followed by its status and date in brackets, along with earlier decisions that were replaced (marked "replaced"). Click **Hide earlier and set-aside versions** to fold them away again.
- **What you can do:** **Bring back** beside a set-aside fact returns it to pencil (Developing).
- **Why it matters:** nothing in Story Canon is ever silently deleted. You can let go of an idea without fear.
- **Example:** you set aside "Eli drowned saving Marisol", because it made her guilt too simple. Months later, it is still there under **Show earlier and set-aside versions (1)** if you want it back.

### The margin beside each fact

To the right of each fact's words is its margin. It holds, from the top:

| In the margin | What it tells you |
| --- | --- |
| The status, in small capital letters (such as **DECIDED**), and the date and time | How sure you are, and when it was written down |
| Where it came from, in slanted type (such as *From* a document's name, or *Suggested by ChatGPT*) | Who said it. Facts you type yourself have no line here, because they are yours. |
| When it is true, for fields that ask (such as *Before the story* or *True when the story opens*) | Whether this was true in the past, at the start, or happens during the story |
| The buttons | What you can do with this fact: **Decide this**, **Not decided after all**, **Set aside**, **Write it**, **Where did this come from?** (only the ones that apply) |

**Where did this come from?** opens a small window showing the document, row or conversation the fact came from, with its date. On a narrow window, the margin moves under the words instead of beside them.

### Writing a fact and choosing its status

1. On a record page, find the field you want and click **Add** at the right of its label. (The button changes to **Close** while the box is open.)
2. Type your words in the box. Some fields offer clickable words under the box, followed by "or your own words"; click one to fill it in, or type your own.
3. In the small menu under the box, choose the status: **Decided**, **Developing**, **Still open** or **Example**. It starts on **Developing**. A gray note beside it reminds you what will happen: "Sits beside any other possibilities." for Developing, or "The earlier decision stays in history." when you decide a field that already has a decision.
4. For fields that can change over time, a second menu asks when this is true: **Before the story**, **True when the story opens**, **Happens during the story** or **Timing not decided**. You can leave it on **Timing**.
5. Click **Save**. (**Save** stays gray until you type something, except for **Still open**, which you may save empty to mark a question you haven't put into words yet.)

To change a fact you already have, use the buttons in its margin.

**Example.** On Marisol's record, under *What they stand to lose*, you click **Add**, type "The last thing she has of Eli", leave the menu on **Developing**, and click **Save**. It appears in pencil beside "The lighthouse itself".

### Record statuses that look wrong but are working

- **No Decide this beside an example.** On purpose. Examples can't become canon.
- **Two or three answers in one field, all in pencil.** On purpose. They wait until you choose.
- **A decided answer vanished after you decided a different one.** It was replaced, not lost. Click **Show earlier and set-aside versions** under that field.
- **Your status words don't match this guide.** Someone renamed them in **Settings**, under **Words for decisions** (for example, "Inked" instead of "Decided"). Your words show everywhere, and the looks stay the same.
- **A decided suggestion still says *Suggested by ChatGPT*.** On purpose. Deciding it makes it yours, but Story Canon still remembers where the idea started.

### Why statuses matter to the rest of the program

Statuses are not decoration. The rest of the program reads them:

- **Where was I** lists your open questions and your latest decisions.
- **Choices ahead**, in the Story Brain, collects every open question, every field with competing pencil answers, and every suggestion waiting.
- **Narrative Zoom** counts a scene card as placed only when its spot is decided, so its checks never report a problem based on a guess.
- **The collaborator** treats Decided facts as true, treats pencil as possibilities, never fills in an open line by guessing, and never offers an example as a fact.

So the more honest your statuses, the more useful everything else becomes.

### Record pages: the numbered tabs

A record with more than a handful of fields is split into numbered pages, like a notebook. Under the legend is the row of page tabs. Each tab shows a page number in a small circle and the page's name.

- **The page you are on** is the tab joined to the page, in bold purple with a pink line across its top.
- **A page number turns ink blue** (a filled blue circle) when that page has something written on it. Hold the mouse over a tab and it says *Something is written on this page* or *Nothing on this page yet*.
- **Turning pages:** click a tab, or use the links at the bottom of each page, which name the page before and after (for example **Page 4: Personality** and **Page 6: What opposes them**).
- **Story Canon remembers** which page you were on for each record while the program is open.

These are the pages each kind has:

| Kind | Its pages |
| --- | --- |
| **Character** | 1 Who they are, 2 Identity, 3 Looks and voice, 4 Personality, 5 Goals, motives and values, 6 What opposes them, 7 Inner life, 8 Arc, 9 Their world and their people, 10 Interview, 11 Notes, guardrails and writing aids |
| **Beat** | 1 In this book, 2 Save the Cat's questions, 3 Notes and connections |
| **Scene card** | 1 The scene, 2 When it happens, 3 Who tells it, 4 Mood and feeling, 5 The reader, 6 Scene and sequel, 7 Notes and connections |
| **Story world** (the record for the whole book) | 1 The heart of the book, 2 What it means, 3 Time, viewpoint and knowledge, 4 Notes and connections |
| **Event** and **Reveal** | 1 What happens, 2 When, and who knows, 3 Notes and connections |
| **Every other kind** | One page named for the kind (for example *The place* or *The clue*), then Notes and connections |

**Pages with nothing to show are left out.** Some fields come with a method you turn on in **Novel setup** (for example, goal and motive from Randy Ingermanson's Snowflake method). A page made only of such fields appears once you turn that method on. So a character may show fewer than eleven tabs, and the numbers close up. A record with only one page shows no tabs at all.

**Nothing is ever hidden.** A group of fields that no page names (such as a field you added yourself) goes on the last page.

### Record pages: All pages

**All pages**, at the end of the row of tabs, shows every page of the record at once, each with its number and name as a heading, separated by dashed lines. There are no page-turn links in this view, because there is nothing to turn to.

- **When to use it:** to read a whole character straight through, or to scroll for something when you don't know which page holds it.
- **The common mistake:** living in All pages all the time and finding records overwhelming. One page at a time is calmer.
- **Example:** before writing Marisol's big scene with Ana, you click **All pages** on Marisol's record and read everything you know about her, top to bottom.

### What sits above the page tabs

Two things stay above the tabs, whatever page you are on:

- **Suggestions waiting for you** (when there are any). Accepting one turns to the page where it landed, so you see it in place.
- **Low-energy day? One line counts.** with **Fill in one line**, on a character, while any of the simplest fields (such as looks or personality) are still empty. Click it and Story Canon picks one empty field, turns to its page and opens it for you to write one line.

**Search** opens a record on the page that holds what you found, too. See [Finding things: Search and Story Trail](#finding-things-search-and-story-trail).

**Example.** It's late and you're tired. On Marisol's record you click **Fill in one line**. It turns to page 3, Looks and voice, and opens a field. You type "Salt-stiff wool coat, always buttoned wrong." One line counts.

## Reading your book at a glance: the story strip

Once a novel is open, a thin line runs across the purple cover above the page. It is a map of your whole book, from the first page (left) to the last (right), and it is there on every view.

### What the story strip shows

- **If your novel has a beat sheet**, the strip shows your acts and beats.
- **If your novel has no beat sheet** (you find the story as you write), the strip shows your chapters instead, each one as wide as its share of your words.

| Mark | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| Slanted words above the line (*Act One*, *Act Two, first half*, *Act Two, second half*, *Act Three*) | The names of the acts, each over a faint band showing how much of the book it covers. In a book without a beat sheet, chapter names show here instead (short chapters may go unlabeled). |
| A thin white tick, or a faint shaded band with a line at its left | One beat, placed where it falls in the book. Short beats are ticks; beats that cover a stretch of pages are bands. |
| A small solid cream box above a beat | One scene card **placed** there: decided, this scene goes here. In a book without a beat sheet, one box stands for one scene in that chapter. |
| A small hollow box (an outline only) | One scene card that **might go** there. It is in pencil: you haven't decided its spot. |
| A pink pin with a round head | Where the scene you have open falls in the book. |

Up to five boxes show above any one beat. A beat with more still shows five.

**Pointing and clicking.** Hold the mouse over a beat and a small label gives its name and what is there, such as *Catalyst, 1 placed, 2 might go here*. Click a beat to open **Narrative Zoom**.

**Example.** Above the Catalyst on the strip of *The Keeper of Gull Point* is one solid box ("The boat on the rocks", decided) and two hollow ones (two other scenes that might go there). While you write "The boat on the rocks", the pink pin stands at the Catalyst.

### When the story strip looks broken but isn't

- **No pink pin.** The pin shows only when a scene is open in the Writing Room and its scene card is decided on a beat. A scene that only *might* go somewhere has no fixed spot to pin. In a book without a beat sheet, the pin shows the chapter you are in.
- **The strip shows only "Chapter 1".** Your novel has no beat sheet yet. Choose one in **Novel setup**, under **My storytelling toolkit**, and the acts and beats appear.
- **The strip is empty.** The novel has no chapters and no beat sheet yet.
- **The strip disappeared.** You are in a sprint (a timed stretch of writing) or in **Focus** (only the page on screen). Both hide everything but the page.

## Getting help

Story Canon teaches in two places: in **Help**, which holds this whole guide, and right beside your work, in small folds you open when you want them. You never need to read anything before you start. Open the teaching when something puzzles you.

### Opening Help inside a novel

There are three ways to open Help while a novel is open:

1. Press **F1** on your keyboard, from any view. Help opens at the start of the guide.
2. Click **Help**, the last of the small names in the list on the right.
3. Click **Read the full chapter in Help** at the end of an *About ...* fold (see below). Help opens at the exact section for that kind of record, and the heading glows pink for a moment so you can see where you landed.

While a sprint is running, **F1** does nothing, because a sprint keeps you on the page.

To leave Help, click any other name in the list on the right, or the pink **Return to Writing** ribbon if you came from the page.

### Opening Help from the Library

You can read the guide before you have a novel at all.

1. In the Library, click **Help** at the top right of the window, on the purple edge, or press **F1**.
2. The whole guide opens over the shelf, with the same search and chapter list as inside a novel.
3. Click **Close Help** at the bottom right, or press **Esc**, to go back to the shelf.

**Example.** On your first day, before starting *The Keeper of Gull Point*, you click **Help** in the Library and read this chapter.

### What is on the Help screen

Help has two columns.

- **Search the guide** (the box at the top left). Type two or more letters, such as *beat* or *decided*. A list of matching sections appears under the box, each with the chapter it is in and a few words around the match. Click a section name to jump there. If nothing matches, it says so; try another word. Clear the box to see the chapter list again.
- **The chapter list** (under the search box). Click a chapter to open it. The chapter you are reading is shown in bold, and its main sections are listed beneath it so you can jump straight to one.
- **The chapter itself** (the large area on the right). Links inside the guide stay inside Help.

**The common mistake** is scrolling through chapters looking for a word. Type it in **Search the guide** instead.

### Why it matters: teaching under a field

Under many fields on a record is one gray line saying what the field is for, followed by a **Why it matters** link.

1. Click **Why it matters**. A fold opens under the field.
2. Read it. It starts with a small label saying how many books need this field: **Most books need this**, **Useful for many books** or **Only for some books**. Then it says why the field helps, **The common mistake**, and an **Example**.
3. Click **Less** to fold it away.

Nothing opens on its own, so the page stays calm once you know your way around. A field with no teaching shows only its gray line, or nothing.

**Not the same as its neighbors.** When two fields sound alike (such as *Goal* and *What they think they want*), a short note under one, starting **Not the same as its neighbors.**, says how they differ.

**Under a group heading**, a line in slanted type says what that group of fields is for. Some group headings name the teacher the fields come from in small type after a plain name, such as *Goal and motive (Ingermanson)*.

**Example.** You wonder whether Ana needs anything in *What they stand to lose*. You click **Why it matters** and see **Most books need this**: without stakes, a reader has no reason to be afraid for her.

### About ...: what they are and how to use them

At the top of the first page of every record, and at the top of each list of records in the Story Brain, is a fold named for its kind, such as **About characters: what they are and how to use them** or **About places: what they are and how to use them**.

- **What it holds:** what this kind of record is and why it helps, how it differs from kinds that sound alike, **When to use it**, **The common mistake** and an **Example**.
- **How it opens:** it is open the first time you meet each kind. Click its heading (with the small ▾) to fold it; it then stays folded for that kind, on every record, until you click it open again (▸).
- **The common mistake:** folding it before reading it once. It is the quickest way to learn what a kind is for.

### Read the full chapter in Help

At the end of every *About ...* fold is **Read the full chapter in Help**. Click it and Help opens at the matching part of [Every kind and every field](#every-kind-and-every-field), such as *Characters: every field*, where every field of that kind is explained in one place.

### How this view works

At the top of each continuity check in Narrative Zoom (the checks that catch problems such as a timeline slip) is a fold called **How this view works**. Click it to see what the check shows, where its information comes from, and what to do with what it finds. The chapter [Catching problems: the continuity checks](#catching-problems-the-continuity-checks) covers each check.

### Every kind and every field: the reference chapter

The last chapter of this guide, [Every kind and every field](#every-kind-and-every-field), explains every kind of record and every field on it: what it is, why it matters, whether most books need it, the common mistake, and an example. It is the same teaching you see when you click **Why it matters**, gathered in one place, so you can read a kind straight through or look up one field.

- **How it is ordered:** one section per kind, named like *Characters: every field* or *Places: every field*, each split into the same groups you see on the record.
- **Kinds that only some books use** (such as Magic and powers, or Languages) appear in the Story Brain when you switch on worldbuilding for a book in **Novel setup**.
- **When to use it:** when you want to know everything a kind of record can hold before you start one, or when **Why it matters** has left you wanting more.

### Which help to reach for

| You want to know | Reach for |
| --- | --- |
| What one field is for | **Why it matters**, under the field |
| How two similar fields differ | **Not the same as its neighbors.**, under the field |
| What a kind of record is for | **About ...: what they are and how to use them**, at the top of the record or list |
| Every field of one kind, in one place | **Read the full chapter in Help** |
| What a continuity check is telling you | **How this view works**, at the top of the check |
| Anything else | **F1**, then **Search the guide** |

## Capture: catching a thought without losing your place

An idea arrives mid-sentence. Don't stop to file it. Catch it.

1. Click **Capture** at the top right of the page, or press **Ctrl+Shift+N**. A small window opens with a box for your words.
2. Type the thought.
3. Choose what it is. The line under the choices says where each one goes:
   - **Just a thought**: anything, unsorted. It waits in *Captured thoughts* until you file it as a question or an idea, or leave it be. If you are not sure, use this one.
   - **A question I have**: something you need to find out or decide. It goes to *Questions I have*, to answer later.
   - **An idea I am thinking about**: something you might use, such as a scene, a twist or a line. It goes to *Things and ideas I'm thinking about*.
4. If you are in the Writing Room with a scene open, two more choices appear. **Attach to this scene** (ticked at first) ties the thought to the scene you are writing. Then choose **Resolve later**, or **I need this to keep writing** if it is blocking you.
5. Click **Save it**, or press **Ctrl+Enter**. **Cancel** or **Esc** closes the window without saving. Saving an empty box simply closes it.

Your words are kept exactly as written. For a few seconds after you save, a short line beside the **Capture** button says where the thought went, such as "Saved. It waits in Captured thoughts, in Story Brain under Questions and ideas." or "Saved. It is attached to this scene, in the scene plan." Then it fades.

Unsorted thoughts also wait on **Where was I**, under **Thoughts waiting to be sorted**. Everything you capture is in the Story Brain, under **Questions and ideas**.

- **Do most writers need it?** Yes. It works from every view of a novel, even during a sprint.
- **The common mistake** is stopping to sort the thought properly right then. That is how a ten-second note becomes a twenty-minute detour. Capture it and go back.
- **Example.** Writing "Port Ellery in the rain", you wonder whether Cyrus was harbor master when Eli drowned. You press **Ctrl+Shift+N**, type it, choose **A question I have**, leave **Attach to this scene** ticked, pick **Resolve later**, and press **Ctrl+Enter**. The line by **Capture** says it is attached to this scene. You keep writing.

## Handy keys for the whole program

Press the keys together. **Ctrl** is the key in the bottom-left corner of your keyboard.

| Keys | What they do | Where |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **F1** | Open Help | Anywhere, including the Library (not during a sprint) |
| **Ctrl+Shift+N** | Capture a thought | Anywhere in a novel, even during a sprint |
| **Ctrl+K** | Open Search | Anywhere in a novel (not during a sprint) |
| **Ctrl+Shift+W** | Return to Writing, to the scene you were in | Anywhere in a novel (not during a sprint) |
| **Ctrl+Shift+F** | Focus: hide everything but the page, and press again to come back | The Writing Room |
| **Esc** | Leave Focus, or close a small window such as Capture, or close Help in the Library | The Writing Room, any small window, the Library |
| **Ctrl+Shift+L** | Leave a note for later, \[\[like this\]\], and keep writing | On the page |
| **Ctrl+Enter** | Save what you typed in Capture | The Capture window |
| **Ctrl+Z** | Undo your last bit of typing | On the page |
| **Ctrl+Y** | Redo it | On the page |
| **Ctrl and +**, **Ctrl and -**, **Ctrl and 0** | Make everything bigger, smaller, or back to normal size | Anywhere |

To undo a change to your story itself (a decision, a deleted record), use **Story Trail**, where every change has its own **Undo**. See [Finding things: Search and Story Trail](#finding-things-search-and-story-trail).
