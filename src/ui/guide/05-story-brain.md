# The Story Brain

The **Story Brain** is where everything you know about your story lives: the people, the places, the clues, the questions you still have, the lines you want to use someday, and the big decisions that cover the whole book. Click the **Story Brain** tab on the right edge of the window to open it.

This chapter walks through the Story Brain screen by screen. It explains every list, button and label you will meet there, what each one does for your book, and what to do when something looks wrong but is working as designed. Characters and arcs have so much in them that they get their own chapter, [Characters and arcs](#characters-and-arcs). Every single blank on every kind of record is explained in the reference chapter, [Every kind and every field](#every-kind-and-every-field).

## How the Story Brain screen is laid out

The Story Brain has two parts.

- **The list on the left** is like the contents page of a book. It names every kind of thing your story holds, with a row of dots and a number after each one. The number is how many you have.
- **The page on the right** shows whatever you clicked in the list: a list of records, one record's page, or one of the three special lists.

Two words from the first chapter come up on every Story Brain screen. A **record** is one thing in your story with its own page, like an index card with a name across the top. A **fact** is one thing written in one blank of a record, with its status, its source and its date beside it. If those are new to you, read [Records and facts: the idea every screen rests on](#records-and-facts-the-idea-every-screen-rests-on) first.

A third word: a **kind** is the sort of record something is. Character is a kind. Place is a kind. Clue is a kind. The kind decides which blanks (the program calls them **fields**) a record offers. A character has a field for *Who they are*; a place has one for *What it is like*.

**Why it helps your book.** A novel holds hundreds of small decisions. The Story Brain keeps each one on the page where you will look for it, with how sure you were, so you stop hunting through notebooks and stop building on guesses you forgot were guesses.

**Story Canon remembers where you were.** If you leave the Story Brain and come back, it opens on the same list or record you had open.

## The left list in the Story Brain

The left list has four parts, from top to bottom: three special lists, the kinds your book uses, a **Show every kind** link, and a box for adding a kind of your own.

### The three special lists at the top

These three are not kinds of records. They gather things from across the whole Story Brain.

| In the list | What it gathers | Its number counts |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Questions and ideas** | Every question you have about the story, every idea you are turning over, and every thought you caught with Capture. | Your questions, ideas and captured thoughts together. |
| **Choices ahead** | A map of everything not yet decided: blanks marked still open, and blanks holding ideas in pencil with nothing chosen. | Those two together. Your open questions are shown on the page, but not counted here. |
| **Suggestions waiting** | Ideas someone else offered (the collaborator, or a chat or document you brought in) that you have not accepted or turned down. | Every suggestion waiting, from both places. |

Each one has its own section later in this chapter: [Questions and ideas: your open questions and possibilities](#questions-and-ideas-your-open-questions-and-possibilities), [Choices ahead: a map of what is still undecided](#choices-ahead-a-map-of-what-is-still-undecided) and [Suggestions waiting: ideas you have not taken yet](#suggestions-waiting-ideas-you-have-not-taken-yet).

### The kinds your book uses

Under a thin line come the kinds of records. Click one to see its list of records. The one you are on is shown in bold purple.

These kinds always appear, even with nothing in them yet, because almost every novel uses them:

| Kind | What one record holds |
| --- | --- |
| **Characters** | One person (or being): their role, identity, wants, wounds, voice, and how they change. See [Characters and arcs](#characters-and-arcs). |
| **Relationships** | What is between two or more characters: trust, power, closeness, and the private words they share. |
| **Storylines** | One thread of plot that runs through the book, such as the A story or the B story. |
| **Scene cards** | Your plan for one scene. Scene cards live on the Board in Narrative Zoom; each one also has a record here. |
| **Events** | Something that happens in the story world, on the page or offstage. |
| **Places** | One location: a kitchen, a harbor town, a whole city. |
| **Objects** | One thing that matters: a ring, a recipe card, a car. |
| **Clues** | A detail you plant that points to a hidden truth, and where it pays off. |
| **Themes** | An idea your story explores underneath what happens. |
| **Motifs** | An image, object, sound or phrase that comes back and gathers meaning each time. |
| **Kept lines** | A line of dialogue or prose you want to use but have not placed. |
| **Soundtrack** | One song that matters to the story. |

Other kinds join this part of the list on their own, as soon as your book has a record of that kind:

| Kind | When it shows up here |
| --- | --- |
| **Beats** | When your book has a beat sheet (such as Save the Cat). Each beat is one turning point. |
| **Notes** | When notes came in from your planning documents, or you made one. |
| **Story world** | When your book has its Story world record. See [The Story world record: the heart of the book](#the-story-world-record-the-heart-of-the-book). |
| **Secret operations** | When anyone in your book has a hidden move. See [Secret operations and hidden moves](#secret-operations-and-hidden-moves). |
| Fantasy and other worldbuilding kinds | When your book's genre or setting calls for them (see below). |
| Kinds you made yourself | Always, once you add them. |

**Worldbuilding kinds.** Kinds of beings, Magic and powers, Cultures and peoples, Factions and institutions, Beliefs and faiths, Languages, Sources and retellings, In-world documents and World history appear only when **Novel setup** says the book needs them: a speculative genre (fantasy, science fiction, paranormal, magical realism, dystopian, gothic, horror and similar), an invented world, our world with a hidden one inside it, the far future, or a retelling. A contemporary comedy never sees them.

### Show every kind and More kinds you can use

At the bottom of the kinds is a small link, **Show every kind**. Click it, and a heading in small capitals appears, **MORE KINDS YOU CAN USE**, with kinds that some books need and many don't:

| Kind | What it is for |
| --- | --- |
| **Acts** | Notes of your own about one act. Your book's acts themselves come from its beat sheet and show in Narrative Zoom, so this list can say 0 even when your book clearly has acts. That is working as designed. |
| **Sequences** | A run of scenes that works as one movement, such as a chase or a wedding day. |
| **Reveals** | The moment a hidden truth comes out, to a character, the reader, or both. |
| **Payoffs** | The later moment that rewards something set up earlier. |
| **Values** | Something a character or the story treats as precious: loyalty, safety, truth. |
| **Story rules** | Something that must always be true in your world: how magic works, what a law allows. |
| **Research** | Something you looked up: a fact, a source, a detail of a job or a place. |
| **Set pieces** | A big, memorable sequence readers will describe to friends. |

In a brand-new novel, **Beats**, **Notes**, **Story world** and **Secret operations** also wait here until the book has one of them.

Click **Show fewer kinds** to fold the extra kinds away again. Folding them away never hides a kind you have used: once a kind has a record, it moves up into the main part of the list for good.

**The common mistake** is thinking every kind is a box to fill. Most books use a handful. A kind that sits at 0 is not a gap in your planning.

### Add your own kind

If none of the kinds fits something your book keeps track of, make your own.

1. Find **Add your own kind** at the bottom of the left list.
2. Type a name in the box, in the singular. The box suggests *For example: Running gag*.
3. Click **Add kind**.

The new kind appears in the list, with its name made plural for you (*Running gags*), and its list page opens. Add your first record with the box at the bottom of that page.

**What a new kind's records look like.** A record of your own kind starts almost empty: a name, a **Notes** box, **Add your own field** and **Connections**. That is working as designed. The program does not guess what a running gag needs. Add the fields you want with **Add your own field**, choose **Every running gag**, and every record of that kind gets them. See [Adding your own fields](#adding-your-own-fields).

**Example.** A writer of a comic novel about rival florists adds a kind called *Running gag*. She gives it two fields of her own, *How it starts* and *How it escalates*, for every running gag. Her first record is "The delivery van that will not start." She connects it to the three scene cards where it appears.

There is no button to rename or remove a kind of your own once it is made, so choose the name with care.

### Why a count can look wrong

The numbers in the list are counts of records, and a few of them surprise people.

- **Beats says 21, but the Board shows 15.** Some beats have smaller steps inside them. In *I Declare War*, Setup has one step inside it and the Finale has five (Gathering the Team, Storming the Castle and so on). The count beside **Beats** includes those steps; the Board shows only the 15 beats. The Beats list page says this in a line at the top.
- **Choices ahead is in the hundreds.** It counts every blank marked still open and every blank with pencil ideas and nothing chosen, across the whole book. A big number early in planning is normal. It is a map, not a to-do list.
- **Suggestions waiting says 30, but a list says "None waiting."** The Suggestions waiting page has two parts. "None waiting" under **From the collaborator** can sit right above thirty suggestions under **From your planning documents**. Scroll down.
- **Story world says 1.** There is only ever one Story world record per book. Clicking **Story world** opens it straight away instead of showing a list of one.

## A kind's list page: every record of one kind

Click a kind in the left list, and its list page opens on the right. From top to bottom:

1. **The kind's name** as a large heading, such as *Characters* or *Kept lines*.
2. **A line about the kind, for some kinds.** Scene cards, Acts, Sequences, Secret operations, Notes and Beats get one sentence in a box that says where else this kind lives. For example, Scene cards say they live on the Board in Narrative Zoom, with an **Open Narrative Zoom** link.
3. **The About fold-out**, such as *About characters: what they are and how to use them*. Click the small triangle to fold it open or shut. It says what the kind is, why it matters, when to use it, the common mistake, and an example. **Read the full chapter in Help** at its end opens the reference chapter at that kind. Once you fold it shut, it stays shut on every page of that kind until you open it again.
4. **The records**, one per row. Each row has:
   - the record's name as a link (click it to open the record);
   - a marker in pink if the record is *Kept for you* (see [Kept for you: work only you will do](#kept-for-you-work-only-you-will-do));
   - on the right, in small gray type, how many facts on it are decided, developing and still open, such as *18 decided, 6 developing, 6 still open*;
   - under the name, a few telling facts in small type, such as *Narrative importance: main Role: Co-lead of the A story*. The label is gray and the fact is dark.
5. **Records inside records.** A record that belongs inside another of the same kind sits indented under it (the Finale's five steps under the Finale beat). A record that belongs to a record of another kind names its owner first: each hidden move in Secret operations starts with the name of the character who makes it.
6. **The add box** at the bottom: a text box that says *A name is enough to start*, and a button named for the kind, such as **Add character** or **Add kept line**.

### Adding a record, step by step

1. Click the kind in the left list.
2. Scroll to the bottom of the list page.
3. Type a name in the box. A name is enough. You can fill in everything else later, or never.
4. Click the button (**Add character**, **Add place** and so on).

The new record's page opens right away, ready for you.

**When to do it.** The moment something shows up in your head that you will want to find again. A minor character with only a name is a perfectly good record.

**The common mistake** is waiting until you know enough. You don't need to know anything but the name. A record with one line is still findable, and it can be connected to scenes.

**Adding a beat here.** On the Beats page, under the add box, a note says that a beat you add here joins the Board after the last beat, and that to change the beat sheet itself you use **My methods** (with an **Open My methods** link). Add a beat here only for a one-off turn in this book; change the beat sheet in My methods.

**Example.** While drafting, a writer of a lighthouse novel realizes the supply boat captain will matter later. She clicks **Characters**, types "Captain Orrin" in the box, and clicks **Add character**. That's all. Three weeks later she connects him to the storm scene.

## A record page from top to bottom

Click a record's name, and its page opens. Every kind of record uses the same page layout, so once you know one, you know them all. This section goes from the top of the page to the bottom.

If you opened a record from inside another one (an arc from its character, a hidden move from its owner), a small link at the very top takes you back: **Back to Kevin Wickley**.

### The title of a record

The record's name is the large heading at the top. It is also a box you can type in.

- **To rename a record:** click the name, change it, then click anywhere else on the page. The new name is kept the moment you click away.
- Renaming never breaks anything. Connections, scene cards and conversations point to the record itself, not to its old name.

### Kept for you: work only you will do

Under the title, on the left, is **Kept for you**, with a dropdown that says **Not marked** until you choose something.

**What it is.** A mark that says "this one is mine." The collaborator (the optional AI writing partner built into Story Canon; see [Who the collaborator is](#who-the-collaborator-is)) will not draft or settle what you keep. Its suggestions about that record wait, and nothing is decided for you.

**What the choices mean:**

| Choice | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Not marked** | No mark. The usual state. |
| ***Your name*, it's on you now** | Handed back to you. The collaborator offers nothing more here unless you ask. (Your first name comes from About you or Settings. Without a name it reads *It's on you now*.) |
| **Mine to write** | You write this one. The collaborator never drafts it. |
| **Mine to build** | You work out how it is built: the mechanics, the setup, how the pieces fit. |
| **Mine to design** | You design it: how it looks, the space, the shape of the gag or the trap. |
| **Mine to choreograph** | You stage the movement: who goes where, the fight, the chase, the physical comedy. |
| **Mine to time** | You set the timing: the pacing, when a reveal lands, the beat before the punchline. |
| **Mine to escalate** | You decide how it builds, and how big it gets before it breaks. |
| **Mine to settle** | You decide the open question here. Suggestions wait; nothing is settled for you. |

You can rename these markers, or add your own, in **Settings** (under the words for decisions, *Your other markers for work you keep*).

**Where the mark shows.** In pink beside the record's name in its kind's list, and in the **Kept for you** column of **Choices ahead**.

**When to use it.** For the parts of the book that are the reason you are writing it: the set piece you have pictured for years, the line only you can write, the ending.

**Example.** The florist writer marks her record for the wedding-disaster set piece **Mine to choreograph**. She will ask the collaborator about other things, but the staging of who falls into the cake is hers.

To clear the mark, choose **Not marked** again.

### Chips beside Kept for you

Some records show a small rounded label (a **chip**) on the same line:

- **Written by you, kept by you, placement open** on a kept line you saved from a conversation. It says who wrote the line, that you chose to keep it, and that it is not placed in a scene yet. If the collaborator wrote it, the chip names the collaborator instead of you.
- **Suggested by** *a name*, such as **Suggested by ChatGPT**, on a record an AI suggested. It is a reminder that the whole record started as someone else's idea.

### Ask about this

**Ask about this** is a link on the same line. Click it, and Story Canon opens **Conversations** with a new conversation titled *About* followed by the record's name, set up to talk the record through with the collaborator.

- **When to use it:** when you are stuck on this person, place or clue and want to think out loud.
- **What it does not do:** it does not change the record. Anything the collaborator suggests comes back as a suggestion, waiting for you.
- If you have not turned the collaborator on in Settings, the conversation opens but cannot get answers until you do.

### Where did this come from? for the whole record

On some records, a **Where did this come from?** link sits on the same line. It appears only when the whole record came from a conversation, such as a line you kept from a chat. Click it to see the conversation and the exact message. (Every single fact has its own **Where did this come from?** too; see [The margin beside each fact](#the-margin-beside-each-fact).)

### The legend: five ways a fact can look

Under that line is a row of five small samples in gray:

- ■ **Decided (inked)**
- – **Developing (pencil)**
- _ **Still open (a blank)**
- ❦ **Example, never canon**
- ▯ **Suggestion (a slip, tucked in)**

This is a key, like the legend on a map. Each fact on the page below wears one of these five looks, so you can tell at a glance what is settled and what is not. The legend is not clickable. The first chapter explains each status in full: [The five statuses: how sure you are](#the-five-statuses-how-sure-you-are).

If you renamed the status words in **Settings**, the legend uses your words.

### On this page: the jump list

On a record with four or more sections, a box headed **ON THIS PAGE** lists every section in order, as links. Click one to slide the page down to that section.

A character page can be twelve screens long. The jump list is how you get from *Role in the story* to *Connections* without scrolling past everything in between. Its order is the order of the page.

### About this kind: the fold-out on a record

Under the jump list is the same **About** fold-out you saw on the kind's list page: what this kind of record is, why it matters, when to use it, the common mistake, an example, and **Read the full chapter in Help**. Fold it shut once you know it; it stays shut on every record of that kind.

### Notes on a record

**Notes** is a large, plain box. Type anything: a hunch, a list, a paragraph you can't place yet.

- It saves when you click anywhere outside the box.
- Notes have no status. They are your scratch paper, so nothing in them counts as decided.
- Search finds words in Notes.

**When to use it.** For thinking that is not yet a fact. Once something becomes a real answer, put it in the field it belongs in, where it gets a status and a date.

### Suggestions waiting for you on a record

If the collaborator has suggested something for this record, a section headed **Suggestions waiting for you** appears under Notes. It works exactly like the **From the collaborator** part of the Suggestions waiting list: see [From the collaborator](#from-the-collaborator).

Suggestions that came in from your planning documents don't sit here. They sit in their own fields, as slips, right where they apply.

### Field groups, and why some are folded

The rest of the page is the record's fields, gathered under headings called **groups**. A character has groups such as *Role in the story*, *Identity* and *Appearance*. A relationship has *Where they stand*, *Underneath* and *Between them*.

**What you see under each group heading:** one line in gray saying what the group is for. Some headings are followed by a teacher's name in small type, such as **Goal and motive** *(Ingermanson)* or **Moral premise** *(Williams)*. That means the group comes from that writing teacher's method. The plain name says what the group is for; the small name says where it comes from. [The groups on a character page, and where each one comes from](#the-groups-on-a-character-page-and-where-each-one-comes-from) explains each one.

**The order of the groups.** Groups that already hold something come first, then the empty ones. On a character, *Role in the story* and *Identity* always lead, and *Other facts* always comes last.

**Folded groups.** An empty group folds down to one line, with a ▸ triangle, its name, and a count such as *8 places to write, all optional*. This is so a new record never looks like a questionnaire. To open it, click the group name. To fold an open empty group again, click its name beside the ▾ triangle.

**Why some empty groups stay open.** Three kinds of empty group are not folded:

- the main group of each kind (such as *Role in the story* on a character, *The line* on a kept line, *Where they stand* on a relationship), because that is where you would start;
- a group with only one or two fields (such as *Physical* or *Environmental* on a character), because folding it would save no space;
- a group holding a field of your own.

So on a new character, *Physical* stays open while *Appearance* is folded. That is working as designed, not a mistake.

**Groups that come and go with your methods.** Some groups appear only when you turn on a method in **Novel setup**, under **My storytelling toolkit**: for example, *Goal and motive (Ingermanson)* appears on characters when you choose *Snowflake (Ingermanson)*. If you turn a method off, its empty fields disappear, but anything you already wrote in them stays on the page. Nothing you write is ever hidden by a change in Novel setup.

### Reading one field

Each field has the same parts:

1. **Its name** in bold, such as **Who they are**. A field you made yourself says *(your field)* after its name, or *(your field, here only)* if it is on this record only. A private field says *(private, never exported)*.
2. **Add**, a link on the far right. Click it to write something in this field. It changes to **Close** while the writing box is open.
3. **A short explanation** in gray under the name: what the field is for.
4. **Why it matters**, a link at the end of that explanation. See [Why it matters and Not the same as its neighbors](#why-it-matters-and-not-the-same-as-its-neighbors).
5. **Not the same as its neighbors**, in purple, on fields that are easy to confuse with another field. It says how they differ.
6. **What is written there**, if anything, each entry with its margin on the right.

Some identity and appearance fields say **Not yet specified** when they are empty. That is a promise: the program never guesses a character's gender, race, age or looks from a name or from any other field. Until you write it, it is unspecified.

### Adding a fact, step by step

1. Find the field. Use the **ON THIS PAGE** list if the page is long, and open the group if it is folded.
2. Click **Add** on the right of the field's name. A writing box opens under the field.
3. Type your words. Some fields show small buttons of common answers under the box (for example *she/her*, *he/him*, *they/them* under Pronouns), followed by *or your own words*. Click one to fill the box, or ignore them and type anything.
4. Choose a status in the first dropdown under the box (see the next section). It starts on **Developing**.
5. If a second dropdown says **Timing**, choose when the fact is true, or leave it (see [Timing: when a fact is true](#timing-when-a-fact-is-true)).
6. Click **Save**.

The fact appears in the field with its margin. The writing box closes.

**The note beside Save** tells you what will happen:

- *Sits beside any other possibilities.* (when you save as Developing): your new idea joins any others in pencil. Nothing is replaced.
- *The earlier decision stays in history.* (when you save as Decided and the field already has a decision): the old decision is retired into the field's history, not erased.

**Save is grayed out** until you type something, unless you chose **Still open**, which can be saved with nothing written (see below).

**Example.** On her new character Rosa Delgado, a writer clicks **Add** beside *Who they are*, types "A baker who feeds the whole street and never lets anyone feed her," leaves the status on **Developing**, and clicks **Save**. The line appears in gray with a dashed underline: pencil.

### Choosing a status when you save

The status dropdown under the writing box has four choices:

| Choice | Choose it when |
| --- | --- |
| **Decided** | You have made up your mind. |
| **Developing** | You are still working on it. This is the default, because pencil is free. |
| **Still open** | You want to mark the field as a real question you have not answered yet. You can leave the box empty, or write the question itself ("Is the marriage solid at the start, or still healing?"). |
| **Example** | You are writing an illustration of what an answer could look like, not an answer. An example never counts as part of the story. |

There is no **Suggestion** choice. A suggestion is, by definition, an idea from someone else. You can't suggest something to yourself.

A field marked **Still open** with nothing written shows *Still open: nothing written yet* and a **Write it** button in its margin. Click **Write it** to open the writing box when you are ready.

**The common mistake** is saving as **Decided** because the page looks more finished. If you are not sure, leave it in pencil. A page of honest pencil is worth more than a page of false ink.

### Timing: when a fact is true

Some facts change over a story: a character's age, job, hair, pronouns, or where they live. Those fields have a second dropdown, **Timing**, with these choices:

- **Before the story** (backstory: true before page one)
- **True when the story opens**
- **Happens during the story**
- **Timing not decided**

Leave it on **Timing** to say nothing about when. The choice you make shows in the fact's margin.

**Why it helps.** A character can have short hair *when the story opens* and a shaved head *during the story* without the two facts arguing. You can keep both, decided, side by side.

**Example.** For the lighthouse keeper, *Work* holds "Assistant keeper" (*Before the story*) and "Head keeper" (*True when the story opens*). Both are decided. They don't contradict each other, because their timing differs.

### Why it matters and Not the same as its neighbors

**Why it matters** is a link at the end of a field's gray explanation. Click it to fold out:

- a label saying how many books need this field: **Most books need this**, **Useful for many books** or **Only for some books**;
- why the field helps a book;
- **The common mistake** people make with it;
- **Example**, often one that lands and one that fails.

Click **Less** to fold it away.

**Not the same as its neighbors.** On fields that sound alike, a line in purple says how this field differs from the one you might confuse it with. For example, under a clue's *Where it is planted*: this is a note in your words, while the Setups and payoffs check reads Connections. When you are unsure where a fact belongs, read this line.

### Earlier and set-aside versions

When a field has old versions, a link appears under it: **Show earlier and set-aside versions (2)**. The number is how many. Click it to see them, in small faded type, each with what happened to it and when:

- *(replaced, Oct 18)*: a decision that a newer decision took the place of.
- *(Set aside, Oct 18)*: something you retired with **Set aside**.

Beside a set-aside version is **Bring back**. Click it to restore it as Developing. Click **Hide earlier and set-aside versions** to fold the list away.

**Why it helps.** Nothing in Story Canon is silently deleted. If you liked last month's version of the Lie better, it is one click away.

**A thing to know.** Deciding one pencil version does not retire the others. If a field holds two ideas in pencil and you click **Decide this** on one, the other stays beside it in pencil, still a possibility. When you are done with it, click **Set aside** on it. This is on purpose: the program never throws away one of your ideas because you chose another.

### The margin beside each fact

To the right of every fact is its margin, a narrow column behind a thin vertical line. From top to bottom:

1. **The status**, in small capital letters with its mark: **■ DECIDED**, **DEVELOPING**, **STILL OPEN**, **EXAMPLE**, **SUGGESTION**.
2. **Where it came from**, in slanted purple type, when it did not come from you typing it:
   - *From* a document's name, such as *From I Declare War story data register* or *From Story Bible v5*: it came in with a document you brought in.
   - *Suggested by* a name, such as *Suggested by ChatGPT* or *Suggested by the collaborator*: an AI offered it.
   - *, accepted by you* added at the end: you accepted that suggestion.
3. **Its timing**, if it has one, such as *True when the story opens*.
4. **The date and time** it was written down. For a fact brought in from planning, this is when it was settled in your planning, not the day you brought the document in.
5. **What you can do with it**, as buttons. Only the buttons that make sense appear:

| Button | Appears on | What it does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Write it** | A blank marked still open | Opens the writing box for this field. |
| **Decide this** (filled purple) | Anything not yet decided | Makes it decided. If the field holds one answer, this replaces the earlier decision, which goes to the history. |
| **Not decided after all** | A decided fact | Moves it back to Developing. Nothing is lost. |
| **Set aside** | Anything not set aside | Retires it into **Show earlier and set-aside versions**. Not deleted. |
| **Where did this come from?** | Any fact with a source | Opens the source window (see below). |

**Why some facts have no Where did this come from? button.** A fact you typed yourself has no source to show: it came from you. Some imported facts also have no passage to point to. The button appears only where there is something to show.

### Where did this come from? for one fact

Click **Where did this come from?** in a fact's margin, and a window titled **Where this came from** opens over the page. What it shows depends on where the fact came from:

- **From a document:** the document's name and, after a comma, the row or place in it (such as *CK-003*). Two dates, explained in plain words: when the fact was settled in your planning, and when the document that carried it in was made. Then the document's own entry, laid out in rows:
  - **About**: what the entry is about (such as *Story role*);
  - **What it says**: the words;
  - **Status in your planning**: how sure you were in the document (shown in Story Canon's status words);
  - **Where your planning got it**: the reference numbers of its sources;
  - **How it changed**: its history in your planning (*Unchanged.* if it never changed).
- **The original passages:** under a slanted heading, the actual paragraphs of your Story Bible that the entry points to, each labeled with where it sits (*Paragraph 60 of your Story Bible, under Kevin Wickley*).
- **From a conversation:** under **In the conversation**, the conversation's title, which message it was, who said it (you, the collaborator, or another AI), when, and the message itself, word for word.
- **Typed by you:** *This was entered directly, not from a conversation or a document.*

Click **Close** (or press **Esc**) to return to the page.

**Why it helps.** Months from now you won't remember whether a line was your idea or ChatGPT's. One click tells you. A suggestion that sounds like you is still a suggestion.

### Reference numbers in imported facts

Facts brought in from planning documents can carry the documents' own reference numbers, such as *CK-003*, *SB-052*, *SB L60* or *T L16949*. Story Canon keeps your words exactly as you wrote them, so these stay. They are shown quietly, in small gray boxed type.

- **Click one** to see what it points to. A small note opens under the line: the reference, what it is (a paragraph of your Story Bible, a line of a transcript), and the passage itself when it is among the documents you brought in. Click **Close** in the note to fold it.
- Inside a link (such as a record name in a list), the numbers are shown but can't be clicked.

These numbers are not broken links. They are your planning's own footnotes, kept so nothing you wrote is changed.

### Delete at the bottom of a record

At the very bottom of every record is a small red **Delete** button. Click it, and Story Canon asks *Move "…" to Deleted? You can restore it from there.* Click **OK** to move the record to **Deleted**; click **Cancel** to keep it. A deleted record can be restored, with everything on it.

A beat has a different button instead: **Remove this beat from the novel**, with the note *Only if this book does not use it. Your scene cards stay.* Scene cards placed on a removed beat go back to *Not on a beat yet*.

**The common mistake** is deleting a record to "start over." Set aside the facts you no longer want instead; the record keeps its connections and its history.

## Suggestions waiting: ideas you have not taken yet

Click **Suggestions waiting** at the top of the left list.

**What a suggestion is.** An idea someone other than you offered: the collaborator, or a chat or document you brought in. Nothing here is part of your story until you accept it. The page says so at the top.

**Why it helps your book.** Saying "sure" to an AI in a chat does not settle anything. This page is where you decide on purpose, one idea at a time.

The page has two parts.

### From the collaborator

Ideas the collaborator offered during a conversation: a fact for a field, a new record, or a connection. Each one shows, in purple-edged slip style:

- the idea itself;
- under it, in small type: *Suggestion, for* the field it is meant for, and the collaborator's reason if it gave one; *(you said not yet)* if you put it off before.

The buttons:

| Button | What it does |
| --- | --- |
| **Accept as Decided** | Takes the idea into your story, decided. (Only for a fact.) |
| **Accept as Developing** | Takes the idea in, in pencil. For a new record or a connection, the button is just **Accept**. |
| **Not yet** | Keeps it waiting, marked *(you said not yet)*. It stays on this page. |
| **No** | Turns it down. It leaves this page. |

When there are none, the page says *None waiting.* That does not mean there are no suggestions at all. Check the next part.

### From your planning documents

Ideas that came in marked as suggestions ("Here's a suggestion") in a document or chat you brought in, such as ChatGPT's ideas in your planning conversations. Each one already sits on its record, in its field, as a slip. This list gathers them all in one place.

Each shows the idea, then a line such as *For Kevin Wickley, last page, from ChatGPT*. The record's name is a link: click it to see the slip in place. The buttons:

- **Accept as Decided**
- **Accept as Developing**
- **No, set it aside** (retires it into that field's set-aside versions, where **Bring back** can restore it)

**When to use this page.** After you bring in a chat or a planning document, and whenever the count in the left list grows. You don't have to clear it before you write.

**The common mistake** is accepting everything at once because it all sounds good. Each idea you accept becomes something you have to stay consistent with. Accept as Developing when you like an idea but haven't committed.

**Example.** After bringing in a brainstorm chat about the florist novel, a writer finds twelve slips. She accepts two as Developing, sets aside five, and leaves the rest for after she drafts the wedding chapter.

## Questions and ideas: your open questions and possibilities

Click **Questions and ideas** at the top of the left list. This page holds three kinds of things that are not facts yet, each in its own section. At the top, **ON THIS PAGE** lists them with counts: **Questions**, **Ideas**, **Captured thoughts** (and **Raised by the assistant** when an AI has raised questions).

**Why it helps.** "I haven't decided" is different from "I forgot." Writing a question down keeps it from nagging at you, and keeps it from quietly getting answered by accident.

### Questions I have

Questions about your story you haven't answered yet. Under the heading are three tabs, each with a count: **Open**, **Answered**, **No longer relevant**. Click a tab to see the questions in that state.

Each question shows its words (a link to its own page), the date you asked it, and buttons to move it to the other two states. For example, an open question has **Answered** and **No longer relevant**.

**Marking a question answered, step by step:**

1. Click **Answered** under the question. A box called **The answer** opens.
2. Type the answer if you like. It is optional.
3. Click **Mark answered**. If you wrote an answer, it is kept on the question as decided. The question moves to the **Answered** tab.

Click **Cancel** to close the box without changing anything.

Questions brought in from planning documents keep your own shorthand, such as "Kevin's Ingermanson sheet" or "her five-sentence spine." That is your planning's wording, kept as you wrote it.

### A question's own page

Click a question to open its page. It looks like any record page, with two additions.

**This question is**, at the top, with three buttons: **Open**, **Answered** and **No longer relevant**. The one that is true now is shown filled. Click another to change it. A note beside them says: *Write the answer under Answer below. Marking it Decided moves the question to Answered.*

**Thinking it through**, the question's group of fields:

- **Possible answers**: several can sit side by side in pencil while you think.
- **Assumptions behind it**: what you are taking for granted that might not be true. Often the real answer is hiding here.
- **Answer**: the answer, once you know it. When you **Decide this** on an answer, the question moves to **Answered** by itself.

**Why "Still open" may sit in the Answer field.** Questions brought in from planning often arrive with their Answer marked *Still open: nothing written yet* and a **Write it** button. That is not a decision that the question stays open. It means "no answer written yet." Click **Write it** when you have one.

**Example.** The lighthouse writer asks, "Why does Ines stay on the island after the storm?" Under *Possible answers* she writes "Guilt" and "She has nowhere else." Under *Assumptions behind it* she writes "That she could leave if she wanted to." That assumption turns out to be the answer: the supply boat won't take her. She writes it under *Answer*, decides it, and the question moves to Answered.

### Things and ideas I'm thinking about

Possibilities you are turning over but have not committed to: a twist, a setting, a scene. Tabs: **Considering**, **Adopted**, **Set aside**. Each idea's page has *What it might change* and *Where it might fit*.

**When to use it.** When an idea is bigger than one field. "What if the brother is alive?" touches ten records. Keep it here until you choose.

### Captured thoughts

Everything you caught with **Capture** (the button at the top right of every screen, or its keyboard shortcut) lands here. Tabs: **Not filed yet** and **Filed**. Each unfiled thought has two buttons: **Make it a question** and **Make it an idea**. A thought you never sort is still kept, and search still finds it.

### Questions an AI raised

When the collaborator, or a chat you brought in, raises a question, it waits here, apart from yours, under **Questions an AI raised**. These are not yours until you adopt them. Click **Make it one of my questions** to move one into Questions I have, or **Not relevant** to put it away.

## Choices ahead: a map of what is still undecided

Click **Choices ahead** in the left list. The page opens with a reminder: *Not a list to finish before you write. Many of these will answer themselves as you draft.*

**What it is.** One map of every place in your book that is not settled, gathered from every record. It has five columns:

| Column | What it lists |
| --- | --- |
| **Still open** | Every field you marked still open, with the record's name (a link), its kind in gray, the field, and any words you wrote. |
| **Possibilities, nothing chosen** | Every field holding ideas in pencil and no decision, with *(one idea so far)* or *(2 ideas side by side)*. |
| **Questions I have** | Your open questions, the same ones as in Questions and ideas, with an **Open Questions and ideas** link. |
| **Kept for you** | Every record you marked Kept for you, with its marker in pink. |
| **Suggestions waiting** | How many suggestions from the collaborator are waiting. (Slips from your planning documents are not counted here; see the Suggestions waiting page.) |

Each entry reads as the record's name, its kind, then the field. *Anthony's arc* *arc*, *Ghost* means the Ghost field on the arc record called "Anthony's arc." The word *arc* appears twice because the record's name and its kind are both shown.

**When to use it.** On a planning day, to choose what to think about next. Before drafting a section, to see what is undecided there. Never as homework.

**The common mistake** is trying to empty it. A first draft written with a hundred open choices is normal. Many choices are best made on the page.

**Example.** Before drafting the storm chapters, the lighthouse writer scans **Possibilities, nothing chosen** and sees *The storm*, *event*, *How it could happen (2 ideas side by side)*. She decides one, and starts writing.

## Connections between records

At the bottom of every record page is **Connections**: the links between this record and the rest of your story. A character *appears in* a scene card. A clue *is planted in* one scene and *pays off in* another. A song *is the theme song of* a character.

**Why it matters for your book.** Connections are what Story Canon's checks are built from. The Cast view knows who is in which scene because of *appears in*. The Setups and payoffs check knows where a clue is planted because of *is planted in*. Who knows what reads *knows*, *suspects* and *wrongly believes*. When you move a scene, Story Canon warns you if the move would put an effect before its cause, because of *causes*. The more you connect, the more the program can catch.

### Reading the Connections section

Under the **Connections** heading, a gray line says *Each heading reads from here outward.* Your connections are gathered under headings by what they say, so fifteen *appears in* links become one heading with fifteen names under it:

> **Kevin Wickley appears in**
> The five-dollar bill *scene card*
> Fish-guts chain reaction *scene card*

Each row has the other record's name (a link; click to open it), its kind in gray, sometimes an **inferred** chip, and a **Remove** button that deletes that one connection (not the record).

Two special headings:

- **Kevin Wickley is in these relationships**: on a character, the relationship records that name them.
- **The people in it**: on a relationship, its characters.

*None yet.* means this record has no connections.

### Making a connection, step by step

At the end of the section is a dashed box headed **Make a connection**. It reads like a sentence, left to right.

1. On the left is this record's name in bold, the start of the sentence.
2. In the first dropdown, choose the connection word. They are grouped under gray headings (*Cause and effect*, *Who knows what* and so on).
3. In the second dropdown, choose the other record. Records are grouped by kind (Beats, Characters, Scene cards…) and listed in name order within each kind.
4. Read the line under the dropdowns, **Reads:**, which shows the whole sentence in bold, such as **Rosa Delgado appears in Block-party pie**, and under it one line explaining the word. If the sentence reads wrong, change the word.
5. Click **Connect**.

The new connection appears in the list above.

**The Tie it to a scene button.** On a kept line, a gray note says *Keeping a line does not place it* with a **Tie it to a scene** button. Click it, and the connection box scrolls into view with *appears in* already chosen. Pick the scene card and click **Connect**.

### Which way a connection reads

Every connection has a direction. "The storm causes the shipwreck" is not the same as "the shipwreck causes the storm."

Story Canon stores each connection once and reads it the right way from both ends. That is why most words appear twice in the dropdown: once forward (*causes*) and once backward (*is caused by*). Pick whichever makes a true sentence starting with **this** record's name.

**Example.** You are on the place record *Miss Francine's garden* and want to say the opening scene happens there. Choose **is where … happens** and pick *Opening image scene*. The line reads **Miss Francine's garden is where Opening image scene happens**. On the scene card's page, the same connection reads *Opening image scene happens at Miss Francine's garden*.

**The common mistake** is choosing the forward word from the wrong record, which makes a backward sentence ("the garden happens at the opening scene"). Always read the **Reads:** line before you click **Connect**. If you already made a backward one, click **Remove** beside it and make it again.

A few words read the same both ways (*relates to*, *is the same person as*) and appear once.

### The inferred chip

Some connections carry a small gray chip that says **inferred**. ## Guardrails: rules that must stay true

**What a guardrail is.** A firm rule about your story, written down so it can't be broken by accident: "Nobody gets seriously hurt." "Francine is never a villain." "No slow leak of Francine's thoughts before the reveal."

**Where they are.** Each character page has a **Guardrails** section, after the arcs: *What must stay true about this character. The collaborator checks every suggestion against these.* Each guardrail is a line in ink with a **Remove** button.

**Adding a guardrail, step by step:**

1. Open the character's page and click **Guardrails** in **ON THIS PAGE**.
2. Type the rule in the box that says **Add a guardrail**.
3. Click **Add**.

**Why it helps your book.** It's easy to forget a rule you made three months ago, and easy for an eager collaborator to suggest exactly the thing you ruled out. The collaborator reads your guardrails before it answers, and if an idea would break one, it says which.

**The common mistake** is using guardrails for preferences. A guardrail is a hard line, not a taste. "I'd like her to be funny" is a preference; put it in her fields. "She is never cruel to children" is a guardrail.

**Example.** The florist writer adds to her heroine Delphine: "Delphine never sabotages Marisol's flowers. The rivalry is about pride, not harm."

**Guardrails for the whole book.** Rules about the whole story, rather than one character, came in with *I Declare War* (such as the brawl rule). These belong to the Story world and are read by the collaborator, but at present the Story world page has no Guardrails section to show or edit them. You will see them by name in the **Make a connection** list and in search. To add a book-wide rule of your own today, put it on the main character it most concerns, or write it in the Story world's Notes.

## Kept lines: lines waiting for a scene

**What a kept line is.** A line of dialogue or prose you want to use somewhere in the book but haven't placed. Good lines arrive at odd times: in the shower, while drafting a different chapter, in the middle of a chat.

**Where they are.** **Kept lines** in the left list. Each row shows the line and, under it, *Said by (in the story)* if you filled it in. You can also keep a line straight from a conversation, which makes a record here with the chip **Written by you, kept by you, placement open** (or written by the collaborator).

**A kept line's page** has two groups:

- **The line**: *Said by (in the story)* and *How it is delivered* (emphasis, timing, the small action a speaker does while talking).
- **Where it goes**: *Where it might go*, notes on possible places, which can sit side by side in pencil.

Under them is the gray note **Keeping a line does not place it** with the **Tie it to a scene** button.

**Placing a kept line, step by step:**

1. Open the kept line.
2. Click **Tie it to a scene**. The connection box comes into view with **appears in** already chosen.
3. Choose the scene card in the second dropdown.
4. Click **Connect**.

The line now shows on that scene card's page, in the Story Brain (under *… has in it*) and in Narrative Zoom, so you find it when you plan that scene. Placing it in your prose is still your job: you type it into the manuscript where it lands best.

**The common mistake** is forcing a kept line into a scene because you love it. A line that fits nowhere in this book can wait for the next one.

**Example.** A writer keeps "You can't fix a recipe by shouting at the oven." She ties it to two scene cards, the mother's flashback and the last chapter, then decides in revision to use it in both.

## Secret operations and hidden moves

**What it is.** A **hidden move** is something a character does in secret, offstage, that the reader does not see yet: a poisoned cup, a forged letter, fish guts dumped in a neighbor's yard during a distraction. **Secret operations** is the list of all of them: the hidden story running under the visible one, in its true order. Only you see it. It never appears in viewpoint summaries.

**Why it helps your book.** When someone acts in secret, the visible story has causes the reader can feel but not see. If you plan the secret moves alongside the visible beats, the reveal holds up when readers look back. If you invent them after the reveal, readers can tell.

**When to use it.** When your book has an antagonist, ally or secret keeper acting out of sight. Mysteries, thrillers, and comedies of misdirection (like *I Declare War*, where Miss Francine is quietly running the whole war) need it most.

**Where it is.** Two places show the same moves:

- **Story Brain > Secret operations**: each move is a record. The list shows whose move it is first, then *When (beat or rough point)*.
- **Narrative Zoom > Who knows what**: under **Secret operations (author only)**, the moves are grouped by character in a table: *When*, *The hidden move*, *What the others see*, *Who takes the blame*.

**A hidden move's page** has one group, **The move**:

- **When (beat or rough point)**: such as *Fun and Games* or *about 10%*.
- **The hidden move**: what the character actually does.
- **What the others see, and how they misread it**: what is visible, and the wrong conclusion people draw.
- **Who takes the blame**: who gets blamed until the truth comes out.

**Logging a hidden move, step by step:**

1. Open **Narrative Zoom** and the **Who knows what** view.
2. At the bottom, find **Log a hidden move**.
3. In **Whose?**, choose the character who makes the move.
4. Type what they secretly do in the box.
5. Click **Add**. The move is filed under that character, in both places.
6. Open it in the Story Brain to fill in when, what the others see, and who takes the blame.

**Adding one from the Story Brain** works too (type a name in the box at the bottom of the Secret operations list and click **Add hidden move**), but a move made there belongs to no one. It shows as *Unassigned* in Narrative Zoom, and the record page has no way to choose its owner. Use **Log a hidden move** in Narrative Zoom when you know whose it is.

**The Story world's question.** The Story world record asks *Does this book hide a truth from the reader?* Answering it records your intent. The Secret operations list and the Who knows what view are there whether you answer it or not.

**Example.** In a lighthouse mystery, the harbor master's hidden moves: he cuts the radio wire (*When: before the Catalyst*), the town sees a storm-damaged radio and blames the weather (*Who takes the blame: no one*); he moves the supply boat's schedule (*What the others see: an ordinary delay*). At the reveal, every one of those small, ordinary moments makes sense.

## The Story world record: the heart of the book

**What it is.** The **Story world** record holds the decisions that cover the whole book, rather than one person or place: the book in one sentence and one paragraph, what it means (its moral premise and controlling idea), when it happens and how long it lasts, and how it is told (viewpoint, person, tense). There is only one per book.

**Why it matters for your book.** Every scene leans on these. Viewpoint and tense shape every sentence. The one sentence and the controlling idea keep the book pointed at one meaning. When they are vague, chapters drift, and the ending feels like it belongs to a different book.

**When to use it.** Fill in what you can at the very start, even as guesses in pencil. Come back when a scene feels aimless, or when someone asks what your book is about and you want to answer in one breath.

**The common mistake** is treating it as paperwork to finish once. These answers change as you write. Keep older versions in pencil and decide one only when you're sure.

### Finding the Story world

- In the left list, click **Story world**. Because a book has only one, it opens the record straight away.
- From **Novel setup** (Settings, then **Open novel setup**), click **Open the story world** under *The heart of the book*.
- In a brand-new novel, **Story world** may not be in the main list yet. It is created the first time you write the book's one sentence (when you start the novel, in Novel setup, or with **Plant the seed**). Until then it waits under **Show every kind**. Start it from Novel setup rather than with the add box, so the book has exactly one.

The Story world is a long record. Use **ON THIS PAGE** to move around it.

### The heart of the book group

The first group is **The heart of the book** *(Ingermanson, the Snowflake)*. Its first two fields come from Randy Ingermanson's Snowflake Method, which grows a novel outward from a single sentence the way a snowflake grows from a speck.

- **The one sentence.** The whole book in about twenty-five words. Ingermanson starts here. Blake Snyder (*Save the Cat*) adds that a strong one-line pitch, a **logline**, carries **irony**: a built-in contradiction that makes a stranger want to hear more. *"A lighthouse keeper who has saved a hundred strangers can't save the one person she lets in"* has irony. *"A lighthouse keeper faces challenges"* does not.
- **The one paragraph.** Five sentences: the setup, three disasters, and the ending. A **disaster** here is a turning point that makes things much worse and forces a new choice. This is the Snowflake's second step: the sentence grows into the shape of the book.
- **How it might end.** Your best current guess. Endings found early give the middle somewhere to go. A guess in pencil is fine.

**Example.** The florist writer's one sentence, in pencil: "Two rival florists fight over the cathedral wedding contract, until the bride turns out to be the daughter one of them gave up." Her paragraph: Delphine and Marisol run competing shops (setup); Marisol steals the contract (disaster one); the cathedral flowers wilt on the eve of the wedding (disaster two); Delphine learns who the bride is (disaster three); the two make the bouquet together (ending).

### Moral premise and controlling idea

Two groups put what the book *means* into one sentence. They come from two different teachers and appear when you turn those methods on in Novel setup.

**Moral premise** *(Williams)*, from Stanley D. Williams's book *The Moral Premise*. He argues that a story that works argues one truth, which can be written as an equation:

> [A vice] leads to [defeat], but [a virtue] leads to [success].

The group has three fields: **The vice**, **The virtue**, and **The moral premise, as one sentence**. Fill in the vice and virtue first; they are usually opposites. Then write the sentence.

- **Why it helps:** once you have it, every subplot can argue the same truth from a different side. The villain lives the vice. The hero starts out living it too, and learns the virtue.
- **The common mistake:** choosing a vice and virtue that aren't opposites ("greed" and "courage"), so the book argues two things.
- **Example:** "Pride leads to a lonely, ruined shop, but generosity leads to a full house." In the florist novel, Marisol's pride costs her the contract; Delphine's gift of her best customer wins her a partner.

**Controlling idea** *(McKee)*, from Robert McKee's *Story*. It is the meaning of the story in one sentence, built from two parts: the **value** at the end (a value is something characters care about that can swing positive or negative: love, justice, safety, respect) and the **cause** that brought it there.

- **Why it helps:** it is a test for every scene. Does this scene move the value, and is it moved by that cause?
- **The common mistake:** writing a topic ("this book is about grief") instead of value plus cause.
- **Example:** "Respect returns to the street when the neighbors finally see the woman they overlooked." Value: respect. Cause: being seen.

These two can sit together. The moral premise is the argument; the controlling idea is the verdict.

### Time and the story clock

The **Time** group anchors the calendar so dates, seasons and ages stay consistent:

- **Era or year**: when the story takes place.
- **Season**: the season it mostly happens in, or opens with.
- **The atmosphere now**: the mood of the story's world when the book opens, what everyone is feeling or fearing.
- **How long the story lasts**: a weekend, a season, a year, a lifetime.
- **Name of your story clock**: a name for the count you use to say when scenes happen, such as *day of the feud* or *days since the storm*.

**What a story clock is.** Many books count time in their own way: "day 3 of the feud," "week 2 at sea." Naming your clock here keeps you using one count everywhere. Each scene card has a field called *Day in the story* where you write the number on that clock. The name is for you; the program does not calculate with it.

### Viewpoint settings for the whole book

The **Viewpoint** group decides how every sentence is told. **Viewpoint** (or point of view) means the character whose eyes and thoughts the reader is inside.

- **Viewpoint approach**: your overall plan, such as "alternating first person between Kevin and Anthony."
- **Viewpoint rule**: when the viewpoint may change. Write it in plain words, such as "One head per scene" or "One viewpoint per chapter."
- **Default narrative person**: first ("I"), second ("you") or third ("she," "he," "they").
- **Default tense**: past ("she ran") or present ("she runs").

**How the rest of the program uses these.** The Viewpoint view in Narrative Zoom shows your default person and tense on every scene that doesn't set its own. The Story review reads your viewpoint rule: if your words say *scene*, it asks about any scene that lists more than one viewpoint; if they say *chapter*, it asks about any chapter whose scenes have different viewpoints. So write the rule with the word *scene* or *chapter* in it if you want it checked.

**The common mistake** is head-hopping: drifting from one character's thoughts into another's in the middle of a scene. A clear rule here, and the review that reads it, catch it.

### Knowledge: does the book hide a truth?

The **Knowledge** group has one field: **Does this book hide a truth from the reader?**, with **Yes** and **No** buttons under the writing box. Say yes if the book keeps a secret from the reader on purpose, such as a hidden culprit or a narrator who isn't telling everything. It records your intent; see [Secret operations and hidden moves](#secret-operations-and-hidden-moves) for the tools that track the secret.

### Story types and their ingredients

If you choose one or more of Blake Snyder's story types in Novel setup (such as *Buddy Love* or *Monster in the House*), each one adds a group to the Story world named for it, with its three ingredients as fields. Buddy Love, for example, asks for *An incomplete hero*, *A counterpart* and *A complication*.

### Groups from your planning

A book you bring in can carry groups of its own onto the Story world. *I Declare War* has one called **From my planning**, with fields such as *Premise, theme and tone (your field)*, *Setting and timelines (your field)*, *Viewpoint rules (your field)* and *Who knows what, and when (your field)*. These are fields made from your planning documents. They work like any field of your own. The *Who knows what, and when* field there is your planning's own notes; it is separate from the **Who knows what** view in Narrative Zoom, which is built from connections.

### The Story world and Novel setup

**The heart of the book** appears in two places: on the Story world record, and at the top of **Novel setup**. They are the same place. Novel setup says so: *The one sentence and the one paragraph live in the story world. Change them here or there; it is the same place.*

- Type the one sentence in Novel setup, and it appears on the Story world as a fact in pencil.
- If the Story world's one sentence is already decided, a new wording typed in Novel setup stays decided, and the earlier wording goes to the field's history.
- The status of each is shown beside its label in Novel setup.
- The one sentence you type when you start a novel in the Library becomes this same sentence.

Novel setup also decides which groups the Story world shows: *Moral premise* appears when you choose *The Moral Premise (Williams)* in **My storytelling toolkit**, *Controlling idea* with *McKee (Story)*, and a story type's ingredients when you choose that story type.

## Adding your own fields

If a record doesn't have a blank you want, add one. At the bottom of every record, above Connections, is **Add your own field**.

### Just this one or every one

1. Type the field's name in the box that says *For example: Weather*.
2. Choose where it goes:
   - **Just this** *kind* (such as **Just this character**): the field appears on this record only. It shows *(your field, here only)* after its name.
   - **Every** *kind* (such as **Every character**): the field appears on every record of this kind in this novel, including ones you add later. It shows *(your field)*. This is chosen for you to start.
3. If the field should hold several separate entries, tick **A list, with several entries** (see below).
4. Click **Add field**.

The field appears in a group called **My fields** on the record, and in **ON THIS PAGE**.

**When to use each.** Choose **Every** when the field is a question you want to ask of every character (or place, or clue) in this book: a comedy writer might add *Running gag* to every character. Choose **Just this** for a detail only one record needs: *Signature bake* for the one baker in the book.

**The common mistake** is adding a field that already exists under another name. Before you add *Fears*, check the folded groups: *Psychological* already has it. Fields you add can't be removed from the page yet, so look first.

### A list, with several entries

A list field holds several separate entries that all count at once, each with its own status and margin. Without the tick, a field holds one answer: deciding a new one replaces the old decision.

- **A list** fits things a character has many of: favorite insults, scars, places they have lived.
- **One answer** fits things with one true value at a time: a nickname everyone uses, the town they were born in.

**Example.** The florist writer adds *Favorite insult* to **Every character**, as a list. Delphine's page gets three entries, all decided: "Carnation person," "You arrange like a hedge," and "Bless your filler greens."

## Story Brain questions people ask

**Something I wrote moved up the page.** Groups that hold something come before empty ones, so the first fact you write in a group moves that whole group up, above the empty groups. Use **ON THIS PAGE** to find it.

**A field I filled in is gone after I changed Novel setup.** It isn't gone. Fields that came with a method stay on the page as long as they hold something. If an empty field disappeared, that method is switched off; switch it back on to see its empty fields again.

**Two answers in pencil are both still there after I decided one.** That's on purpose. Deciding one never throws the other away. Click **Set aside** on the one you no longer want.

**The date in a fact's margin is days before I brought the book in.** For imported facts, the date is when the fact was settled in your planning. **Where did this come from?** shows both dates.

**A count in the left list doesn't match another screen.** See [Why a count can look wrong](#why-a-count-can-look-wrong).

**There's no Scenes kind.** Scenes live in the Writing Room (as prose) and on the Board (as scene cards). Each scene card has a record under **Scene cards**.

**My Secret operations list has moves that say nothing about whose they are.** They were added from the Story Brain list. Log moves from Narrative Zoom's **Who knows what** to file them under a character.
