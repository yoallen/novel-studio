# The Story Brain

The Story Brain is where everything you know about your story lives: the people, the places, the clues, the questions you still have, the lines you want to use someday, and the big decisions that cover the whole book. This chapter walks through it in the order you will meet it: what it is and when to use it, the list down its left side, one record page from top to bottom, the six statuses as they look and behave on a record, your own fields, guardrails and connections, then characters and their arcs in depth, every other kind of record, and the three special lists (Questions and ideas, Suggestions waiting and Choices ahead). A short reference section at the end holds details you can look up when you need them: every connection word, Robert McKee's value ladder, and foils, doubles and mirrors. Read it the first time you open the Story Brain, and come back to the part you need whenever a screen there puzzles you. Every single field on every kind of record is described in the reference chapter, [Every kind and every field](#every-kind-and-every-field).

## What the Story Brain is and when to use it

**What it is.** The **Story Brain** is the view that holds your novel's records. Each record is one thing in your story (a person, a place, a clue, a song) with its own page of fields. When you write something in a field, it becomes a fact, with a status that says how sure you are, a date, and a note of where it came from.

**Why it matters.** A novel holds hundreds of small decisions. The Story Brain keeps each one on the page where you will look for it, marked with how sure you were, so you stop hunting through notebooks and stop building on guesses you forgot were guesses. It is also what the rest of Story Canon reads. The collaborator reads it before it answers you, and the checks you will meet later in [Narrative Zoom](#narrative-zoom) and [Catching problems: the continuity checks](#catching-problems-the-continuity-checks) are built from the records and the connections between them.

**Do most writers need it?** Yes, but only a little at a time. You never have to fill it. A writer who keeps five characters, two places and a handful of questions here is using it well. Some writers plan in it for weeks before they draft; others write first and only come here to jot down what they discovered. Both work.

**When to use it.**

- When someone or something new shows up in your head and you will want to find it again.
- When you make a decision you do not want to forget ("Marisol has never once left the island").
- When you are stuck on a person or a place and want to think it through on paper.
- When you want to see what is still undecided before you draft a section.

**How to open it.** Click **Story Brain** in the list of views on the right edge of the window.

**Story Canon remembers where you were.** If you leave the Story Brain and come back, it opens on the same list or record you had open, and a record opens on the same page you were reading.

### Story Brain: how the screen is laid out

The Story Brain has two parts.

- **The list on the left** works like the contents page of a book. At the top are three special lists that gather things from across the whole book. Under them are the kinds of record your book uses, each with a number after it: how many records of that kind you have.
- **The page on the right** shows whatever you clicked in the list: a list of records of one kind, one record's page, or one of the three special lists.

**The example in this chapter.** Every example here comes from one imaginary novel, *The Keeper of Gull Point*, a mystery with a heart. Marisol Vega, 41, keeps the Gull Point lighthouse and has never left the island. Her younger brother Eli drowned twelve years ago. One morning an empty boat washes onto her rocks, and the story begins. Ana Ruiz is the new doctor in the harbor town of Port Ellery; Cyrus Hale is the harbor master, who knows more than he says.

## The left list in the Story Brain

The left list has four parts, from top to bottom: the three special lists, the kinds your book uses, a **Show every kind** link, and a box for adding a kind of your own.

### The three gathering lists at the top of the Story Brain

These three are not kinds of records. They gather things from every record in the book.

| In the list | What it gathers | Its number counts |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Questions and ideas** | Every question you have about the story, every idea you are turning over, and every thought you caught with Capture. | Your questions, ideas and captured thoughts together, in every state (open and answered alike). |
| **Choices ahead** | A map of everything not yet decided: fields marked **Still open**, and fields holding ideas in pencil with nothing chosen. | Those two together. Your open questions have a page of their own there but are not counted here. |
| **Suggestions waiting** | Ideas someone else offered (the collaborator, or a chat or document you brought in) that you have not accepted or turned down. | Every suggestion waiting, from both places. |

Each has its own section at the end of this chapter: [Questions and ideas: your open questions and possibilities](#questions-and-ideas-your-open-questions-and-possibilities), [Suggestions waiting: ideas you have not taken yet](#suggestions-waiting-ideas-you-have-not-taken-yet) and [Choices ahead: a map of what is still undecided](#choices-ahead-a-map-of-what-is-still-undecided).

### The kinds your book uses

Under a thin line come the kinds of records. Click one to see its list of records. The one you are on is highlighted.

These kinds always appear, even with nothing in them yet, because almost every novel uses them:

| Kind | What one record holds |
| --- | --- |
| **Characters** | One person (or being): their job in the book, who they are, how they look and sound, what they want, what wounds them, and how they change. See [Characters in depth: a character's pages](#characters-in-depth-a-characters-pages). |
| **Relationships** | What is between two or more characters: trust, power, closeness, and the private words they share. |
| **Storylines** | One thread of plot that runs through the book, such as the A story (the main plot) or the B story (the second thread, often a relationship that carries the theme). |
| **Scene cards** | Your plan for one scene. Scene cards are laid out on the Board, the planning space in Narrative Zoom; each one also has a record here. |
| **Events** | Something that happens in the story world, on the page or offstage. |
| **Places** | One location: a kitchen, a lighthouse, a whole harbor town. |
| **Objects** | One thing that matters: a compass, a key, a boat. |
| **Clues** | A detail you plant that points to a hidden truth, and where it pays off. |
| **Themes** | An idea your story explores underneath what happens. |
| **Motifs** | An image, object, sound or phrase that comes back and gathers meaning each time. |
| **Kept lines** | A line of dialogue or prose you want to use but have not placed. |
| **Soundtrack** | One song that matters to the story. |

Other kinds join this part of the list on their own, as soon as your book has a record of that kind:

| Kind | When it shows up here |
| --- | --- |
| **Beats** | When your book has a beat sheet. A **beat** is one turning point the story must reach, and a **beat sheet** is a writing method's list of them in order (Save the Cat, for example, has fifteen). Where to choose one: [Choosing a beat sheet for a novel](#choosing-a-beat-sheet-for-a-novel). |
| **Notes** | When notes came in from your planning documents, or you made one. |
| **Story world** | When your book has its Story world record, the one record that holds decisions about the whole book. See [The Story world record: the heart of the book](#the-story-world-record-the-heart-of-the-book). |
| **Secret operations** | When anyone in your book has a hidden move. See [Secret operations and hidden moves](#secret-operations-and-hidden-moves). |
| **Guardrails** | When your book has a guardrail, a rule that must stay true. See [Guardrails: rules that must stay true](#guardrails-rules-that-must-stay-true). |
| Any other kind | Once it has a record. |
| Worldbuilding kinds | When your book's genre or setting calls for them (see below). |
| Kinds you made yourself | Always, once you add them. |

**Worldbuilding kinds.** **Kinds of beings**, **Magic and powers**, **Cultures and peoples**, **Factions and institutions**, **Beliefs and faiths**, **Languages**, **Sources and retellings**, **In-world documents** and **World history** appear only when Novel setup says the book needs them. **Novel setup** is the page where you tell Story Canon what kind of book you are writing (open **Settings**, then click **Open novel setup**). The worldbuilding kinds appear when you choose a genre such as fantasy, science fiction, urban fantasy, paranormal, paranormal romance, magical realism, dystopian, gothic or horror, or a setting such as an invented world, our world with a hidden one inside it, or the far future, or when you say the book is a retelling. A quiet mystery like *The Keeper of Gull Point* never sees them. Each one is described in [Worldbuilding kinds](#worldbuilding-kinds-of-records).

### Show every kind and More kinds you can use

At the bottom of the kinds is a small link, **Show every kind**. Click it, and a heading appears, **More kinds you can use**, with kinds that some books need and many don't:

| Kind | What it is for |
| --- | --- |
| **Acts** | Notes of your own about one act (one of the large parts a story is divided into, such as the beginning, middle and end). Your book's acts themselves come from its beat sheet and show in Narrative Zoom, so this list can say 0 even when your book clearly has acts. That is working as designed. |
| **Sequences** | A run of scenes that works as one movement, such as a chase or a wedding day. |
| **Reveals** | The moment a hidden truth comes out, to a character, the reader, or both. |
| **Payoffs** | The later moment that rewards something set up earlier. |
| **Values** | Something a character or the story treats as precious: loyalty, safety, truth. |
| **Story rules** | Something that must always be true in your world: how the tides work at Gull Point, what a law allows. |
| **Research** | Something you looked up: a fact, a source, a detail of a job or a place. |
| **Set pieces** | A big, memorable sequence readers will describe to friends. |

In a brand-new novel, **Beats**, **Notes**, **Story world**, **Secret operations** and **Guardrails** also wait here until the book has one of them.

Click **Show fewer kinds** to fold the extra kinds away again. Folding them away never hides a kind you have used: once a kind has a record, it moves up into the main part of the list for good.

**The common mistake** is thinking every kind is a box to fill. Most books use a handful. A kind that sits at 0 is not a gap in your planning.

### Add your own kind

**What it is.** A kind of record you invent, for something your book keeps track of that none of the built-in kinds fits.

**Do most writers need it?** No. Most never do. It is there for the book that has its own recurring thing: a running gag, a recipe, a spell, a superstition.

**How to do it.**

1. Find **Add your own kind** at the bottom of the left list.
2. Type a name in the box, in the singular. The box suggests *For example: Running gag*.
3. Click **Add kind**.

The new kind appears in the list with an "s" added to make it plural (*Running gags*), and its list page opens. Add your first record with the box at the bottom of that page.

**What a new kind's records look like.** A record of your own kind starts almost empty: a name, a **Notes** box, **Connections**, and **Add your own field**. That is working as designed. The program does not guess what a superstition needs. Add the fields you want with **Add your own field**, choose **Every** followed by the kind's name, and every record of that kind gets them. See [Adding your own fields](#adding-your-own-fields).

**The common mistake** is choosing a name whose plural is not just "add an s". The program adds an "s", so *Ferry* would become *Ferrys*. Choose a name that takes a plain "s" (*Ferry crossing* becomes *Ferry crossings*). There is no button to rename or remove a kind of your own once it is made, so choose the name with care.

**Example.** The islanders of Gull Point live by old sea superstitions, and they matter to the mystery. Marisol's writer adds a kind called *Superstition*. She gives it two fields of her own, *Who believes it* and *When it seems to come true*, for every superstition. Her first record is "A boat that comes in empty brings the drowned home." She connects it to the scene card where the empty boat arrives.

### Why a count in the left list can look wrong

The numbers in the list are counts of records, and a few of them surprise people.

- **Beats says 21, but the Board shows 15.** Some beats have smaller steps inside them (the Finale of Save the Cat has five). The count beside **Beats** includes those steps; the Board shows only the beats. When this happens, the Beats list page says so in a line at the top.
- **Choices ahead is in the hundreds.** It counts every field marked **Still open** and every field with pencil ideas and nothing chosen, across the whole book. A big number early in planning is normal. It is a map, not a to-do list.
- **Suggestions waiting says 30, but the page says "None waiting."** Suggestions waiting has two pages. "None waiting." on **From the collaborator** can mean all thirty are on the other page. Look at the page tabs: **From your planning documents (30)** says where they are. Click it.
- **Questions and ideas counts answered questions too.** Its number in the left list counts every question, idea and captured thought, whatever state it is in. The page tabs inside it count less: each tab counts only the first state on its page (**Open** questions, ideas you are **Considering**, thoughts **Not filed yet**).
- **Story world says 1.** There is only ever one Story world record per book. Clicking **Story world** opens it straight away instead of showing a list of one.

## A kind's list page: every record of one kind

Click a kind in the left list, and its list page opens on the right. From top to bottom:

1. **The kind's name** as a large heading, such as *Characters* or *Kept lines*.
2. **A line about the kind, for some kinds.** Scene cards, Acts, Sequences, Secret operations and Notes get a sentence in a box that says what the kind is or where else it lives. Scene cards, for example, say they live on the Board in Narrative Zoom, with an **Open Narrative Zoom** link. Beats get a line when some beats have smaller steps inside them.
3. **The About fold-out**, such as *About characters: what they are and how to use them*. Click it to fold it open or shut. It says what the kind is, why it matters, when to use it, the common mistake, and an example. **Read the full chapter in Help** at its end opens the reference chapter at that kind. Once you fold it shut, it stays shut for that kind (here and on its records) until you open it again.
4. **The records**, one per row. Each row has:
   - the record's name as a link (click it to open the record);
   - its Kept for you mark, if it has one (see [Kept for you: work only you will do](#kept-for-you-work-only-you-will-do));
   - on the right, in small gray type, how many facts on it are decided, developing and still open, such as *18 decided, 6 developing, 6 still open*;
   - under the name, a few telling facts in small type, such as *Narrative importance: main Role: Lead*. The label is gray and the fact is dark.
5. **Records inside records.** A record that belongs inside another of the same kind sits indented under it (the Finale's five steps under the Finale beat). A record that belongs to a record of another kind names its owner first: each hidden move under Secret operations starts with the name of the character who makes it.
6. **The add box** at the bottom: a text box that says *A name is enough to start*, and a button named for the kind, such as **Add character** or **Add kept line**. (The Story world has no add box once it exists, because a book has only one.)
7. **Bring from another novel**, under the add box, for copying records you choose from another novel on your shelf. See [Bringing a record from another novel](#bringing-a-record-from-another-novel).

*None yet.* in place of the rows means this kind has no records.

### Adding a record to the Story Brain, step by step

**What it is.** Making a new record: a new person, place, clue or anything else, with its own page.

**Why it matters.** A record is findable. Once something has a record, search finds it, you can connect it to scenes, and the collaborator knows about it.

**How to do it.**

1. Click the kind in the left list.
2. Scroll to the bottom of the list page.
3. Type a name in the box. A name is enough. You can fill in everything else later, or never.
4. Click the button (**Add character**, **Add place** and so on).

The new record's page opens right away, ready for you.

**When to do it.** The moment something shows up in your head that you will want to find again. A minor character with only a name is a perfectly good record.

**The common mistake** is waiting until you know enough. You don't need to know anything but the name. A record with one line is still findable, and it can be connected to scenes.

**Adding a beat here.** On the Beats page, under the add box, a note says that a beat you add here joins the Board after the last beat, and that to change the beat sheet itself you use **My methods**, with an **Open My methods** link. **My methods** is the screen where you build methods of your own, including their beats; it is where you change the beats of a beat sheet you made yourself. Choosing which beat sheet your novel uses, or switching to another, happens in Novel setup or Narrative Zoom, as [Choosing a beat sheet for a novel](#choosing-a-beat-sheet-for-a-novel) explains. Add a beat here only for a one-off turn in this book.

**Example.** While drafting the harbor chapter, Marisol's writer realizes the ferry pilot will matter later. She clicks **Characters**, types "Tomás the ferry pilot" in the box, and clicks **Add character**. That's all. Three weeks later she connects him to the storm scene.

### Bringing a record from another novel

**What it is.** A way to copy a record you already made in another novel on your shelf into this one. You choose each record yourself, one tick at a time. Each one comes over as its own copy, with everything you knew about it.

**Where it is.** On a kind's list page, under the add box: a button named **Bring from another novel**. It is on every kind's list, so you bring characters from the Characters page, places from the Places page, and so on. If this is the only novel on your shelf, the button is grayed out and a line beside it says there is no other novel to bring from yet. Novels you removed from the shelf are not offered.

**What comes over.** For each record you bring:

- its name, its kind, and its notes;
- every field, with each entry's status exactly as it stands in the other novel. Decided stays decided. Pencil stays pencil. A blank you marked Still open stays open. A suggestion you had not taken yet stays a suggestion;
- only the newest entry in each field. Earlier versions and anything you set aside stay behind;
- fields of your own, both the ones on that one record and the ones you made for every record of that kind;
- a note on every entry saying where it came from.

**What stays behind.** Connections stay behind, because the records they point to are in the other novel. Nothing from the manuscript comes over: no scenes, no chapters. A scene card's place on the Board stays behind too, because the beats it sat on belong to the other novel.

**How to do it.**

1. Open this novel's Story Brain and click a kind in the left list, such as **Characters**.
2. Click **Bring from another novel**. A small window opens.
3. Under **From which novel?**, choose the novel to bring from. Its records of the same kind appear, each with a short line about it underneath.
4. Tick the records you want. Leave the rest.
5. Read any line that starts with **Already here:**. It means this novel already has a record of the same kind with the same name. Nothing is merged. If you tick it anyway, you will have two, and you can keep the one you like.
6. Click the button at the bottom. It counts for you: **Bring 2 characters**.

The window closes, and the list page says what happened, such as *Brought 2 characters from I Declare War.*, followed by each new name as a link. Nothing opens by itself. Click a name when you are ready to look.

**How you can tell a record was brought.** On its page, a chip beside **Kept for you** says *Brought from* the other novel's name and the date. In each fact's margin, the slanted line reads *From* the other novel's name. Click **Where did this come from?** on any fact to see the day it was brought, and any note it had in the other novel.

**The copy is its own record.** Change it, decide things on it, rename it. The original in the other novel does not change, and changing the original never reaches the copy.

**Changed your mind?** The whole bring is one step in the Story Trail. One **Undo** there removes every record it brought, together. The other novel is never touched.

**Why it matters.** A new novel deserves a clean start. When you begin again, the old novel often holds years of clutter: three versions of the same scene, characters you cut, guesses you never decided. Bringing records one at a time lets you start clean and still keep the work that earned its place. You decide what crosses over, and each record arrives already knowing what you decided about it, so you do not retype a single fact.

**The common mistake** is bringing everything at once. It feels safe to tick every box, so nothing is lost. But then the new novel holds the same clutter as the old one, and the clean start is gone. Nothing in the old novel goes anywhere. It waits on your shelf. So bring only what the next scenes need, and come back for more when the story asks for it.

**Example.** You are starting *The Keeper of Gull Point* fresh, and your older planning novel is still on the shelf with forty characters in it. You are about to write the opening, where Marisol Vega meets Ana Ruiz on the dock at Port Ellery.

*Where it lands:* you open **Characters**, click **Bring from another novel**, choose the older novel, and tick only Marisol Vega and Ana Ruiz. You also open **Places** and bring Port Ellery. Marisol arrives with her decided hair and her want still in pencil, exactly as you left them. Cyrus Hale stays behind for now. He does not appear until chapter six, and when you get there, you will bring him then.

*Where it goes wrong:* on a tired night you tick every one of the forty characters "just in case." The new novel's Characters list fills with cut characters and half-ideas, and the clean start is gone. One **Undo** in the Story Trail takes all forty back out, and you start again with the two you need.

## A record page from top to bottom

Click a record's name, and its page opens. Every kind of record uses the same layout, so once you know one, you know them all. This section goes from the top of the page to the bottom. In order, you will meet:

1. **Back to**, if you came from another record.
2. **The record's name.**
3. A line only some kinds have: **Whose move is this?** on a hidden move, **Between** on a relationship, **This question is** on a question.
4. **Kept for you**, any chips, **Ask about this**, and sometimes **Where did this come from?**
5. **The legend**: a key to the status inks.
6. **Suggestions waiting for you**, if the collaborator has suggested something for this record.
7. On a character, **Fill in one line**.
8. **The page tabs**: the numbered pages and **All pages**.
9. **One page**: its heading, the About fold-out (on the first page), and its groups of fields, each field with its facts.
10. Links to the page before and after.
11. **Delete** at the very bottom.

### Back to: returning to the record you came from

If you opened a record from inside another one (an arc from its character, a guardrail or hidden move from its owner), a small link at the very top takes you back: **Back to Marisol Vega**.

### The record's name, and renaming it

The record's name is the large heading at the top. It is also a box you can type in.

- **To rename a record:** click the name, change it, then click anywhere else on the page. The new name is kept the moment you click away.
- Renaming never breaks anything. Connections, scene cards and conversations point to the record itself, not to its old name.
- A name can't be empty. If you clear it and click away, the change is not saved, and the old name comes back the next time you open the record.

### Kept for you: work only you will do

Under the name, on the left, is **Kept for you**, with a dropdown that says **Not marked** until you choose something.

**What it is.** A mark that says "this one is mine." The collaborator will not draft or settle what you keep. Its suggestions about that record wait for you, and nothing is decided for you.

**Why it matters.** Some parts of a book are the reason you are writing it. Marking them protects them from an eager helper, and the mark shows you at a glance, across the whole book, what kind of work is waiting for you.

**Do most writers need it?** Not at first. It earns its place once you work with the collaborator, or once your book has a few set pieces you have been carrying in your head for years.

**What the choices mean:**

| Choice | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Not marked** | No mark. The usual state. |
| ***Your name*, it's on you now** | Handed back to you. The collaborator offers nothing more here unless you ask. (Your first name comes from About you or from Settings. Without a name it reads *It's on you now*.) |
| **Mine to write** | You write this one. The collaborator never drafts it. |
| **Mine to build** | You work out how it is built: the mechanics, the setup, how the pieces fit. |
| **Mine to design** | You design it: how it looks, the space, the shape of the trick or the trap. |
| **Mine to choreograph** | You stage the movement: who goes where, the fight, the chase. |
| **Mine to time** | You set the timing: the pacing, when a reveal lands, the beat before the punchline. |
| **Mine to escalate** | You decide how it builds, and how big it gets before it breaks. |
| **Mine to settle** | You decide the open question here. Suggestions wait; nothing is settled for you. |

You can rename these markers, or add your own, in **Settings**, under **Words for decisions**: the first marker is *The marker for what is yours to write or decide*, and the rest are in *Other markers for work you keep (one per line)*.

**How to do it.** Click the **Kept for you** dropdown and choose a marker. To clear it, choose **Not marked** again.

**Where the mark shows.** Beside the record's name in its kind's list, and on the **Kept for you** page of **Choices ahead**.

**The same mark elsewhere.** Beats and scene cards in Narrative Zoom carry the same dropdown, and so does each written scene, in the Writing Room's scene plan (there it is called **Kept for you to write or decide**, and the marker shows above the scene's title). The choices and their meanings are the ones in the table above.

**The common mistake** is marking everything. If every record is kept for you, the mark stops telling you anything. Keep it for the parts that are truly yours.

**Example.** Marisol's writer has pictured the night Eli drowned for years. She marks that event record **Mine to write**. She will ask the collaborator about the harbor town's history, but not about that night.

### Chips beside Kept for you

Some records show a small rounded label (a **chip**) on the same line:

- **Written by you, kept by you, placement open** on a kept line you saved from a conversation. It says who wrote the line, that you chose to keep it, and that it is not placed in a scene yet. If the collaborator wrote it, the chip names the collaborator instead of you.
- **Suggested by** *a name*, such as **Suggested by ChatGPT**, on a record an AI suggested. It reminds you that the whole record started as someone else's idea.
- **Brought from** *a novel's name* and a date, such as **Brought from I Declare War on October 21, 2026**, on a record you brought from another novel. See [Bringing a record from another novel](#bringing-a-record-from-another-novel).

### Ask about this on a record

**Ask about this** is a link on the same line. Click it, and Story Canon opens **Conversations** with a new conversation titled *About* followed by the record's name, set up to talk the record through with the collaborator.

- **When to use it:** when you are stuck on this person, place or clue and want to think out loud.
- **What it does not do:** it does not change the record. Anything the collaborator suggests comes back as a suggestion, waiting for you.
- If you have not turned the collaborator on in Settings, the conversation opens but cannot get answers until you do. See [Conversations and the collaborator](#conversations-and-the-collaborator).

### Where did this come from? for a whole record

On some records, a **Where did this come from?** link sits on the same line. It appears only when the whole record came from a conversation, such as a line you kept from a chat. Click it to see the conversation and the exact message. (Facts have their own **Where did this come from?** too; see [Where did this come from? for one fact](#where-did-this-come-from-for-one-fact).)

### The status legend and the status inks

Under that line is a row of six small samples. This is the **legend**, a key like the one on a map. Each fact on the page wears one of these looks, so you can tell at a glance what is settled and what is not. The words are colored the way the facts below them are, which is why the colors are called **inks**.

| In the legend | How a fact with this status looks on the page |
| --- | --- |
| **Decided (inked)**, beside a small blue square | The words in ink blue, with a small blue square before them. |
| **Developing (pencil)**, beside a short dashed line | The words in pencil gray, with a dashed underline. |
| **Still open (a blank line)**, beside a short solid line | A blank line to write on, with a gray prompt in slanted type (*Still open: nothing written yet* when nothing is written). |
| **Example, never canon**, in sepia slanted type | The words in sepia (a warm brown), slanted, after a small boxed label that reads "Example". |
| **Suggestion (a slip, tucked in)**, beside a tiny slip | The words on a white slip of paper, tucked in at a slight angle, in a handwriting typeface, as if someone else wrote them. |
| **Set aside (faded, kept below)**, faded, with a line through it | Not on the field itself. A set-aside fact moves into the field's earlier versions, folded away below the field, in small faded type with a line through it. |

"Kept below" is the reminder: a set-aside fact is never deleted, only folded away under its field. When you show the earlier versions, a set-aside fact has a line through it, just like its sample. An earlier decision that a newer one replaced sits in the same list, faded but with no line through it, marked *replaced*.

In dark mode the inks are lighter versions of the same colors, so they still read clearly.

The legend is not clickable. If you renamed the status words in **Settings** (on its page **3 Writing**, under **Words for decisions**), the legend uses your words, and the looks stay the same. Each status is taught in full in [How each status looks and behaves on a record](#how-each-status-looks-and-behaves-on-a-record).

### Suggestions waiting for you on a record

If the collaborator has suggested something for this record, a section headed **Suggestions waiting for you** appears under the legend, above the page tabs, so you see it whichever page you are on. Each suggestion shows the idea on a slip and these buttons:

| Button | What it does |
| --- | --- |
| **Accept as Decided** | Takes the idea into your story, decided. (Only for a fact.) |
| **Accept as Developing** | Takes the idea in, in pencil. (Only for a fact.) |
| **Accept** | Takes the idea in. This single button replaces the two above when the suggestion is not a fact but a new record (it reads *New* followed by the kind and name, such as a new question) or a connection (it reads *Connect:* followed by the connection word). |
| **Not yet** | Keeps it waiting, marked *(you said not yet)*. It comes back each time you open the record. Once you have said not yet, this button no longer shows. |
| **No** | Turns it down. It leaves every list of waiting suggestions and does not come back. Nothing is erased: the conversation it came from still holds the collaborator's message, word for word. |

When you accept a fact, the record turns to the page where it landed, so you can see it in place, and its margin says *Suggested by* the collaborator *, accepted by you*.

Suggestions that came in from your planning documents don't sit here. They sit in their own fields, as slips, right where they apply. Both kinds are gathered on one page: see [Suggestions waiting: ideas you have not taken yet](#suggestions-waiting-ideas-you-have-not-taken-yet).

### Fill in one line, above the page tabs

On a character, a small line reads *Low-energy day? One line counts.* with a **Fill in one line** button, just above the page tabs. It opens one empty field for you and turns to its page. It is described in [Fill in one line on a low-energy day](#fill-in-one-line-on-a-low-energy-day).

### The numbered pages and All pages

**What they are.** A record reads like a notebook. Its fields are gathered onto numbered pages, each holding things that belong together, and one page shows at a time. A character has up to eleven pages (ten on most novels); a place has two.

**Why it matters.** A character can hold more than a hundred fields. On one long scroll you would lose your place; on pages you always know where you are, and you can see at a glance which pages you have started.

**How to use them.**

- **The page tabs** sit in a row under the name, like the edge of a notebook. Each tab has a number in a small circle and a title, such as **1 Who they are**. Click a tab to turn to that page. The page you are on is the tab joined to the page, in bold purple with a pink line across its top.
- **A page number turns ink blue** (a filled blue circle) when that page has something written on it (a fact, an arc, a connection, a note). Point at a tab with the mouse, and it says *Something is written on this page* or *Nothing on this page yet*.
- **All pages**, at the end of the row, shows every page one after another, each with its number and title as a heading, separated by dashed lines. There are no page-turn links in this view, because there is nothing to turn to. Use it when you want to read the whole record straight through, or when you don't know which page holds something.
- **Turning pages.** At the bottom of each page, links go to the page before and the page after. They name the page, such as **Page 2: Identity** and **Page 4: Personality** at the bottom of a character's Looks and voice page.
- **Where a record opens.** A record opens on its first page the first time, and after that on the page you last read, while the program is open. Search opens a record on the page that holds what you found, and accepting a suggestion turns to the page where it landed.

**Pages that are left out, and why the numbers change.** A page with nothing to show is left out of the row, and the numbers close up behind it. Most pages are always there, but a page made only of a writing method's fields, or of fields for a choice about how the book is told, appears only once that method or choice is turned on in Novel setup. (A **writing method** here means a teacher's way of building a story, such as Randy Ingermanson's Snowflake Method; you choose the ones you want on the page **My storytelling toolkit** in Novel setup.) For example, a character's **What opposes them** page holds only Robert McKee's fields, so it appears only when you turn McKee on. Without it, the pages after it each move up one number. That is why this guide names pages (the **Arc** page) instead of giving their numbers: on your novel, the number may differ. A record with only one page, such as a record of a kind you made yourself, shows no tabs at all.

**Pages in front, in Narrative Zoom.** When you open a record inside Narrative Zoom (the view where you plan the book's shape), one or two pages about where it sits in the story come first, and the record's own pages follow, numbered after them. See [Zoom: a beat page](#zoom-a-beat-page), [Zoom: a scene card page](#zoom-a-scene-card-page) and [Following a record through the story](#following-a-record-through-the-story).

**Other screens use the same pages.** Where was I?, Settings, Novel setup, the Writing Room's scene plan, the Story Brain's **Questions and ideas**, **Choices ahead** and **Suggestions waiting**, the continuity checks **Timelines**, **Who knows what**, **Setups and payoffs** and **Cast**, and **Send something out** are laid out the same way: numbered tabs, **All pages** at the end, links to the page before and after, and the page you were on remembered while the program is open.

**The pages of each kind.** The last page of every kind is **Notes and connections**: the Notes box, Connections, any fields of your own, and **Add your own field**. The pages before it depend on the kind. Pages marked *(only with…)* appear only when that method or choice is on; the others are always there.

| Kind | Its pages, in order |
| --- | --- |
| Character | Who they are; Identity; Looks and voice; Personality; Goals, motives and values; What opposes them *(only with McKee (Story))*; Inner life; Arc; Their world and their people; Interview; Notes, guardrails and writing aids |
| Scene card | The scene; When it happens; Who tells it *(only with Unreliable narrator or A narrator who is not who they seem)*; Mood and feeling; The reader; Scene and sequel *(only with Scene and Sequel (Swain))*; Notes and connections |
| Beat | In this book; Questions for this beat *(only on the Save the Cat beats that have questions)*; Notes and connections |
| Story world | The heart of the book; What it means *(only with The Moral Premise (Williams) or McKee (Story))*; Time, viewpoint and knowledge; Notes and connections |
| Event or reveal | What happens; When, and who knows; Notes and connections |
| Every other kind | A page named for the kind (*The place*, *The clue*, *The kept line*); Notes and connections |

So on most novels a character has ten pages, and a scene card has five. A character is different in one more way: its Notes, guardrails and its own fields are on its last page, **Notes, guardrails and writing aids**, and its Connections are on **Their world and their people**.

**Nothing is ever hidden.** A group of fields that no page names (such as a group that came in with your planning documents) goes on the last page.

**The common mistake** is thinking a record is empty because its first page is. Look at the page numbers: a blue number means something is written on that page.

**Example.** Marisol Vega's tabs show blue numbers on **Who they are**, **Identity**, **Inner life** and **Arc**. Her writer has her role, her identity, her inner life and her arc started, and nothing yet on how she looks. She clicks the **Looks and voice** tab and writes one line about her hands.

### The About fold-out on a record's first page

At the top of the first page is the same **About** fold-out you saw on the kind's list page: what this kind of record is, why it matters, when to use it, the common mistake, an example, and **Read the full chapter in Help**. Fold it shut once you know it; it stays shut on every record of that kind.

### Notes on a record

**Notes** is a large, plain box on the **Notes and connections** page (on a character, the **Notes, guardrails and writing aids** page). Type anything: a hunch, a list, a paragraph you can't place yet.

- It saves when you click anywhere outside the box.
- Notes have no status. They are your scratch paper, so nothing in them counts as decided.
- Search finds words in Notes.

**When to use it.** For thinking that is not yet a fact. Once something becomes a real answer, put it in the field it belongs in, where it gets a status and a date.

### Groups of fields on a page

Each page holds one or more **groups**: fields gathered under a heading because they answer related questions. A character's **Looks and voice** page has the groups *Appearance*, *Physical* and *Language and voice*.

**What you see under each group heading:** one line in gray saying what the group is for. Some headings are followed by a teacher's name in small type, such as **Goal and motive** *(Ingermanson)* or **Moral premise** *(Williams)*. That means the group comes from that writing teacher's method. The plain name says what the group is for; the small name says where it comes from. [Turning on a writing method for characters](#turning-on-a-writing-method-for-characters) explains where each one comes from.

**Groups come and go with your methods.** A group that belongs to a method appears when you turn that method on in Novel setup. If you turn a method off, its empty fields disappear, but anything you already wrote in them stays on the page. Nothing you write is ever hidden by a change in Novel setup.

**A group no page names** (for example a group that came in with your planning documents, or the ingredients of a story type on the Story world) appears on the record's last page, so nothing is ever hidden.

### Reading one field: its teaching line and Why it matters

Each field has the same parts, from top to bottom:

1. **Its name** in bold, such as **Who they are**. A field you made yourself says *(your field)* after its name, or *(your field, here only)* if it is on this record only. A private field says *(private, never exported)*.
2. **Add**, a link on the far right. Click it to write something in this field. It changes to **Close** while the writing box is open. (A field of your own on every record of a kind also has **Remove field**; see [Removing a field of your own](#removing-a-field-of-your-own).)
3. **The teaching line**, in gray under the name: a sentence or two saying what the field is for.
4. **Why it matters**, a link at the end of the teaching line (on fields that have teaching).
5. **Not the same as its neighbors**, on fields that are easy to confuse with another field. It says how they differ.
6. **Not yet specified**, on some empty identity and appearance fields.
7. **What is written there**, if anything: each fact with its margin on the right. Decided facts come first, then pencil ideas and suggestions, then Still open, then examples.
8. **Show earlier and set-aside versions**, when the field has any.

**Why it matters, in detail.** Click **Why it matters** to fold out:

- a label saying how many books need this field: **Most books need this**, **Useful for many books** or **Only for some books**;
- why the field helps a book;
- **The common mistake** people make with it;
- **Example**, often one that lands and one that fails.

Click **Less** to fold it away.

**Not the same as its neighbors, in detail.** Some fields sound alike: *Wounds* and the arc's *Ghost*, *Goal* and the arc's *Want*. Under such a field, a line starting **Not the same as its neighbors.** says how this field differs from the one you might confuse it with. When you are unsure where a fact belongs, read this line.

**Not yet specified, in detail.** Identity and appearance fields say **Not yet specified** when they are empty. That is a promise: the program never guesses a character's gender, race, age or looks from a name or from any other field. Until you write it, it is unspecified.

### Add: the small form for writing in a field

**What it is.** The small form that opens under a field when you click **Add**. It is how every fact gets written.

**How to do it.**

1. Find the field. Turn to its page, using the page tabs.
2. Click **Add** on the right of the field's name. A writing box opens under the field, with the cursor in it.
3. Type your words. Some fields show small buttons of common answers under the box (for example *she/her*, *he/him*, *they/them* under **Pronouns**), followed by *or your own words*. Click one to fill the box, or ignore them and type anything.
4. Choose a status in the first dropdown under the box. It starts on **Developing**. (See the next section.)
5. If a second dropdown says **Timing**, choose when the fact is true, or leave it. (See [Timing: when a fact is true](#timing-when-a-fact-is-true).)
6. Click **Save**.

The fact appears in the field with its margin, and the writing box closes. Click **Close** instead of **Save** to stop without saving.

**The note beside Save** tells you what will happen:

- *Sits beside any other possibilities.* (when you save as **Developing**): your new idea joins any others in pencil. Nothing is replaced.
- *The earlier decision stays in history.* (when you save as **Decided** and the field already has a decision): the old decision is retired into the field's earlier versions, not erased.

**Save is grayed out** until you type something, unless you chose **Still open**, which can be saved with nothing written.

**The common mistake** is typing a whole paragraph of mixed thoughts into one field. One fact per entry. If you have three possible answers, save three entries in pencil; they sit side by side, and you can decide one later.

**Example.** On Marisol Vega, her writer clicks **Add** beside **Who they are**, types "A woman who keeps a light for strangers so she never has to let anyone close," leaves the status on **Developing**, and clicks **Save**. The line appears in pencil gray with a dashed underline.

### Choosing a status when you save

The status dropdown in the small form has four choices:

| Choice | Choose it when |
| --- | --- |
| **Decided** | You have made up your mind. |
| **Developing** | You are still working on it. This is the default, because pencil is free. |
| **Still open** | You want to mark the field as a real question you have not answered yet. You can leave the box empty, or write the question itself ("Did Eli take the skiff out alone, or did someone go with him?"). |
| **Example** | You are writing an illustration of what an answer could look like, not an answer. An example never counts as part of the story. |

There is no **Suggestion** choice. A suggestion is, by definition, an idea from someone else; you can't suggest something to yourself. There is no **Set aside** choice either, because you set aside something that is already there (see [Set aside](#set-aside-on-a-fact)).

**The common mistake** is saving as **Decided** because the page looks more finished. If you are not sure, leave it in pencil. A page of honest pencil is worth more than a page of false ink.

### Timing: when a fact is true

**What it is.** Some facts change over a story: a character's age, job, hair, pronouns, or where they live. Those fields have a second dropdown in the small form, **Timing**, with these choices:

- **Before the story** (backstory: true before page one)
- **True when the story opens**
- **Happens during the story**
- **Timing not decided**

Leave it on **Timing** to say nothing about when. The choice you make shows in the fact's margin. Every field in a character's Identity group offers Timing, and so do many others (hair, build, dress, home, history, a relationship's trust).

**Why it matters.** A character can have long hair *when the story opens* and cut it off *during the story* without the two facts arguing. You can keep both, decided, side by side.

**The common mistake** is deciding a new fact and letting it replace the old one when both were true at different times. Give each its timing instead.

**Example.** For Marisol, **Work** holds "Assistant keeper, under her father" (*Before the story*) and "Head keeper" (*True when the story opens*). Both are decided. They don't contradict each other, because their timing differs.

### Story Brain: the margin beside each fact

To the right of every fact is its **margin**, a narrow column behind a thin line. From top to bottom:

1. **The status and the date**, on one line: the status in small capital letters (**DECIDED** with its small blue square, **DEVELOPING**, **STILL OPEN**, **EXAMPLE**, **SUGGESTION**), then the date and time it was written down. For a fact brought in from your planning documents, the date is when it was settled in your planning, not the day you brought the document in.
2. **Where it came from**, in slanted type, when it did not come from you typing it:
   - *From* a document's name: it came in with a document you brought in.
   - *From* another novel's name: you brought the record it sits on from that novel.
   - *Suggested by* a name, such as *Suggested by the collaborator*: an AI offered it.
   - *, accepted by you* at the end: you accepted that suggestion.
3. **Its timing**, if it has one, such as *True when the story opens*.
4. **What you can do with it**, as buttons. Only the buttons that make sense appear:

| Button | Appears on | What it does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Write it** | A Still open field with nothing written | Opens the writing box for this field. |
| **Decide this** | A fact in pencil, a suggestion, or a Still open fact with words in it | Makes it Decided. |
| **Not decided after all** | A Decided fact | Moves it back to Developing. |
| **Set aside** | Every fact | Retires it into the field's earlier versions. |
| **Where did this come from?** | A fact that came from a conversation, a document or another novel | Opens the source window. |

An **Example** has no **Decide this** button, on purpose; see [Example on a record](#example-on-a-record).

**Why it matters.** The margin answers the three questions you will ask about any fact months from now: how sure was I, when did I write this, and whose idea was it. A fact you typed yourself has no "where it came from" line, because it is yours.

**On a narrow window**, the margin moves under the fact's words instead of beside them. Nothing in it changes.

**Example.** Beside "Gray-streaked, always braided" on Marisol's **Hair**, the margin reads **DECIDED** and the date, then *True when the story opens*, then **Not decided after all** and **Set aside**.

### Decide this on a fact

**What it is.** The button that turns a fact into a decision: ink blue, settled, part of your story.

**What happens to the field.** It depends on whether the field holds one answer or a list (see [A list, with several entries](#a-list-with-several-entries)):

- **A one-answer field** (most fields): the fact you decide becomes the field's decision. If the field already had a decision, the old one retires into the earlier versions, marked *replaced*. Nothing is erased.
- **A list field** (such as **Values** or **Habits and props**): the fact joins the other decided entries. Nothing is replaced.

**What does not happen.** Deciding one pencil version does not retire the others. If a field holds two ideas in pencil and you click **Decide this** on one, the other stays beside it in pencil, still a possibility. When you are done with it, click **Set aside** on it. This is on purpose: the program never throws away one of your ideas because you chose another.

**On a question.** When you decide the **Answer** on a question record, the question moves to **Answered** by itself.

**Example.** Marisol's **Lie** holds two wordings in pencil: "Anyone I care about will leave" and "Love is a door people walk out of." Her writer clicks **Decide this** on the first, then **Set aside** on the second.

### Not decided after all on a fact

**What it is.** The button on a Decided fact that moves it back to **Developing**, in pencil.

**When to use it.** When you made up your mind too soon, or a new idea has shaken an old decision. It is the honest move: better to reopen a decision than to keep writing on top of one you no longer believe.

Nothing is lost. The fact stays where it is, now in pencil, and you can decide it again with one click.

### Set aside on a fact

**What it is.** The button that retires a fact without deleting it. A set-aside fact leaves the field and moves into **Show earlier and set-aside versions**, marked *Set aside* with the date.

**When to use it.** For an idea you have moved past, a pencil version you no longer want beside your decision, or a suggestion you have turned down. It keeps the page clean while keeping your history.

**How to bring it back.** Open the earlier versions (see [Earlier and set-aside versions](#earlier-and-set-aside-versions)) and click **Bring back** beside it. It returns as **Developing**.

**The common mistake** is deleting a whole record to "start over." Set aside the facts you no longer want instead; the record keeps its connections and its history.

### Where did this come from? for one fact

Click **Where did this come from?** in a fact's margin, and a window titled **Where this came from** opens over the page. What it shows depends on where the fact came from:

- **From a document:** the document's name and, after a comma, the row or place in it. Two dates, explained in plain words: when the fact was settled in your planning, and when the document that carried it in was made. Then the document's own entry, laid out in rows when it has them: **About**, **What it says**, **Status in your planning** (shown in Story Canon's status words), **Where your planning got it**, and **How it changed**. When the entry points to passages of your planning documents, those passages follow under a heading of their own.
- **From a conversation:** under **In the conversation**, the conversation's title, which message it was, who said it (you, the collaborator, or another AI), when, and the message itself, word for word.
- **From another novel:** a line such as *Brought from I Declare War on October 21, 2026.*, and, if the fact had a note in that novel, the note itself.
- **Typed by you:** *This was entered directly, not from a conversation or a document.*

Click **Close** (or press **Esc**) to return to the page.

If a document you brought in labeled its entries with reference codes of its own, a fact keeps them exactly as written. Bringing documents in is taught in [Bringing things in and sending things out](#bringing-things-in-and-sending-things-out).

**Why it matters.** Months from now you won't remember whether a line was your idea or an AI's. One click tells you. A suggestion that sounds like you is still a suggestion.

### Earlier and set-aside versions

When a field has old versions, a link appears under it: **Show earlier and set-aside versions (2)**. The number is how many. Click it to see them, in small faded type, each with what happened to it and when:

- *(replaced, Oct 18)*: a decision that a newer decision took the place of.
- *(Set aside, Oct 18)*: something you retired with **Set aside**.

Beside a set-aside version is **Bring back**. Click it to restore it as **Developing**. Click **Hide earlier and set-aside versions** to fold the list away.

**Why it matters.** Nothing in Story Canon is silently deleted. If you liked last month's version of the Lie better, it is one click away.

### Delete at the bottom of a record

At the very bottom of every record, under its pages, is a small red **Delete** button. Click it, and Story Canon asks *Move "…" to Deleted? You can restore it from there.* Click **OK** to move the record to **Deleted** (a view that keeps everything you delete, ready to restore); click **Cancel** to keep it. A deleted record can be restored with everything on it.

A beat has a different button instead: **Remove this beat from the novel**, with the note *Only if this book does not use it. Your scene cards stay.* Story Canon asks you to confirm, and explains that your notes on the beat go to Deleted and that scene cards placed on it go back to *Not on a beat yet*.

**The common mistake** is deleting a record to clear it out. Set aside the facts instead; see [Set aside](#set-aside-on-a-fact).

## How each status looks and behaves on a record

Every fact has a **status**: a word that says how settled it is. There are six, and they are always called by the same words on screen. Chapter 01 gives each a one-line meaning. This section teaches each one in full, as you meet it on a record: what it means, how it looks, how a fact gets it, what you can do with it, why it matters, the common mistake, and an example.

| Status | How it looks | How it gets there | What you can do next |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Decided** | Ink blue, small blue square | You choose it when you save, click **Decide this**, or accept a suggestion as Decided | **Not decided after all**, **Set aside** |
| **Developing** | Pencil gray, dashed underline | The default when you save; **Not decided after all**; **Bring back**; accepting a suggestion as Developing | **Decide this**, **Set aside** |
| **Still open** | A blank line with a gray prompt | You choose it when you save | **Write it** (if empty) or **Decide this** (if it holds words), **Set aside** |
| **Example** | Sepia, slanted, boxed "Example" label | You choose it when you save | **Set aside** |
| **Suggestion** | A white slip, tucked in, in handwriting | Only someone else makes one: the collaborator, or a document or chat you brought in | **Decide this**, **Set aside**, or the accept buttons on Suggestions waiting |
| **Set aside** | Faded, line through it, in the earlier versions | **Set aside**, or **No, set it aside** on a planning slip | **Bring back** |

### Decided on a record

**What it means here.** You have made up your mind. This is canon: part of your story, what the collaborator and the checks treat as true.

**How it looks.** Ink blue words with a small blue square in front, like something written in pen. The margin says **DECIDED**, in blue, with its own small square.

**How it behaves.** A one-answer field has one decision at a time; deciding a new one retires the old one into the field's earlier versions, marked *replaced*. A list field can hold many decided entries at once. Decided facts are listed first under the field.

**Why it matters.** You can build on a decision without second-guessing it, and so can everything else in Story Canon.

**The common mistake** is deciding early to make a page feel finished. Decide when you would be surprised to change it.

**Example.** Marisol's **Year born**: "1983", Decided. And on the event *The night Eli drowned*: "October 2012, off the north rocks", Decided. You know these.

### Developing on a record

**What it means here.** Pencil: an idea you are working on. Several can sit side by side in one field, which is the right way to work on anything hard. Write three wordings of a Lie, live with them, decide one.

**How it looks.** Pencil-gray words with a dashed underline and a short dashed mark in front. The margin says **DEVELOPING**, in gray.

**How it behaves.** Developing facts never replace each other. They wait until you decide one or set them aside. Fields that hold pencil and no decision appear in **Choices ahead**, on its page **Possibilities**.

**Why it matters.** Nothing forces you to choose before you're ready, and you can compare versions side by side.

**The common mistake** is thinking two pencil answers in one field are a problem to fix. They are a choice you haven't made yet.

**Example.** Two pencil ideas under **How it could happen** on the event *The boat comes in*: "Its line was cut and it drifted in on the tide" and "Someone towed it in at night and let it go."

### Still open on a record

**What it means here.** A real question you have chosen not to answer yet. It is different from an empty field: an empty field says nothing; Still open says "I know this matters, and I haven't decided."

**How it looks.** Slanted gray words sitting on a ruled line, like a form waiting to be filled, with a short solid dash in front. The margin says **STILL OPEN**.

**How it behaves.** A Still open field with nothing written shows a blank line and *Still open: nothing written yet*, with a **Write it** button in its margin. Click **Write it** when you are ready; the writing box opens. If you wrote the question itself when you marked it, the words show on the blank line, and **Decide this** can make them the answer. Every Still open field appears in **Choices ahead**, under **Still open**. Nothing in Story Canon, and nothing the collaborator does, ever fills a Still open line in by guessing.

**Why it matters.** "I haven't decided" is different from "I forgot." Marking a question open keeps it in view and keeps it from being answered by accident.

**The common mistake** is reading Still open as a failure, or feeling you must answer every open line before you write. It is a note to your future self. Many answers turn up while you write.

**Example.** On Cyrus Hale, **What they stand to lose**: Still open, "Does he lose the harbor, or his son?"

### Example on a record

**What it means here.** An illustration, never canon. Use it to try out what an answer could look like (for yourself, or to show the collaborator the kind of thing you mean) without committing to it.

**How it looks.** Sepia (a warm brown) slanted words, in a book typeface, after a small boxed label reading "Example", so you can't mistake it for a plan. The margin says **EXAMPLE**, in sepia.

**How it behaves.** An example cannot be decided. There is no **Decide this** button on it, and the program refuses to turn an example into a decision directly. If you come to love an example, write it again in the same field as your own fact, in pencil or decided, and set the example aside. That way your story never contains something you did not choose on purpose.

**Why it matters.** An example teaches the shape of an answer without sneaking in as the answer.

**The common mistake** is reading a vivid example as the plan. Vivid is not the same as decided.

**Example.** Under Marisol's **Sayings and verbal habits**, an Example: "Weather's a liar." Her writer is trying out the flavor of her speech before choosing her real sayings.

### Suggestion on a record

**What it means here.** An idea someone else offered, waiting for you. It is never part of your story until you take it.

**How it looks.** A white slip of paper with a purple left edge, tilted very slightly, in a handwriting typeface, as if someone else had written it and tucked it between the pages. The margin says **SUGGESTION** and names who suggested it, such as *Suggested by ChatGPT*.

**How it behaves.** Suggestions reach a record in two ways, and the buttons differ:

| Where you see it | Where it came from | What you can do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Inside a field, as a slip | A document or chat you brought in | **Decide this** takes it in as Decided. **Set aside** lets it go. |
| Under **Suggestions waiting for you**, above the page tabs | The collaborator | **Accept as Decided**, **Accept as Developing** (or a single **Accept**, for a new record or a connection), **Not yet** or **No**. See [Suggestions waiting for you on a record](#suggestions-waiting-for-you-on-a-record). |

An accepted suggestion keeps its history: its margin says *Suggested by* the collaborator (or ChatGPT, or a document's name) *, accepted by you*, even once it is Decided. Deciding it makes it yours, but Story Canon still remembers where the idea started. **Choices ahead** counts every slip, and **Suggestions waiting** gathers them all on one page.

**Why it matters.** You see the idea right where it applies, but it never counts as yours until you choose it.

**The common mistake** is accepting everything because it all sounds good. Each idea you accept is something you have to stay consistent with. Accept as Developing when you like an idea but haven't committed.

**Example.** Under **Suggestions waiting for you** on Cyrus's record: "He was the last person to see Eli alive." Marisol's writer clicks **Not yet**. It stays, marked *(you said not yet)*, until she is ready.

### Set aside on a record

**What it means here.** Retired, never deleted. A set-aside fact no longer counts, no longer shows in the field, and no longer appears in Choices ahead.

**How it looks.** You don't see it on the field. It waits under the field's link **Show earlier and set-aside versions**, faded and small, with a line through it, followed by *(Set aside,* and the date*)*. Earlier decisions that a newer one replaced sit in the same list, faded but not struck through, marked *replaced*.

**How it behaves.** **Bring back** returns it as Developing. Search still finds it.

**Why it matters.** Nothing in Story Canon is ever silently deleted, so you can let go of an idea without fear of losing it.

**The common mistake** is deleting a whole record, or typing over a fact, to be rid of an idea. Set it aside instead: the page stays clean and the idea stays findable.

**Example.** Marisol's writer sets aside "Eli drowned saving Marisol," because it made her guilt too simple. Months later it is still there under **Show earlier and set-aside versions (1)**, if she wants it back.

### Statuses and the rest of the program

Statuses are not decoration. The rest of Story Canon reads them:

- **Where was I?** lists your open questions and your latest decisions, on its page **2 Still open**.
- **Choices ahead**, in the Story Brain, collects every Still open field, every field with pencil answers and no decision, and every suggestion waiting.
- **Narrative Zoom** counts a scene card as placed only when its spot is decided, so its checks never report a problem based on a guess.
- **The collaborator** treats Decided facts as true, treats pencil as possibilities, never fills in an open line by guessing, and never offers an example as a fact.

So the more honest your statuses, the more useful everything else becomes.

### Story Brain: statuses that look wrong but are working

- **No Decide this beside an example.** On purpose. Examples can't become canon.
- **Two or three answers in one field, all in pencil.** On purpose. They wait until you choose.
- **A decided answer vanished after you decided a different one.** It was replaced, not lost. Click **Show earlier and set-aside versions** under that field.
- **Your status words don't match this guide.** Someone renamed them in **Settings**, under **Words for decisions** (for example, "Inked" instead of "Decided"). Your words show everywhere, and the looks stay the same.
- **A decided suggestion still says *Suggested by ChatGPT*.** On purpose. Deciding it makes it yours, but Story Canon still remembers where the idea started.

## Adding your own fields

**What it is.** A field you add to a record, when the record doesn't have a place for something you want to keep.

**Why it matters.** Story Canon's fields cover what most books need, but your book is not most books. A field of your own works like a built-in one: statuses, a margin for each fact, earlier versions, and search. (It has no Timing dropdown.)

**Do most writers need it?** Some do, a few times. Look first: a field you want may already exist on a page you haven't opened.

**Where it is.** On the record's last page (on a character, **Notes, guardrails and writing aids**), under the heading **Add your own field**. Fields you add appear in a group called **My fields** on that same page.

### Just this one or every one

1. Type the field's name in the box that says *For example: Weather*.
2. Choose where it goes:
   - **Just this** followed by the kind (such as **Just this character**): the field appears on this record only. It shows *(your field, here only)* after its name. This is chosen for you to start.
   - **Every** followed by the kind (such as **Every character**): the field appears on every record of this kind in this novel, including ones you add later. It shows *(your field)*.
3. If the field should hold several separate entries, tick **A list, with several entries** (see below).
4. Click **Add field**.

**When to use each.** Choose **Every** when the field is a question you want to ask of every character (or place, or clue) in this book. Choose **Just this** for a detail only one record needs.

**The common mistake** is adding a field that already exists under another name. Before you add *Fears*, look at a character's **Inner life** page: *Psychological* already has it.

**Example.** Everyone on Gull Point has a relationship with the sea. Marisol's writer adds *What the sea means to them* to **Every character**. On Cyrus Hale alone, she adds *Keys he carries* with **Just this character**.

### A list, with several entries

A **list field** holds several separate entries that all count at once, each with its own status and margin. Without the tick, a field holds one answer: deciding a new one replaces the old decision.

- **A list** fits things a character has many of: scars, places they have lived, things they keep.
- **One answer** fits things with one true value at a time: a nickname everyone uses, the town they were born in.

**Example.** Marisol's writer adds *Things she keeps from the sea* to Marisol, as a list. It gets three entries, all decided: "A jar of green sea glass," "Eli's brass compass," "A cork float with no net."

### Removing a field of your own

A field you added to **Every** record of a kind has a small **Remove field** link beside **Add**. Click it, and Story Canon asks *Remove your field "…" from every record of this kind?* and reminds you that anything already written in it stays in Story Trail (the record of every change; see [Finding things: Search and Story Trail](#finding-things-search-and-story-trail)) and search. Click **OK** to remove it.

A field you added to **Just this** record has no remove link. Choose its name with care. If you no longer want what is in it, **Set aside** its facts.

## Guardrails: rules that must stay true

**What it is.** A **guardrail** is a firm rule about your story, written down so it can't be broken by accident: "Marisol never lets the light go dark, even on her worst night." "No one in Port Ellery is cruel to a child."

**Why it matters.** It's easy to forget a rule you made three months ago, and easy for an eager collaborator to suggest exactly the thing you ruled out. The collaborator reads every guardrail in the book before it answers you, and if an idea would break one, it says which.

**Do most writers need it?** Many books have two or three. You need one the first time you catch yourself thinking "whatever happens, this never happens."

**The common mistake** is using guardrails for preferences. A guardrail is a hard line, not a taste. "I'd like her to be dry and funny" is a preference; put it in her fields. "She is never cruel to Ana, even when she pushes her away" is a guardrail.

### Guardrails for one character

Each character has a **Guardrails** section on its **Notes, guardrails and writing aids** page, with the line *What must stay true about this character. The collaborator checks every suggestion against these.* Each guardrail is a line in ink with a **Remove** button.

**How to add one.**

1. Open the character and click the **Notes, guardrails and writing aids** tab (the last one).
2. Type the rule in the box that says **Add a guardrail**.
3. Click **Add**.

**Remove** beside a guardrail deletes it (it goes to Deleted, where you can restore it).

**Example.** On Cyrus Hale: "Cyrus did not kill Eli. Whatever he hides, it is not murder."

### Guardrails for the whole book

A rule about the whole story, rather than one character, is a guardrail record of its own.

1. In the left list, click **Show every kind**, then **Guardrails** under **More kinds you can use**. (Once your book has any guardrail, **Guardrails** sits in the main list.)
2. Type the rule in the box at the bottom, such as "No one in Port Ellery is cruel to a child."
3. Click **Add guardrail**.

The **Guardrails** list shows every guardrail in the book: the book-wide ones, and each character's, with the character's name first. The collaborator reads the guardrail's Notes if you wrote any, and otherwise its name, so keep the whole rule in one of those two places.

## Connections between records

**What a connection is.** A **connection** is a link between two records that says how they are related, in a short phrase that reads like a sentence. A character *appears in* a scene card. A clue *is planted in* one scene and *pays off in* another. A song *is the theme song of* a character.

**Why it matters.** Connections are what Story Canon's checks are built from. The program knows who is in which scene because of *appears in*, where a clue is planted because of *is planted in*, and who knows a secret because of *knows* and *suspects*. When you move a scene, Story Canon warns you if the move would put an effect before its cause, because of *causes*. The more you connect, the more the program can catch for you.

**Do most writers need it?** Yes, a little. *Appears in* is the one nearly every book uses. The rest you add when a check would help you.

**Where it is.** On the **Notes and connections** page of every record. On a character, Connections are on the **Their world and their people** page.

### Reading the Connections section

Under the **Connections** heading, a gray line says what the section is for and adds *Each heading reads from here outward.* Your connections are gathered under headings by what they say, so fifteen *appears in* links become one heading with fifteen names under it:

> **Marisol Vega appears in …**
>
> The empty boat *scene card*
>
> Night watch *scene card*

Each row has the other record's name (a link; click it to open that record), its kind in gray, sometimes an **inferred** chip, and a **Remove** button that deletes that one connection (never the record).

Two headings are special:

- **Marisol Vega is in these relationships**: on a character, the relationship records that name them.
- **The people in it**: on a relationship, its characters.

*None yet.* means this record has no connections.

### Making a connection, step by step

At the end of the section is a box headed **Make a connection**. It reads like a sentence, left to right.

1. On the left is this record's name in bold, the start of the sentence.
2. In the first dropdown, choose the connection word. The words are grouped under gray headings (*Cause and effect*, *Who knows what* and so on). It starts on **relates to**.
3. In the second dropdown, choose the other record. Records are grouped by kind (Beats, Characters, Scene cards and so on) and listed in name order within each kind.
4. Read the line under the dropdowns. After **Reads:**, it shows the whole sentence in bold, such as **Marisol Vega appears in The empty boat**, and under it one line explaining the word. If the sentence reads wrong, change the word.
5. Click **Connect**.

The new connection appears in the list above. If the two are already connected that way, Story Canon says so and adds nothing.

### Which way a connection reads

Every connection has a direction. "The storm causes the shipwreck" is not the same as "the shipwreck causes the storm."

Story Canon stores each connection once and reads it the right way from both ends. That is why most words appear twice in the dropdown: once forward (*causes*) and once backward (*is caused by*). Pick whichever makes a true sentence starting with **this** record's name.

**Example.** You are on the place record *The lighthouse* and want to say the scene where the empty boat arrives happens there. Choose **is where … happens** and pick the scene card *The empty boat*. The line reads **The lighthouse is where The empty boat happens**. On the scene card's page, the same connection reads *The empty boat happens at The lighthouse*.

**The common mistake** is choosing the forward word from the wrong record, which makes a backward sentence ("the lighthouse happens at the empty boat"). Always read the **Reads:** line before you click **Connect**. If you already made a backward one, click **Remove** beside it and make it again.

A few words read the same both ways (*relates to*, *is the same person as*) and appear once.

### Which connection words there are

The dropdown in **Make a connection** groups its words under gray headings: *Cause and effect* (such as **causes** and **depends on**), *Setups, clues and secrets* (**plants**, **pays off**, **reveals**), *Who knows what* (**knows**, **suspects**), *Where and who* (**appears in**, **happens at**), *Echoes and sources*, *Music*, *Structure*, and *General* (**relates to**). The line under the dropdowns explains each word as you choose it, so you never need to learn them in advance. When you want the whole list, with what in Story Canon reads each word, see [Reference: every connection word](#reference-every-connection-word).

### The inferred chip

Some connections carry a small gray chip that says **inferred**. When any do, a line above the list explains it: the program worked the connection out from your planning (for example, a character named in a scene's plan) rather than you drawing it. Point at the chip with the mouse to see why it was made. A suggestion of a connection that you accept from the collaborator is also marked inferred.

- **Why it helps:** a book you bring in arrives already connected, so the checks work from day one.
- **What to do:** glance over them. Click **Remove** on any that is wrong. Leave the rest. An inferred connection counts exactly like one you made.

## Characters in depth: a character's pages

A character record holds everything you know about one person in your story: their job in the book, who they are in the world, how they look and sound, what they want and what stands in the way, what hurts them, and how they change. This part of the chapter walks through a character's pages in order, and teaches the craft behind them: Randy Ingermanson's character questions, Robert McKee's forces of antagonism, Angela Ackerman and Becca Puglisi's thesauri of wounds and traits, and K.M. Weiland's character arcs. You don't need any of that vocabulary to start. Every term is explained where it first appears.

### Starting and opening a character

- **To open a character:** click **Story Brain**, then **Characters** in the left list, then the character's name.
- **To start one:** on the Characters list, type a name in the box at the bottom (*A name is enough to start*) and click **Add character**. The new record opens on its first page, **Who they are**.
- **Plant the seed**, offered on a brand-new novel's home page, also starts your lead character from a few short questions, all in pencil (see [Where was I? Your novel's home page](#where-was-i-your-novels-home-page)). Its answers land on the character as **Who they are**, **Role** (*Lead*), **Goal**, **Conflict** and **What they stand to lose**, and its guess at the ending goes to the Story world.

The Characters list shows, for each person, how many facts are decided, developing and still open, and a few telling facts such as *Narrative importance: main*.

**The common mistake** is treating an empty record as a to-do list. A minor character with three good facts is complete. Depth is measured against how much the character matters to the book, never against how many fields are filled.

### A character's pages at a glance

| Page, in order | What it is for | Groups on it | Shown |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Who they are | Their job in the book. Fill this first. | Role in the story | Always |
| Identity | Who they are in the world, never guessed. | Identity; Beyond human (worldbuilding books only) | Always |
| Looks and voice | How they look, move and sound. | Appearance, Physical, Language and voice | Always |
| Personality | Temperament, traits and flaws, and who they remind you of. | Personality; Traits and flaws (Ackerman and Puglisi); Comparisons | Always |
| Goals, motives and values | What they are after, and what drives them beyond the plot. | Goal and motive (Ingermanson); Depth lenses | Always |
| What opposes them | Every force against them. | What works against them (McKee, forces of antagonism) | Only with *McKee (Story)* on in Novel setup |
| Inner life | Fears, wounds, and the gaps between their public and private selves. | Psychological; Wound and its fallout (Ackerman and Puglisi); Contradiction layers | Always |
| Arc | How they change, and how old they are in each scene. | Arcs; Across time | Always |
| Their world and their people | Home, history, and every connection. | Environmental; Connections | Always |
| Interview | Questions you ask them, answered in their voice. | Interview | Always |
| Notes, guardrails and writing aids | Scratch notes, rules, private tools, and the catch-all. | Notes; Guardrails; Writing aids (private); Other facts; My fields; Add your own field | Always |

So with McKee off, which is how most novels start, a character has ten pages, and **Inner life** is page 6, not 7. The numbers on your screen are the ones to trust.

Groups with a teacher's name come from a writing method and appear only when that method is on in Novel setup; see [Turning on a writing method for characters](#turning-on-a-writing-method-for-characters). On a novel you haven't set up yet, the **Goals, motives and values** page ends with a gray line, *More places to write come with the methods you choose in Novel setup, such as goal and motive (Ingermanson) or the wound and its fallout*, with an **Open Novel setup** link.

### Character page: Who they are

**What it is for.** The character's job in the book: what this person is *for*. It holds one group, **Role in the story**, and the program suggests filling it first, because it tells you how much of everything else this character needs.

| Field | What to write | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Role** | Their structural job: lead, co-lead, B-story lead (who carries the second storyline, often the one that teaches the theme), supporting, narrator, secret keeper, or a hidden engine driving events offstage. | Tells you how much page time and inner life they need. |
| **Who they are** | A sentence or two, the way you'd describe them to a friend who will meet them tomorrow. | Your anchor in chapter twenty when you're unsure how they'd react. |
| **Narrative importance** | Main, supporting or minor. | A measure of weight, not worth. Depth is judged against it. |
| **Narrates?** | Whether they tell part of the story, and how: first person ("I") or close third ("she," from inside her head). | Settles whose voice carries which chapters. |
| **Viewpoint withheld until** | For a secret keeper: the beat before which the reader never gets inside their head. | Keeps a secret from leaking through their thoughts. |
| **What they stand to lose** | What is gone for good if they fail. | Stakes make every obstacle tense. |
| **Last page, in one line** | Who they are on the last page, in one line, even before you know the ending. | Gives every scene a direction to push them. |

**Viewpoint withheld until, explained.** A **viewpoint** (or point of view) is the character whose eyes and thoughts the reader is inside for a scene. A **secret keeper** is a character who knows something the reader must not learn yet. If the reader is inside their head, the secret is hard to hide honestly. Write the name of a beat here, exactly as it appears on your Board, the planning space in Narrative Zoom (such as *All Is Lost*, Save the Cat's name for the story's lowest point; beats are taught in [Narrative Zoom](#narrative-zoom)). The Story review in Narrative Zoom (a list of questions Story Canon asks about your book) then asks about any scene told from inside this character's head before that beat. In *The Keeper of Gull Point*, Cyrus Hale is the secret keeper: his viewpoint is withheld until the beat where the truth about the boat comes out.

**What they stand to lose, done well.** Stakes are the cost of losing, not the reward for winning, and they land on this one person. "She could lose everything" is vague. "If the light is automated, Marisol loses the only reason she has to stay on the island, and the only place Eli still feels near" is a stake.

**Example.** Marisol Vega. *Role*: Lead. *Who they are*: "A woman who keeps a light for strangers so she never has to let anyone close." *Narrative importance*: main. *Narrates?*: Close third. *What they stand to lose*: the lighthouse, which the harbor board wants to automate. *Last page*: "She leaves the door unlocked, and someone comes in."

### Character page: Identity

**What it is for.** Who the character is in the world. The **Identity** group holds the facts people most often get wrong by assuming: **Gender identity**, **Pronouns**, **Sexual orientation**, **Romantic orientation, if it differs**, **Intersex or sex characteristics, if it matters**, **Race or ethnicity**, **Heritage, nationality and roots**, **Age**, **Year born**, **Age, if it never changes**, **Faith or religion**, **Culture and community**, **Disability, chronic illness or neurodivergence**, **Work**, and **Family**.

**How it works, and why.**

- **Nothing is ever guessed.** These fields say *Not yet specified* until you write something. The program never works out a character's gender from a name, or anything from another field.
- **Your words, always.** Some fields offer a row of common answers when you click **Add** (*Woman*, *Man*, *Nonbinary* and more for gender; *she/her*, *they/them* and more for pronouns). Click one, or type your own words. The buttons are a convenience, never a list you must choose from.
- **Timing.** Every field here offers the **Timing** dropdown, because pronouns, orientation, age, disability, work and family can change over a story. Someone can be a nurse before the story and out of work during it, both decided.
- **What reads these fields.** *Gender identity* (or *Pronouns*, if there is no gender) is what the Bechdel test in the Cast view uses (a quick test of whether two named women talk to each other about something other than a man). *Year born* lets the program work out the character's age in every scene that has a year (see [Character page: Arc](#character-page-arc)). Nothing else is worked out from identity.

**Beyond human.** In a worldbuilding book, this page also has **Beyond human**: *Kind of being* (connect the character to its record with **is a** for everything their kind shares), *Their own powers or abilities* and *Their own limits and weaknesses*.

**The common mistake** is filling every identity field for every walk-on character. If it doesn't matter to the story, leave it unspecified. That is a choice too.

**Example.** For Marisol: *Gender identity*: Woman. *Pronouns*: she/her. *Year born*: 1983. *Heritage, nationality and roots*: "Born on Gull Point; her mother's family came from the Azores." *Work*: Assistant keeper (*Before the story*); Head keeper (*True when the story opens*). *Family*: "Parents dead; younger brother Eli drowned twelve years ago."

### Character page: Looks and voice

**What it is for.** How they look, move and sound, so you stay consistent across a long draft (her eyes were gray in chapter two). It is not a description to copy onto the page.

- **Appearance**: *The overall look*, *Skin tone*, *Hair*, *Eyes*, *Height*, *Build and body*, *Distinctive features* and *How they dress*. Like identity, most say *Not yet specified* until you write them, and hair, build and dress can carry timing.
- **Physical**: *Physical notes*, a list: health, energy, how they move.
- **Language and voice**: *Languages they speak* (and how well), *Accent and how they sound*, and *Sayings and verbal habits*. Rhythm and word choice carry a voice better than spelled-out dialect.

**Example.** Marisol: *Hair*: "Gray-streaked, always braided" (*True when the story opens*). *Distinctive features*: "Rope burns across both palms." *Physical notes*: "Climbs the 112 stairs twice a night without thinking." *Sayings*: "Tide doesn't care."

### Character page: Personality

**What it is for.** Their everyday texture, and the traits that will drive their choices.

- **Personality**: *Temperament* (their natural emotional weather), *Humor*, *Habits and props* (a list; make sure each habit belongs to the right character), *Strengths*, *Weaknesses*.
- **Traits and flaws** *(Ackerman and Puglisi)*, with the thesauri method on: **Positive traits** (with where each came from, and how it shows without being named) and **Flaws** (with what each one protects them from, and what it costs). A flaw usually protects against the wound. The thesauri are explained under [Character page: Inner life](#character-page-inner-life).
- **Comparisons**: *Like…*, a real person or character whose qualities you are borrowing. Note which qualities, so it never becomes a copy.

**Example.** Marisol. *Temperament*: "Still water; storms underneath." *Flaw*: "Self-reliance, which protects her from being left and costs her every friendship on the island." *Like…*: "The stubbornness of a lighthouse keeper's widow in an old newspaper story, without the grief."

### Character page: Goals, motives and values

**What it is for.** What the character is after, and what drives them beyond their job in the plot. It holds two groups.

#### Goal and motive: Randy Ingermanson's character questions

**Who he is.** Randy Ingermanson is a novelist and teacher best known for the **Snowflake Method**, a way of designing a novel by growing it outward from one sentence, the way a snowflake grows from a speck. One of its early steps is a short sheet for each main character. The **Goal and motive** group is that sheet. It appears when you turn on *Snowflake (Ingermanson)* in Novel setup.

**The fields, taught in order:**

- **Values.** What the character holds most dear. Ingermanson's prompt: finish "Nothing is more important than ___" as many ways as you can. This field is a list: keep every answer.
  - *Why it works:* the two or three values that conflict with each other are the engine of the character. Someone who values both loyalty and honesty will be torn when a friend lies.
  - *How it fails:* values that never collide. Then nothing inside the character is at stake.
- **Ambition.** The abstract hunger underneath everything: to be respected, to be safe, to matter.
- **Goal.** The concrete, reachable thing they are trying to get in this story. You should be able to picture the moment they win it.
  - *Why the two are separate:* the goal is what they chase; the ambition is why it matters. The gap between them is where the truest choices come from. A character who wins the goal and finds the ambition still hungry has a story.
- **Conflict.** What stands between them and the goal, in a line.
- **Epiphany.** What they learn by the end.

**Example.** Marisol. *Values*: nothing is more important than keeping the light; than owing no one; than Eli's memory. *Ambition*: to be safe from loss. *Goal*: keep the lighthouse from being automated. *Conflict*: Cyrus Hale and the harbor board, and her own refusal to ask anyone for help. *Epiphany*: the light was never what kept her safe.

**How it connects.** Plant the seed's answers to "What do they want?" and "What stands in the way?" go into *Goal* and *Conflict*. Ingermanson's Goal is often the same thing as Weiland's Want seen from outside (see [Arcs: how a character changes](#arcs-how-a-character-changes)): the Want is the chase the false belief drives; the Goal is the target as the plot sees it.

#### The Depth lenses group

**What they are.** Questions that test whether a character is alive beyond their job in the plot. A **lens** here just means a way of looking at the character. This group is always there. Use it on anyone who feels flat.

- **What they think they want**: the want they'd admit to.
- **What drives them underneath**: the need they don't see in themselves.
- **Under pressure**: how they behave under pressure, uncertainty, success and failure.
- **Comic engine**: what reliably makes them funny on the page (the flaw, habit or contradiction that keeps producing laughs).
- **Voice**: how they sound as a whole: vocabulary, rhythm, what they notice, how they joke.
- **With different people**: how they act with a rival, an ally, family, a stranger. A list.
- **Agency**: what they make happen, rather than react to.

**The common mistake** is a character who only reacts. If *Agency* is hard to fill, the plot is pushing the character around.

**Example.** Ana Ruiz. *What she thinks she wants*: "A quiet posting where nobody needs her too much." *Under pressure*: "Gets calmer and kinder, then falls apart alone afterward." *Agency*: "She is the one who notices the dead man's hands were not a sailor's."

### Character page: What opposes them

**What it is for.** Every force working against the character, and how dark their story can get. It holds one group, **What works against them** *(McKee, forces of antagonism)*, and appears when you turn on *McKee (Story)* in Novel setup.

**Who he is.** Robert McKee is a screenwriting teacher whose book *Story* is one of the most widely read books on structure. One of his central ideas is the **principle of antagonism**: a protagonist and a story can only be as interesting as the forces against them.

**The idea.** Every story is about a **value**: something characters care about that can swing positive or negative, such as love, justice, respect or safety. McKee asks two things of the forces against a character: that they come from every level of the character's life, and that they push the value as far down as your book allows. The second part, how far a value can fall, is a ladder of four steps, ending at its very worst form; it is taught in [Reference: McKee's value ladder](#reference-mckees-value-ladder). You can fill in these fields without it.

**The three levels of opposition.** The forces against a character come from three places:

- **Inner**: from inside them: fear, pride, a wound, a false belief.
- **Personal**: from people close to them: family, lovers, friends, rivals.
- **Extra-personal**: from the wider world: institutions, society, nature, chance.

A full story uses all three.

**The fields:**

- **The value they live by**: the value under pressure in their own story.
- **Inner force**, **Personal force**, **Extra-personal force**: what works against them at each level.
- **The worst form of their value**: the value pushed past its opposite to the limit, worse in kind and not only in degree (McKee calls it the negation of the negation), set for your book's tone. A cozy mystery's darkest point is different from a tragedy's.

**Why it helps.** It shows you whether your opposition is strong enough. If one of the three forces is empty, or the value never reaches its worst form, the middle of the book may sag.

**The common mistake** is making the villain the only force. For Marisol, the strongest force against her is Marisol.

**Example.** Marisol. *Value*: trust. *Inner*: her belief that anyone she cares about will leave. *Personal*: Ana, who keeps coming back no matter how often Marisol sends her away. *Extra-personal*: the harbor board automating the light, and the sea itself. *Worst form*: trusting no one, not even herself, and calling it keeping watch.

**Conflict versus What works against them.** Ingermanson's *Conflict* is what blocks the goal, in a line. McKee's group breaks the opposition into its three levels and follows the value to the bottom. Use either or both.

### Character page: Inner life

**What it is for.** What happens inside the character: fears, wounds, desires, and the gaps between who they seem to be and who they are. It holds three groups.

#### The Psychological group

Always there: **Fears**, **Wounds**, **Desires** and **Beliefs**, the raw material of motivation. *Wounds* is for every old hurt, of any size; it offers Timing.

#### Wound and its fallout: the Ackerman and Puglisi thesauri

**Who they are.** Angela Ackerman and Becca Puglisi run the Writers Helping Writers site and wrote a series of reference books called thesauri. *The Emotion Thesaurus* lists how each emotion shows in the body, the mind and behavior. *The Emotional Wound Thesaurus* traces painful past events through the beliefs and behaviors they leave behind. *The Positive Trait Thesaurus* and *The Negative Trait Thesaurus* explain where traits come from and how they show. All of them teach one thing: **show, don't tell.** Don't write "she was afraid"; write what fear does to her hands.

Turning on *The Writers Helping Writers thesauri (Ackerman and Puglisi)* in Novel setup adds three groups: **Traits and flaws** on the **Personality** page, **Wound and its fallout** here, and **Emotion on the page** on each scene card (see [Scene cards](#scene-card-records)).

**Wound and its fallout** follows one defining wound through its effects:

- **The wounding event**: what happened, and how old they were. (Weiland calls this the Ghost.)
- **What they came to believe because of it**: the false belief (Weiland's Lie).
- **What they fear will happen again**.
- **What they avoid or guard against now**: a list.
- **How it shows in what they do**: a list of behaviors the reader can see.
- **What sets it off**: a list of triggers.

*Why it works:* the last three fields are things you can put in a scene. A reader never needs to be told the wound if they watch her refuse every offered hand. *How it fails:* a wound that explains nothing she does on the page.

**Example.** Marisol. *Wounding event*: at 29, she argued with Eli and told him to go if he wanted to; he took the skiff out into a squall and never came back. *Came to believe*: anyone I care about will leave. *Fears*: being the one left again. *Avoids*: making plans with anyone; going down to the harbor. *How it shows*: does every task herself; won't let Ana stay the night when the road floods. *What sets it off*: anyone saying "I'll be back."

#### The Contradiction layers group

Complex characters live in the gaps between who they seem to be and who they are. This group, always there, has four fields in two pairs: **Public identity** beside **Private identity**, and **What they do** beside **Why they really do it**.

**Example.** Cyrus Hale. Public: the steady harbor master everyone trusts with their boats. Private: a man terrified his son will be found out. What he does: pushes hard to automate the lighthouse. Why: an automated light means no keeper watching the rocks at night.

### Character page: Arc

**What it is for.** How the character changes, and how old they are across the book. It holds two sections: **Arcs**, explained fully in [Arcs: how a character changes](#arcs-how-a-character-changes), and **Across time**, explained here.

#### Across time: ages and other selves

**Across time** appears once the character has a **Year born**, an **Age, if it never changes**, or another record connected as **is the same person as**.

- **The same person at other ages.** When one person appears at different ages as separate characters (the nineteen-year-old in the flashbacks and the grown woman), give each self its own record and connect them with **is the same person as**. Each self's page then lists the others under *The same person at other ages*, with their ages.
- **Age in each scene.** With a *Year born* on the character, a *Year* on a scene card (on its **When it happens** page), and the character connected to the scene card with **appears in** (or set as its viewpoint), the page lists, under *Age in each scene*, their age in every scene. Until then it says *Give scenes a year, and connect this character to them, to see their age in each.*
- **Someone who never ages.** A ghost, a remembered younger self: use *Age, if it never changes*, and the page says, for example, *Always 19.*

**The common mistake** is leaving the scene card's **Year** empty. Without it there is nothing to count from.

**Example.** Marisol, born 1983, appears in the scene card *The empty boat*, whose *Year* is 2024: the page lists her at 41. Her writer also makes a record called "Young Marisol," with *Age, if it never changes*: 19, connected to Marisol as **is the same person as**. Young Marisol appears only in the summer flashback of 2002, when she teaches seven-year-old Eli to row, so her page says *Always 19.* and each self's page lists the other under *The same person at other ages*.

### Character page: Their world and their people

**What it is for.** Where they come from, and everyone and everything they are tied to.

- **Environmental**: *Home and neighborhood* and *History* (past events, one per entry), both with Timing.
- **Connections**: every connection to this character, including the scene cards they appear in and **is in these relationships**. Connections work the same on every record; see [Connections between records](#connections-between-records).

### Character page: Interview

**What it is for.** Questions you ask the character, answered in their own voice. It is explained in [Interviewing a character](#interviewing-a-character).

### Character page: Notes, guardrails and writing aids

**What it is for.** Everything around the character rather than inside them.

- **Notes**: your scratch paper (see [Notes on a record](#notes-on-a-record)).
- **Guardrails**: what must stay true about this character (see [Guardrails for one character](#guardrails-for-one-character)).
- **Writing aids (private)**: tools just for you, marked *(private, never exported)*. **Casting tuning fork** is an actor whose voice and tempo help you hear the character while you write (a tuning fork gives a musician the right note). It is never a description of the character. **Theme music** and **Playlist** are songs that put you back inside them. Private fields never leave the program in a manuscript export. To put a song *in the book*, add it to the **Soundtrack** and connect it with **is the theme song of** instead.

When a fact here has a proper home (a fear belongs in *Fears*), write it there and **Set aside** the copy here.
- **My fields** and **Add your own field** (see [Adding your own fields](#adding-your-own-fields)).

## Arcs: how a character changes

**What an arc is.** An **arc** is the change a character goes through inside, from who they are at the start to who they are at the end. Plots resolve; arcs are what make readers feel the ending meant something.

**Where arcs live.** Each arc is a record of its own, kept under its character. There is no Arcs kind in the left list. Open the character and turn to its **Arc** page.

Story Canon builds arcs on the teaching of **K.M. Weiland**, a novelist and writing teacher whose book *Creating Character Arcs* breaks inner change into a few linked parts. Her idea is simple and strong: a character changes because they start out believing something false, and the story forces them to test that belief until they either let it go or are destroyed by it.

### Weiland's arc, one part at a time

The parts form a chain. Each one explains the next. Learn them in this order.

**The Ghost.** Something in the character's past that shaped them, often called the **wound**. It must have happened before the story starts.

- *Why it works:* it makes the false belief (the Lie) believable. Readers forgive a character's mistakes when they understand where the mistakes come from.
- *How it fails:* a Ghost chosen for drama that has nothing to do with the Lie, or one so vague it explains nothing ("something bad happened once").
- *It need not be tragic.* It needs to be personal and convincing. Being praised only when she won, every time, for twelve years, is a Ghost.

**The Lie.** The false belief the character holds about themselves or how life works, taken from the Ghost. Once, it protected them. Now it holds them back.

- *Why it works:* it is the engine of the whole arc. It explains why the character keeps making the wrong choice, and its defeat is what the ending means.
- *How it fails:* a Lie that is only a mistake about facts ("she thinks the ferry leaves at noon"). A Lie is a belief about how life works, and it must feel true to them.
- *Write it in their voice,* as a sentence they would say if they were honest.

**The Want.** What the Lie makes the character chase: usually an outer goal they believe will fix things.

- *Why it works:* it drives the plot and gives the reader something to root for.
- *How it fails:* a Want that would heal them if they got it. Then there is no inner conflict.

**The Need.** What would truly heal them. Usually the opposite of the Want, and tied to letting the Lie go.

- *Why it works:* it is where the arc is heading. When the character reaches for the Need at the cost of the Want, the change becomes visible on the page.
- *How it fails:* a Need written as a moral ("she needs to learn a lesson"). A Need is a change in what they reach for.

**The Truth.** The true belief that replaces the Lie: the exact answer to it.

- *Why it works:* it is the theme of the book made personal to this one person.
- *How it fails:* a Truth that is a slogan ("love conquers all") instead of the answer to this character's Lie.

**One example, all the way through.** Marisol Vega.

- *Ghost:* the night she told Eli to go if he wanted to, and he took the skiff out and drowned.
- *Lie:* "Anyone I care about will leave."
- *Want:* never to need anyone.
- *Need:* to let someone stay.
- *Truth:* "Some people stay. Letting them is my part."

Read it back as a chain: because of the Ghost, she believes the Lie; because of the Lie, she wants the Want; what she needs is the opposite; the Truth is what she finally knows. If any link doesn't lead to the next, that link is the one to rewrite.

### The three arc types

Weiland describes three shapes an arc can take. You choose one in the arc's **Arc type** field.

| Arc type | What happens | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Positive Change** | The character starts with the Lie, fights it, and ends holding the Truth. The most common arc. | Marisol lets Ana stay. |
| **Flat** | The character already holds the Truth at the start. They don't change much; they change the world around them, and are tested hard along the way. | Ana, who already believes people can be trusted, in a town that has stopped believing it. |
| **Negative Change** | The character ends worse. Weiland describes three kinds: they learn the Truth too late and it is bitter (disillusionment), they reject the Truth and cling to the Lie (a fall), or they start with the Truth and trade it for a Lie (corruption). | Cyrus, who protects his son by lying until the lie costs him the son. |

**The common mistake** is assuming every arc must be Positive. Flat arcs drive many beloved books, and Negative arcs make tragedies. Another mistake is calling an arc Flat when the character is simply unchanged *and* changes nothing; a Flat character must still push on the world.

### Adding an arc, step by step

1. Open the character and click the **Arc** tab.
2. Click **Add an arc**. A box appears: **What part of life is this arc about?**
3. Type a **value**: one word or a short phrase for the quality that will change, such as *trust*, *courage* or *belonging*. The box suggests *A value, such as trust, courage or belonging*. You can leave it empty.
4. Click **Add the arc**. (Click **Cancel** to stop.)

The arc opens on its own page, named for the character and the value (*Marisol Vega: trust*), or *arc 2* in place of the value if you left it empty. The value is saved in pencil. Fill in the rest there. **Back to Marisol Vega** at the top of the arc's page takes you back.

**Why the value comes first.** An arc follows one value as it changes. "Trust" is something you can watch rise and fall scene by scene; "growth" is not. A character can carry more than one arc, each on a different value: Marisol could have an arc on trust and another on grief. Until a character has an arc, the section says *No arc yet. A character can carry more than one, each on a different value.*

### The arc card on the Arc page

Under **Arcs**, each arc appears as a card:

- **The arc's title**, such as *Marisol Vega: trust*. Click it to open the arc's own page.
- **Open and change**, a button on the right that opens the same page. The card itself is for reading; you change an arc on its page.
- **The arc type** as a chip, such as **Positive Change**, or *Arc type not set*. Then *About trust.* (what the arc is about), or *What it is about is not set.*
- **The chain**, in columns: **Ghost**, then **which leads to the Lie**, **which leads to the Want**, **which leads to the Need**. Each shows what is written, with *(Developing)* after anything in pencil. An empty part shows as an empty ruled line; click it to open the arc's page.

The Truth, the tests and the ending are on the arc's page, not on the card.

If your novel does not use Weiland's method, the card says *Open the arc to write where it starts, how it turns and who they are at the end. Weiland's Ghost, Lie, Want and Need come with Weiland in Novel setup.* Turn on *Weiland (character arcs)* under **My storytelling toolkit** in Novel setup to get the chain. (A novel you haven't set up yet shows the chain anyway.)

### The arc's own page

An arc is a record like any other, with two pages: **1 The arc** and **2 Notes and connections**. Page 1 has two groups.

**Arc**: the arc's shape.

- **Value this arc is about**: the quality that changes.
- **Arc type**: click **Add** and choose **Positive Change**, **Flat**, **Negative Change** or **Not decided yet** from the buttons under the box, or write your own words.
- **Key tests and turns**: the moments where the character chooses between the Lie and the Truth. A list: one entry per test. Let some of them fail; a character who passes every test hasn't changed, they were already fine.
- **Who they are at the end**: the inner state at the end, not the plot outcome. "Lets people in, even when it is inconvenient," not "saves the lighthouse."
- **Still unresolved**: loose ends you know about. Some stay open on purpose.

**Lie and truth** *(Weiland)*: **Ghost** (with Timing), **Lie**, **Want**, **Need** and **Truth**, as taught above.

Every field can hold several ideas in pencil side by side. That is the right way to work on a Lie: write three wordings, live with them, decide one.

**Example.** On *Marisol Vega: trust*, *Key tests and turns*: "Refuses Ana's offer to help search the boat (fails)." "Lets Ana sleep in the keeper's cottage during the storm (passes)." "Tells Ana about the night Eli drowned (passes)." *Who they are at the end*: "Someone who leaves a door open."

### Reading two Lies as a pair

Under the arc cards, **Read the Lies as a pair with** has a dropdown of your other characters. Choose one, and a box called **The two Lies, read as a pair** shows both characters' Lies, one under the other. A character with no Lie yet shows *No Lie written yet.*

**Why it helps.** Two characters set side by side tell the reader something about each other. Sometimes their Lies pull in opposite directions, so each one's choices make the other's visible (writers call that pair **foils**). Sometimes their Lies are the same belief in different clothes (**doubles**). Neither is wrong, but you should know which you have. Both words, and a third, **mirrors**, are explained in [Reference: foils, doubles and mirrors](#reference-foils-doubles-and-mirrors).

**The name-swap test.** Tick **Read with the names swapped**. The two Lies are shown with the characters' first names exchanged. If you can swap the names and nothing changes, the two are doubles, not foils. If you meant them to be foils, push one Lie until it pulls the other way.

The swap is only a reading test. It changes nothing you wrote. It swaps first names only, so a "her" may read oddly; read past it.

**Example.** Marisol's Lie: "Anyone I care about will leave." Ana's Lie: "If I stay anywhere too long, I'll be trapped." Swapped, each sentence is plainly wrong for the other woman: they are foils. One fears being left; the other fears staying. Their scenes will generate friction without your having to force it.

## Interviewing a character

**What it is.** On a character's **Interview** page, under the heading **Interview** followed by the character's name, you ask the character questions and answer in their own voice.

**Why it works.** A character who can answer personal questions honestly is a person. One who can't is still a plot device. Answering in the first person, as them, gets you out of your own head.

**Do most writers need it?** No, but many find it the fastest way to unstick a flat character. Try it on anyone you can't hear yet.

**What you see.** At first, a line: *Ask* the character's name *questions and answer in their own voice. Answers stay in pencil.* Then a button for each ready-made set of questions, each with a line about it, and a box for your own question.

### Adding a question set, step by step

1. Click **Add the questions:** followed by the set's name.
2. The set's questions appear under its name, each with a writing box that says *As* the character's name*…*
3. Click in a box and answer as the character, in their words.
4. Click anywhere outside the box. The answer is kept in pencil, as Developing. Change it any time; earlier answers are kept.

Once a set is added, its button disappears, so a set can't be added twice.

### Interview set: Getting to know them

Ten questions that get under the surface: the first thing they remember, who taught them how to love, what they want people to think when they walk into a room, what they lie about most often, what they'd never forgive, what they're most ashamed of, a perfect ordinary day, what they're afraid someone will find out, who they miss, and what they'd do with one more day. Answer as the character, in their words, not yours.

**Example.** Marisol, *Who do you miss?* "Nobody. I don't do that." Her writer leaves the answer exactly as Marisol gave it, because the lie is the answer.

### Interview set: The villain interview

A villain exercise taught by the writer Dara Resnik. Five questions about childhood: the happiest memory, the first friend and where they are now, the biggest fear when small, what makes them cry, and who hurt them most as a child.

**How to use it.** Answer as the villain, who believes they are right, helping, or saving someone, and that the cost is worth it. Then say the answers out loud without looking. If the villain can't answer as honestly as your hero, you have written an obstacle, not a person.

**Example.** Cyrus Hale, *What makes you cry?* "My boy, asleep, when he was small. Before he did the thing I've spent twelve years covering." He is still the antagonist. Now he is also a father.

### Your own interview questions

Type a question in **A question of your own** and click **Ask**. It joins the page under **Your own questions**, with its own answer box.

## Fill in one line on a low-energy day

**What it is.** A small line on every character, just above the page tabs: *Low-energy day? One line counts.* with a **Fill in one line** button.

**Why it matters.** Some days you can't write a chapter. You can almost always write one line about a person you love. That still counts as work on your book, and it keeps you in touch with the story.

**How to do it.**

1. Click **Fill in one line**.
2. The program picks one empty field for you, at random, from the **Physical**, **Personality**, **Environmental** and **Psychological** groups. It turns to that field's page and opens its writing box, with the cursor ready.
3. Write a line, choose a status if you like, and click **Save**.

Just one. You are done. The button disappears once those four groups have something in every field.

**Example.** On a tired Sunday, Marisol's writer clicks **Fill in one line** and gets **Humor**. She writes "Laughs only at the weather forecast," saves it in pencil, and closes the laptop.

## The other kinds of records

Every other kind works exactly like a character: the same name, Kept for you, legend, pages, fields, margin and connections. Each kind's full list of fields, with its teaching, is in [Every kind and every field](#every-kind-and-every-field).

### Relationship records

**What it is for.** What is between two or more characters. A relationship record lets you track how a bond moves over the book, which a character record alone can't.

**Its pages.** **1 The relationship** and **2 Notes and connections**.

**Making one.** Add it on the **Relationships** list (*Add relationship*), then connect each person to it with **relates to** (from the relationship's page, choose **relates to** and the character). The Cast view in Narrative Zoom can also make one for two people who share scenes, with the people already connected.

**What's on it.**

- **Between** followed by the people's names, under the name of the record, each a link to their page. Until you connect them it says *Connect the people in it under Connections (relates to), and they are named here.*
- **Where they stand**: **Trust**, **Respect**, **Closeness**, **Resentment**, **Dependence**, **Rivalry**, and **Balance of power**. A word or a short line is enough, such as "high, and slipping." These change over a story, so each has **Timing**: add a new entry as it changes, and mark when each is true.
- **Underneath**: what they share underneath, the opposition on the surface, where the relationship is really going, hidden influence, false assumptions, and who knows what the other doesn't.
- **Between them**: **What they call each other** (names nobody else uses) and **Words only they share**.

On each person's page, the relationships they are in are listed in Connections under **is in these relationships**.

**The common mistake** is describing a relationship once and never tracking how it moves. A relationship that changes over the book should have more than one *Trust* entry by the end, each with its timing.

**Example.** Marisol and Ana. *Trust*: "none" (*True when the story opens*); "grudging, then real" (*Happens during the story*). *Balance of power*: "Ana, because she keeps choosing to come back." *What they share underneath*: both have run from something. *What they call each other*: "Doctor" and "Keeper," long after they know each other's names.

### Storyline records

**What it is for.** One thread of plot that runs through the book. **1 The storyline**: *Letter (A, B, C…)* (optional; the letter is its role, not its identity), *Story type* (Blake Snyder's type for this thread; see [Story types and their ingredients](#story-types-and-their-ingredients)), *Where it stands* (Introduced, Developing, Escalating, Dormant, Resolved and so on, or your own words), *Resolves now, or held open on purpose*, *Values it carries*, and *Promises made to the reader*. Then **2 Notes and connections**. The Storylines view in Narrative Zoom reads **moves forward** connections to show which scenes push each thread.

**Example.** *Who sent the boat* (A story): who set the empty boat adrift toward Gull Point, and why. *Marisol and Ana* (B story): two women learning to stay.

### Scene card records

**What it is for.** Your plan for one scene, before or after you write it. Scene cards are laid out on the Board in Narrative Zoom, placed on their beats; each one also has a record here, with everything you know about it. The Scene cards list says so at the top, with an **Open Narrative Zoom** link. A scene card has up to seven pages, in this order. Most cards have five, because two of the pages appear only with a choice turned on in Novel setup, and the numbers close up when a page is left out:

| Page, in order | What it holds |
| --- | --- |

| **When it happens** | **Story time**: *Day in the story* (a number on your story clock), *Date*, *Year* (which gives everyone's age here), *Time of day*, *Time since the previous scene*. **Timeline strand** (*Which timeline*), with a dual timeline or frame story. |

| **The reader** | **What the reader knows**: what the reader suspects by the end, and whether this is the visible or hidden story. **Reader response**: what you want the reader to feel (*Profound*, *Funny*, *Relatable*, *Shocking*, or your own word). **Callbacks**, with running gags on: callbacks and running gags here. |
| **Scene and sequel** | Appears with *Scene and Sequel (Swain)*. Dwight Swain taught that a story alternates a **scene** (goal, conflict, disaster) with a **sequel** (reaction, dilemma, decision), and that the decision becomes the next scene's goal. Fields: *Scene or sequel*, *Reaction*, *Dilemma*, *Decision*. |
| **Notes and connections** | Notes, Connections (who **appears in** it, where it **happens at**), and your own fields. |

When you open a scene card in Narrative Zoom instead of here, one or two pages come first (**Where it sits**, and **The page so far** once the card has a page in the Writing Room), and these pages follow them, numbered after them. So the same page can carry a different number in Narrative Zoom than here. See [Zoom: a scene card page](#zoom-a-scene-card-page).

**Example.** *The empty boat*: *What this scene must do*: "Bring the mystery to Marisol's door, literally." *Value at the start*: "Safety +." *Value at the end*: "Safety -." *Year*: 2024. *Weather*: "Fog so thick the light is useless."

### Beat records

**What it is for.** One turning point from your book's beat sheet, as it plays out in this book. A beat has up to three pages:

- **In this book**: *What this beat does in this book*, *What it is (your plan)*, *How it could happen* (a list of ideas side by side), *One way it could be done* (examples, never decisions), *Disaster, discovery or win*, *Caused by the hero's own efforts?*, and *Scene type*.
- **Questions for this beat**: the questions Save the Cat (Blake Snyder's beat sheet, adapted for novels by Jessica Brody) asks at this beat. The tab says **Questions for this beat**; the heading inside the page credits where they come from, **Save the Cat's questions for this beat**. Only some beats have them: the Theme Stated beat asks for the line that states the theme; the Setup asks about the hero's *Home*, *Work* and *Play* and *Six things that need fixing*; the Debate asks its question; Break into Two asks for the hero's WANT and Break into Three for the NEED; the Midpoint and All Is Lost ask whether it is a false victory or false defeat (and All Is Lost asks for the *Whiff of death*); and the Final Image asks whether the hero is changed or rejects change. This page appears only on beats that have questions, and only when Save the Cat is your structure (or the novel is not set up yet). On any other beat, Notes and connections comes straight after In this book.
- **Notes and connections**.

When you open a beat in Narrative Zoom instead of here, two pages come first, **About this beat** and **Scenes here**, and these pages follow them, numbered after them. See [Zoom: a beat page](#zoom-a-beat-page).

The Beats list explains when some beats have smaller steps inside them. At the bottom of a beat is **Remove this beat from the novel** instead of Delete (see [Delete at the bottom of a record](#delete-at-the-bottom-of-a-record)). To choose or change the beat sheet itself, see [Choosing a beat sheet for a novel](#choosing-a-beat-sheet-for-a-novel).

### Event and reveal records

**What they are for.** An **event** is something that happens in the story world, on the page or offstage. A **reveal** is the moment a hidden truth comes out. Both have three pages:

- **1 What happens**: *What it must accomplish* (the dramatic job, which can be decided while how it happens stays open) and *How it could happen* (ideas side by side until you choose one).
- **2 When, and who knows**: *When it happens in the story*; then *What actually happened*, *Who knows, and when* (a list), *What the reader knows or suspects*, and *Visible or hidden story*.
- **3 Notes and connections**.

**Example.** The event *The night Eli drowned*. *What it must accomplish*: "Give Marisol a reason to believe everyone leaves." *What actually happened*: Still open. *Who knows, and when*: "Cyrus, from the start."

### Place records

**What it is for.** One location. **1 The place**: *What it is like*, *Atmosphere*, *Season* (with Timing), *Who is usually here*, *Details to reuse* (a cracked step, a smell, a sound), and *What it means to the story* (why the story happens here and not somewhere else). In a worldbuilding book it also has **Settlement** (who governs, how people earn a living, roads and power, defenses, districts, who is rich and poor, the sounds and smells) and **Between worlds** (which world it is in, doorways, the rules for crossing). Then **2 Notes and connections**.

**Example.** *The lighthouse*: *Details to reuse*: "The 112th stair that rings like a bell," "Eli's initials scratched in the lamp room." *Port Ellery*: *What it means*: "The place Marisol can see from her window every night and never goes."

### Clue and payoff records

**What they are for.** A **clue** is a detail you plant that points to a hidden truth. A **payoff** is the later moment that rewards something set up earlier. Both have **1 The clue** (or **1 The payoff**) and **2 Notes and connections**. The first page holds *What it really means*, *Significant enough that it must pay off?* (with the buttons *Yes, it must pay off*, *No, it is texture*, *Not sure yet*), *Where it is planted* and *Where it pays off* (lists, as notes in your words), and *What it is mistaken for*. With misdirection and red herrings chosen in Novel setup, it adds **Red herring**: *Is this a red herring?* and *What it draws attention away from*.

The Setups and payoffs check in Narrative Zoom reads the **plants** and **pays off** connections, not the notes. To have a clue checked, connect it to its scene cards too.

**Example.** The clue *The cut mooring line*: *What it really means*: "Someone wanted the boat to drift to Gull Point." Connected: **is planted in** *The empty boat*, **pays off in** *Cyrus at the harbor office*.

### Kept line records

**What it is for.** A line of dialogue or prose you want to use somewhere in the book but haven't placed. Good lines arrive at odd times: on a walk, while drafting a different chapter, in the middle of a chat.

**Its pages.** **1 The kept line**: **The line** (*Said by (in the story)* and *How it is delivered*: emphasis, timing, the small action a speaker does while talking) and **Where it goes** (*Where it might go*, possible places, side by side in pencil). Then **2 Notes and connections**, which starts with a gray note, *Keeping a line does not place it. To tie it to a scene, connect it with "appears in" and the scene card.*, and a **Tie it to a scene** button.

The Kept lines list shows each line and who says it. You can also keep a line straight from a conversation, which makes a record here with the chip **Written by you, kept by you, placement open** (or the collaborator's name, if it wrote the line).

**Placing a kept line, step by step:**

1. Open the kept line and turn to **2 Notes and connections**.
2. Click **Tie it to a scene**. The **Make a connection** box comes into view with **appears in** already chosen.
3. Choose the scene card in the second dropdown.
4. Click **Connect**.

The line now shows on that scene card's record (under *… has in it*) and in Narrative Zoom, so you find it when you plan that scene. Placing it in your prose is still your job: you type it into the manuscript where it lands best.

**The common mistake** is forcing a kept line into a scene because you love it. A line that fits nowhere in this book can wait for the next one.

**Example.** "The sea gives back what it wants, not what you ask for." Marisol's writer ties it to two scene cards, *The empty boat* and the last chapter, and decides in revision which one earns it.

### Motif, theme and song records

- **Motifs**: an image, object, sound or phrase that comes back and gathers meaning. **1 The motif** has *What it means*, a list, for how its meaning changes across the book. (The light itself is Marisol's motif: a warning, then a refuge, then a welcome.)
- **Themes**: an idea the story explores underneath what happens. **1 The theme** has one field, *Description*.
- **Soundtrack**: one song that matters to the story. **1 The song** has *Artist*, *Year it came out*, *What it carries* (the feeling or memory it holds; titles only, never lyrics) and *When it plays*. Connect a song with **plays in** or **is the theme song of**, and the Soundtrack view in Narrative Zoom shows it.

Each also has **2 Notes and connections**.

### Objects, values, story rules, research, acts and sequences

These kinds keep things simple: **1** a page named for the kind (*The object*, *The value*, *The story rule*, *The research*, *The act*, *The sequence*) with one field, **Description**, then **2 Notes and connections**. Add your own fields with **Every** followed by the kind's name when you want more. Acts and sequences each have a line at the top of their list saying how they relate to Narrative Zoom.

### Set piece records

**What it is for.** A big, memorable sequence readers will describe to friends. **1 The set piece**: *The story job it performs*, *Storylines it moves* (a list) and *One card or a sequence*. Then **2 Notes and connections**. Many set pieces are worth marking **Mine to choreograph** in Kept for you.

**Example.** *The storm rescue*: Marisol rows out alone, in the storm she has feared for twelve years, to bring Ana back.

### Secret operations and hidden moves

**What it is.** A **hidden move** is something a character does in secret, offstage, that the reader does not see yet: a cut line, a forged log, a boat towed out at night. **Secret operations** is the list of all of them: the hidden story running under the visible one, in its true order. Only you see it. It never appears in viewpoint summaries.

**Why it matters.** When someone acts in secret, the visible story has causes the reader can feel but not see. If you plan the secret moves alongside the visible beats, the reveal holds up when readers look back. If you invent them after the reveal, readers can tell.

**When to use it.** When your book has an antagonist, ally or secret keeper acting out of sight. Mysteries, thrillers and comedies of misdirection need it most.

**Where it is.** Two places show the same moves:

- **Story Brain, Secret operations**: each move is a record. The list shows whose move it is first, then *When (beat or rough point)*.
- **Narrative Zoom, Who knows what**: under **Secret operations (author only)**, the moves are grouped by character in a table: *When*, *The hidden move*, *What the others see*, *Who takes the blame*.

**A hidden move's record.** Under its name is **Whose move is this?**, a dropdown of your characters (*Not chosen yet* until you pick one). Its pages are **1 The hidden move** and **2 Notes and connections**. Page 1 has one group, **The move**:

- **When (beat or rough point)**: such as *Catalyst* or *about 10%*.
- **The hidden move**: what the character actually does.
- **What the others see, and how they misread it**: what is visible, and the wrong conclusion people draw.
- **Who takes the blame**: who gets blamed until the truth comes out.

**Logging a hidden move, step by step:**

1. Open **Narrative Zoom** and the **Who knows what** view.
2. At the bottom, find **Log a hidden move**.
3. In **Whose?**, choose the character who makes the move.
4. Type what they secretly do in the box.
5. Click **Add**. The move is filed under that character, in both places.
6. Open it in the Story Brain to fill in when, what the others see, and who takes the blame.

You can also add one from the Story Brain: type a name in the box at the bottom of the Secret operations list and click **Add hidden move**. A move made there belongs to no one until you choose its owner in **Whose move is this?** on its record. Until then it shows as *Unassigned* in Narrative Zoom.

**Example.** Cyrus Hale's hidden moves: he cuts the empty boat's mooring line and tows it toward Gull Point (*When*: before the Catalyst; *What the others see*: a boat lost in the fog; *Who takes the blame*: no one); he files the harbor log late (*What the others see*: an ordinary delay). At the reveal, every one of those small, ordinary moments makes sense.

### The Story world record: the heart of the book

**What it is.** The **Story world** record holds the decisions that cover the whole book, rather than one person or place: the book in one sentence and one paragraph, what it means, when it happens and how long it lasts, and how it is told. There is only one per book.

**Why it matters.** Every scene leans on these. Viewpoint and tense shape every sentence. The one sentence keeps the book pointed at one meaning. When these are vague, chapters drift, and the ending feels like it belongs to a different book.

**When to use it.** Fill in what you can at the very start, even as guesses in pencil. Come back when a scene feels aimless, or when someone asks what your book is about and you want to answer in one breath.

**The common mistake** is treating it as paperwork to finish once. These answers change as you write. Keep older versions in pencil and decide one only when you're sure.

#### Finding the Story world

- In the left list, click **Story world**. Because a book has only one, it opens the record straight away.
- From **Novel setup** (open **Settings**, then **Open novel setup**), click **Open the story world** under **The heart of the book**.
- In a brand-new novel, **Story world** may not be in the main list yet. It is made the first time you write the book's one sentence (when you start the novel, or in Novel setup) or a guess at the ending (with **Plant the seed**). Until then it waits under **Show every kind**. Start it from Novel setup rather than with the add box, so the book has exactly one.

Its pages are **The heart of the book**, **What it means** (only with *The Moral Premise (Williams)* or *McKee (Story)* on), **Time, viewpoint and knowledge** and **Notes and connections**.

#### Story world page: The heart of the book

The group **The heart of the book** *(Ingermanson, the Snowflake)* has three fields. The first two are the first steps of the Snowflake Method.

- **The one sentence.** The whole book in about twenty-five words. Ingermanson starts here. Blake Snyder (*Save the Cat*) adds that a strong one-line pitch, a **logline**, carries **irony**: a built-in contradiction that makes a stranger want to hear more. *"A lighthouse keeper who has guided a thousand strangers home can't let one person in"* has irony. *"A lighthouse keeper solves a mystery"* does not.
- **The one paragraph.** Five sentences: the setup, three disasters, and the ending. A **disaster** here is a turning point that makes things much worse and forces a new choice.
- **How it might end.** Your best current guess. Endings found early give the middle somewhere to go.

**Example.** The one paragraph, in pencil: Marisol keeps the Gull Point light alone (setup); an empty boat washes onto her rocks, and the man found near it is dead (disaster one); the harbor board votes to automate the light (disaster two); Marisol learns Cyrus sent the boat, and why (disaster three); she rows out into the storm to bring Ana back, and lets her stay (ending).

**The Story world and Novel setup.** **The heart of the book** appears in two places: on the Story world record, and at the top of Novel setup. They are the same place. Novel setup says so: *The one sentence and the one paragraph live in the story world. Change them here or there; it is the same place.* Type the one sentence in Novel setup, and it appears here as a fact in pencil. If the one sentence here is already Decided, a new wording typed in Novel setup stays Decided, and the earlier wording goes to the field's earlier versions. The one sentence you type when you start a novel in the Library becomes this same sentence.

#### Story world page: What it means

This page appears when you turn on one of its two methods in Novel setup. Each puts what the book *means* into one sentence.

**Moral premise** *(Williams)*, from Stanley D. Williams's book *The Moral Premise*, appears with *The Moral Premise (Williams)*. He argues that a story that works argues one truth, which can be written as an equation:

> [A vice] leads to [defeat], but [a virtue] leads to [success].

The group has three fields: **The vice**, **The virtue**, and **The moral premise, as one sentence**. Fill in the vice and virtue first; they are usually opposites. Then write the sentence.

- **Why it helps:** every subplot can argue the same truth from a different side. The antagonist lives the vice. The hero starts out living it too, and learns the virtue.
- **The common mistake:** choosing a vice and virtue that aren't opposites ("fear" and "honesty"), so the book argues two things.
- **Example:** "Shutting people out leads to a life kept safe and empty, but letting them in leads to a home." Cyrus shuts the truth in and loses his son; Marisol lets Ana in and gains a life.

**Controlling idea** *(McKee)*, from Robert McKee's *Story*, appears with *McKee (Story)*. It is the meaning of the story in one sentence, built from two parts: the **value** at the end and the **cause** that brought it there.

- **Why it helps:** it is a test for every scene. Does this scene move the value, and is it moved by that cause?
- **The common mistake:** writing a topic ("this book is about grief") instead of value plus cause.
- **Example:** "Trust returns when someone chooses to stay." Value: trust. Cause: someone staying.

These two can sit together. The moral premise is the argument; the controlling idea is the verdict.

#### Story world page: Time, viewpoint and knowledge

**Time** anchors the calendar so dates, seasons and ages stay consistent: *Era or year*, *Season*, *The atmosphere now* (the mood of the story's world when the book opens), *How long the story lasts*, and *Name of your story clock*.

**What a story clock is.** Many books count time in their own way: "day 3 of the fog," "week 2 at sea." Naming your clock here keeps you using one count everywhere. Each scene card has a field called *Day in the story* where you write the number on that clock. The name is for you; the program does not calculate with it.

**Viewpoint** decides how every sentence is told:

- **Viewpoint approach**: your overall plan, such as "close third, Marisol only, with three chapters from Ana."
- **Viewpoint rule**: when the viewpoint may change, in plain words, such as "One viewpoint per scene" or "One viewpoint per chapter."
- **Default narrative person**: first ("I"), second ("you") or third ("she," "he," "they").
- **Default tense**: past ("she ran") or present ("she runs").

**How the rest of the program uses these.** The Viewpoint view in Narrative Zoom shows your default person and tense on every scene that doesn't set its own. The Story review reads your viewpoint rule: if your words say *scene*, it asks about any scene that lists more than one viewpoint; if they say *chapter*, it asks about any chapter whose scenes have different viewpoints. So write the rule with the word *scene* or *chapter* in it if you want it checked. That catches **head-hopping**: drifting from one character's thoughts into another's in the middle of a scene.

**Knowledge** has one field: **Does this book hide a truth from the reader?**, with **Yes** and **No** buttons under the writing box. Say yes if the book keeps a secret from the reader on purpose, such as a hidden culprit. It records your intent for you and the collaborator; the tools that track the secret (Secret operations and Who knows what) are there whether you answer it or not.

#### Story types and their ingredients

If you choose one or more of Blake Snyder's ten story types in Novel setup (such as *Whydunit* or *Buddy Love*), each one adds a group to the Story world named for it, with its three ingredients as fields. *Whydunit* asks for *A detective*, *A secret* and *A dark turn*. These groups sit on the last page, **Notes and connections**, along with any groups that came in from your planning documents.

### Note records from your planning documents

**What it is for.** Notes that came in from your planning documents: findings, conflicts and reminders, kept word for word. The Notes list says so at the top. A note has **1 The note** (with *Description*) and **2 Notes and connections**. Search finds every word.

### Worldbuilding kinds of records

These appear in worldbuilding books (see [The kinds your book uses](#the-kinds-your-book-uses)). Each has a first page named for it and **Notes and connections**.

| Kind | First page | What it holds |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Kinds of beings** | The kind of being | What they are, where they come from, body and senses, lifespan, abilities, limits; how they see humans, how humans see them, how they live among their own. Connect a character with **is a**. |
| **Magic and powers** | The magic or power | Where it comes from, who can use it, what it costs, what it can never do, what misuse brings, what it looks like, who knows it exists. |
| **Cultures and peoples** | The culture | Customs, what they value, taboos, food, dress, celebrations, how they treat outsiders, how they speak. |
| **Factions and institutions** | The faction | What they want, who leads them, what they control, who holds power over whom, their methods, what they hide. |
| **Beliefs and faiths** | The belief | Gods or powers, what believers hold true, rituals, who speaks for it, heresies, how it shapes daily life. |

### Kinds you made yourself

A record of a kind you made has one page only, so it shows no page tabs: its Notes, Connections, **My fields** and **Add your own field**. Give the kind its fields with **Every** followed by its name; see [Add your own kind](#add-your-own-kind).

## Questions and ideas: your open questions and possibilities

Click **Questions and ideas** at the top of the left list. This screen holds three kinds of things that are not facts yet, and each has its own numbered page, with the same page tabs a record has:

| Page tab | The page's heading | What the number in the tab counts |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Questions (N)** | **Questions I have** | Questions that are **Open** |
| **Ideas (N)** | **Things and ideas I'm thinking about** | Ideas you are **Considering** |
| **Captured thoughts (N)** | **Captured thoughts** | Thoughts **Not filed yet** |
| **Questions an AI raised (N)** | **Questions an AI raised** | Questions waiting there. This tab appears only when there is at least one. |

So a tab reads, for example, **Questions (4)**: four questions still open. The count in a tab is only the first state on that page; the counts for the other states are on the page itself (see below).

**Turning the pages.** Click a tab to turn to that page, or use the links at the bottom of each page, which name the page before and after. **All pages**, at the end of the row, shows every page one after another. A page number turns ink blue when that page holds anything at all, in any state, so **Questions** can be blue while its tab says **Questions (0)**: every question on it has been answered. While the program stays open, Story Canon remembers which page you were on and opens **Questions and ideas** there next time.

**Why it matters.** "I haven't decided" is different from "I forgot." Writing a question down keeps it from nagging at you, and keeps it from quietly getting answered by accident.

**Do most writers need it?** Yes. It is the lightest part of the Story Brain, and the one most writers use every week.

### Questions I have, in the Story Brain

**What it is.** Questions about your story you haven't answered yet, on the page **Questions**. Under the heading **Questions I have** are three small tabs (separate from the page tabs above), each with a count: **Open**, **Answered**, **No longer relevant**. Click a tab to see the questions in that state. *Nothing here.* means that tab is empty.

Each question shows its words (a link to its own page), the date you asked it (with *, needed to keep writing* if you said so when you captured it, and *, from a conversation* if it came from one), and buttons to move it to the other two states. An open question, for example, has **Answered** and **No longer relevant**.

**Marking a question answered, step by step:**

1. Click **Answered** under the question. A box called **The answer** opens.
2. Type the answer if you like. It is optional, and it is kept on the question as decided.
3. Click **Mark answered**. The question moves to the **Answered** tab.

Click **Cancel** to close the box without changing anything.

**Where questions come from.** Most arrive through Capture: choose **A question I have** in the Capture box, and the question lands here. (How Capture works, including the extra choices it offers while you write, is taught in [Writing Room: Capture while writing](#writing-room-capture-while-writing).) A captured thought can also become a question (see [Captured thoughts](#captured-thoughts-in-the-story-brain)). Questions brought in from planning documents keep your own wording, shorthand and all.

### A question's own page

Click a question to open its record. It looks like any record, with two pages, **1 The question** and **2 Notes and connections**, and one addition at the top.

**This question is**, under the name, with three buttons: **Open**, **Answered** and **No longer relevant**. The one that is true now is shown filled. Click another to change it. A note beside them says: *Write the answer under Answer below. Marking it Decided moves the question to Answered.*

**Thinking it through**, the question's group of fields:

- **Possible answers**: several can sit side by side in pencil while you think.
- **Assumptions behind it**: what you are taking for granted that might not be true. Often the real answer is hiding here.
- **Answer**: the answer, once you know it. When you decide an answer, the question moves to **Answered** by itself.

**Why "Still open" may sit in the Answer field.** Questions brought in from planning sometimes arrive with their Answer marked *Still open: nothing written yet* and a **Write it** button. That means "no answer written yet." Click **Write it** when you have one.

**Example.** Marisol's writer asks, "Why did the boat wash up on Marisol's rocks and not in the harbor?" Under *Possible answers* she writes "The current" and "Someone wanted her to find it." Under *Assumptions behind it* she writes "That it drifted." That assumption turns out to be wrong, and the answer is in it: Cyrus towed it. She writes that under *Answer*, decides it, and the question moves to Answered.

### Things and ideas I'm thinking about

**What it is.** Possibilities you are turning over but have not committed to: a twist, a setting, a scene. They are on the page **Ideas**. Under its heading, small tabs with counts: **Considering**, **Adopted**, **Set aside**. Each idea has buttons to move it between them, and its own record with **1 The idea** (*What it might change* and *Where it might fit*, both lists) and **2 Notes and connections**.

**When to use it.** When an idea is bigger than one field. "What if Eli is alive?" touches ten records. Keep it here until you choose.

**The common mistake** is writing a big idea into the records as if it were decided, then forgetting it was only an idea. Keep it here, Considering, until you adopt it.

### Captured thoughts in the Story Brain

**What it is.** Everything you caught with Capture as **Just a thought** lands here, on the page **Captured thoughts** of **Questions and ideas**, exactly as you typed it. Under its heading, small tabs with counts: **Not filed yet** and **Filed**.

**What you can do with one.** Each thought has buttons to move it between the two tabs, plus **Make it a question** (it moves to Questions I have, as Open) and **Make it an idea** (it moves to Things and ideas I'm thinking about, as Considering). Click a thought's words to open its record, where you can connect it to anything.

**Do you have to sort them?** No. A thought you never sort is still kept, and search still finds it. Sort them when you have ten quiet minutes.

**Example.** On a walk, Marisol's writer captures "the dead man's hands are soft, not a fisherman's." Back at her desk she clicks **Make it a question**, and it becomes "Whose hands are those, really?"

### Questions an AI raised

**What it is.** A fourth page, **Questions an AI raised**, appears as the last page tab (with its count, such as **Questions an AI raised (2)**) when there is at least one. When there are none, the tab is not there at all. It holds questions that an AI asked rather than you: questions you marked in a conversation from a message the collaborator wrote, or from an AI's message in a chat you brought in (marking passages in a conversation is taught in [Conversations and the collaborator](#conversations-and-the-collaborator)). They wait here, apart from yours, under the line *From the collaborator, or from a chat you brought in. These are not yours until you adopt them.*

**Why it matters.** Questions I have is your own list. A question an AI asked might be a good one, but it should not be filed under your name until you say so.

**What you can do with one.**

- **Make it one of my questions** moves it into **Questions I have**, as Open.
- **Not relevant** puts it away. It leaves this page and is kept, never deleted.

**Not the same as a suggested question.** When the collaborator offers a question of its own during a conversation, it arrives as a suggestion instead: it waits under **Suggestions waiting**, reading *New question:* and the question, with a single **Accept** button (see [Suggestions from the collaborator](#suggestions-from-the-collaborator)). Accepting it makes it one of your questions straight away, in Questions I have.

## Suggestions waiting: ideas you have not taken yet

Click **Suggestions waiting** at the top of the left list.

**What a suggestion is.** An idea someone other than you offered: the collaborator, or a chat or document you brought in. Nothing here is part of your story until you accept it. The page says so at the top.

**Why it matters.** Saying "sure" to an AI in a chat does not settle anything. This page is where you decide on purpose, one idea at a time.

**When to use it.** After you bring in a chat or a planning document, and whenever the count in the left list grows. You don't have to clear it before you write.

The screen has two numbered pages, with page tabs like a record's, and **All pages** at the end of the row to see both at once:

| Page tab | What is on it |
| --- | --- |
| **From the collaborator (N)** | Ideas the collaborator offered in a conversation |
| **From your planning documents (N)** | Ideas that came in marked as suggestions in a document or chat you brought in |

The number in each tab counts what is waiting on that page, and the page number turns ink blue when anything is. The link at the bottom of each page turns to the other one. While the program stays open, Story Canon remembers which page you were on.

### Suggestions from the collaborator

On the page **From the collaborator**: ideas the collaborator offered during a conversation. A suggestion can be a fact for a field, a new record (it reads *New* followed by the kind and name), or a connection (it reads *Connect:* followed by the kind of connection). Each one shows, on a slip:

- the idea itself;
- under it, in small type: *Suggestion, for* the field it is meant for, the collaborator's reason if it gave one, and *(you said not yet)* if you put it off before.

The buttons are the same as on a record (see [Suggestions waiting for you on a record](#suggestions-waiting-for-you-on-a-record)):

- **Accept as Decided** and **Accept as Developing**, for a fact.
- A single **Accept**, for anything that is not a fact: a new record (including a question the collaborator raised, which reads *New question:*) or a connection. An accepted question goes straight into **Questions I have**; an accepted connection is marked **inferred**.
- **Not yet** keeps it waiting, marked *(you said not yet)*. Once you have said not yet, this button no longer shows.
- **No** turns it down. It leaves this page and does not come back. Nothing is erased: the conversation still holds the message it came from.

When there are none, the page says *None waiting.* That does not mean there are no suggestions at all. Look at the count in the other page tab, **From your planning documents**.

### Suggestions from your planning documents

On the page **From your planning documents**: ideas that came in marked as suggestions ("Here's a suggestion") in a document or chat you brought in. Each one already sits on its record, in its field, as a slip. This page gathers them all in one place. When there are none, it says *None waiting.*

Each shows the idea, then a line such as *For Marisol Vega, last page, from ChatGPT*: the record (a link; click it to see the slip in place), the field, and who suggested it. The buttons:

- **Accept as Decided**
- **Accept as Developing**
- **No, set it aside** (retires it into that field's set-aside versions, where **Bring back** can restore it)

**The common mistake** is accepting everything at once because it all sounds good. Each idea you accept becomes something you have to stay consistent with. Accept as Developing when you like an idea but haven't committed.

**Example.** After bringing in a brainstorm chat about *The Keeper of Gull Point*, Marisol's writer finds twelve slips. She accepts two as Developing, sets aside five, and leaves the rest for after she drafts the storm chapters.

## Choices ahead: a map of what is still undecided

Click **Choices ahead** in the left list. The page opens with a reminder: *Not a list to finish before you write. Many of these will answer themselves as you draft.*

**What it is.** One map of every place in your book that is not settled, gathered from every record. It is laid out in five numbered pages, with page tabs like a record's. Each tab shows a count in brackets, and its page number turns ink blue when that page has anything on it:

| Page tab | What the page lists |
| --- | --- |
| **Still open (N)** | *Places you marked as not decided yet, gathered by kind of record*: every field marked Still open, with the record's name (a link), the field, and any words you wrote. If you renamed **Still open** in **Settings**, the tab uses your word. When there are none, it says *Nothing marked.* |
| **Possibilities (N)** | *Places with ideas in pencil and no decision yet, gathered by kind of record*, with *(one idea so far)* or *(2 ideas side by side)*. Suggestion slips count here too. |
| **Questions I have (N)** | Your open questions, the same ones as in Questions and ideas, with an **Open Questions and ideas** link. When there are none, it says *None open.* |
| **Kept for you (N)** | Every record (and every scene) you marked Kept for you, with its marker. |
| **Suggestions waiting (N)** | How many suggestions are waiting, from the collaborator and your planning documents together: "3 waiting for you, in Suggestions waiting in the list on the left." |

Click a tab to turn to that page, or use the links at the bottom of each page, which name the page before and after. **All pages**, at the end of the row, shows all five one after another. While the program stays open, Story Canon remembers which page you were on.

**Gathered by kind.** On **Still open** and **Possibilities**, entries are grouped under small headings, one for each kind of record, such as *Characters (3)* or *Scene cards (12)*, with the number of entries under that heading. The kinds come in the order they first turn up. Under a heading, each entry reads as the record's name, then the field: under *Scene cards*, *The empty boat*, *Value at the end*. The kind is in the heading, so it is not repeated after each name.

**When to use it.** On a planning day, to choose what to think about next. Before drafting a section, to see what is undecided there. Never as homework.

**The common mistake** is trying to empty it. A first draft written with a hundred open choices is normal. Many choices are best made on the page.

**Example.** Before drafting the storm chapters, Marisol's writer turns to the page **Possibilities** and, under the heading *event*, sees *The boat comes in*, *How it could happen (2 ideas side by side)*. She decides one, sets the other aside, and starts writing.

## Story Brain questions people ask

**I filled in a field, but I can't find it now.** It is on one of the record's pages. Look for the blue page numbers in the tabs, or click **All pages** to see everything at once.

**A field I filled in is gone after I changed Novel setup.** It isn't gone. Fields that came with a method stay on the page as long as they hold something. If an empty field disappeared, that method is switched off; switch it back on to see its empty fields again.

**Two answers in pencil are both still there after I decided one.** That's on purpose. Deciding one never throws the other away. Click **Set aside** on the one you no longer want.

**I can't decide an Example.** That's on purpose: an example is never canon. Write it again as your own fact, then set the example aside.

**The date in a fact's margin is days before I brought the book in.** For imported facts, the date is when the fact was settled in your planning. **Where did this come from?** shows both dates.

**A count in the left list doesn't match another screen.** See [Why a count in the left list can look wrong](#why-a-count-in-the-left-list-can-look-wrong).

**There's no Scenes kind.** Scenes live in the Writing Room (as prose) and on the Board (as scene cards). Each scene card has a record under **Scene cards**.

**There's no Arcs kind either.** Arcs live under their character, on its **Arc** page. **Choices ahead** shows every arc field still undecided.

**The arc card has no Add buttons.** The card is for reading. Click **Open and change** (or the arc's title) to change an arc.

**My character has Goal and Conflict facts but I never turned on Ingermanson.** Plant the seed puts the lead's want and obstacle there. A field that holds something always shows, even when its method is off.

**My Secret operations list has moves that say nothing about whose they are.** They were added from the Story Brain list. Open each one and choose its owner in **Whose move is this?**.

**Where are the book-wide guardrails?** Under **Guardrails** in the left list (or under **Show every kind** until you have one). See [Guardrails for the whole book](#guardrails-for-the-whole-book).

### Reference: McKee's value ladder

This belongs to the character's **What opposes them** page (see [Character page: What opposes them](#character-page-what-opposes-them)), and to the field **The worst form of their value**.

**What it is.** Robert McKee shows that a value (something characters care about that can swing positive or negative) doesn't just have an opposite. The forces against it come in degrees, and a story that goes the full distance moves through all of them. McKee names four positions (*Story*, pages 319 to 323):

1. **The positive**: the value itself.
2. **The contrary**: somewhat negative, but not fully the opposite. McKee's example: for justice, unfairness, which may break no law but is neither just nor fair.
3. **The contradictory**: the direct opposite. For justice, injustice; laws have been broken.
4. **The negation of the negation**: a compound negative, where life turns worse in kind, not only in degree, at the limit of the dark powers of human nature. For justice, tyranny: might makes right.

**Every example is one possibility, not the answer.** McKee shows a second variation for love: indifference as the contrary, hate as the contradictory, and hatred masquerading as love as the negation of the negation. Those words are his illustrations. Hate is not the only opposite of love, and a mask is not the only shape the fourth position takes: some forces rule openly, and some turn inward, as when a character comes to loathe himself. Your story decides what each position means for its own value.

**Why it matters.** Most stories stop at the contradictory. Strong ones push all the way to the negation of the negation before the end, so the positive side has to rise to its full strength and the climb back means something.

**How it fails.** Two ways. Stopping at the contradictory because the fourth position feels too dark for your book; set its depth for your book's tone instead, since a cozy mystery's darkest point is different from a tragedy's, but both books need one. Or copying McKee's words into your own story instead of finding what your value's positions really are.

**Example.** For Marisol, whose value is trust: trust (she trusts the light, and Cyrus); doubt, the contrary (the empty boat makes her wonder what Cyrus knows); betrayal, the contradictory (she learns he sent it); trusting no one, not even herself, and calling it keeping watch, the negation of the negation. Another writer with the same value might choose suspicion, abandonment and a cruelty that calls itself honesty. That last position is Marisol's **The worst form of their value**.

### Reference: foils, doubles and mirrors

These words describe two characters set side by side. You meet them when you read two Lies as a pair (see [Reading two Lies as a pair](#reading-two-lies-as-a-pair)).

- A **foil** is a character whose contrast makes another's traits visible. Their Lies pull in opposite directions.
- A **double** shows how alike two people are. Their Lies are the same belief in different clothes.
- A **mirror** is looser: two people alike in situation but different in what they choose.

**Why it matters.** None of these is wrong, but you should know which you have. Two characters you meant as foils who turn out to be doubles will feel repetitive on the page. Doubles you chose on purpose can show the reader the road the hero did not take.

**Example.** Marisol and Ana are foils: one fears being left, the other fears staying. Cyrus and Marisol are closer to mirrors: both have carried the night Eli drowned alone for twelve years, and they choose differently about what to do with it.
